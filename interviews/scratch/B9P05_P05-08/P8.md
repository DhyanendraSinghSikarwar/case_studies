**What good looks like:**
Ownership means you carry work to the end and tell people the truth about its state. Prioritisation means you choose what to do first, say what you are not doing, and write it down so others can disagree early. Strong candidates show four habits.
- They score requests on impact and effort (or another simple framework) rather than on who shouted last.
- They write a short weekly update: done, next, at risk, decisions needed.
- They say no or "not yet" with a reason and an alternative.
- They finish: handover, documentation, follow-up after launch, and a note on what they learned.
Ownership is also about the small, boring end of the job: checking that someone actually uses what you shipped.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always).
1. I keep one visible list of my commitments with owners and dates.
2. Each week I decide the top two or three things and say what I am not doing.
3. I can explain my priorities using impact and effort, or a similar framework.
4. I send a short written update every week, even when nothing is on fire.
5. I raise a risk when I first see it, not when it has already slipped.
6. I say "not this quarter, but here is what I can do" instead of a flat no or a silent yes.
7. I follow up after launch to see whether the work is used.
8. I close loops: handover notes, tickets, tidy-up.
9. When I cannot deliver, I tell the person early and offer options.
10. I can tell the story of one thing I carried through to the end, including what went wrong.

10-24: Week 1; 25-37: focus on the written update and no-scripts; 38-50: practise prioritising for others. Personal baseline only.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Write your top three for tomorrow, and one thing you will not do. (5 min) |
| Daily | Before accepting a new request, ask: who needs it, by when, what happens if it waits? (2 min) |
| Weekly | Send a four-line update: done / next / at risk / need from you. (10 min) |
| Weekly | Sort your backlog on a 2x2 of impact and effort; mark quick wins and things to drop or defer. An AI tool can critique your list for missing risks; the choices stay yours. (15 min) |
| Weekly | Follow up on one thing you shipped: is it used, is anything broken? (10 min) |

Weekly update template:
```
Done: [item, one line]
Next: [item, date]
At risk: [item, why, what I will do by DATE]
Need from you: [decision or help, by DATE]
Not doing for now: [item, reason]
```
Saying no with an alternative:
```
I can't take [request] this [period] because [priority X] has the bigger impact on [goal].
What I can do is [smaller option] by [date], or [request] next [period] if [X] is done.
Which of those works for you?
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | See your work | List everything you own or promised. Give each an owner, a date and a one-line outcome. Daily: write your top three and one "not doing". |
| 2 | Impact and effort | Score each item low, medium or high on impact and effort. Pick the order. Practise explaining the order aloud in 60 seconds. Try the Eisenhower matrix (urgent versus important) on the same list and note where they disagree. |
| 3 | Updates and saying no | Send the weekly update twice. Practise the no-with-alternative script on three imagined or real requests. Raise one risk early. |
| 4 | Finish and follow up | Close the loops on one piece of work: handover note, follow-up with users, a short "what I learned" note. Write your best ownership story as STAR from real events. |

**How to show it in interviews:**
Pick one story where you owned something to the end and one where you chose between competing requests. Say what you did not do and why.
- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q14 (five requests, time for two), Q10 (vague or shifting problem), Q31 (initiative to fix a recurring problem), Q42 (mid level, owned a project end to end), Q12 (deadline pressure), Q15 (something you owned failed).
- Book 9 Part 2 (Communication, Data Storytelling and Stakeholder Management): Q11 (turning a vague request into a project), Q33 (six teams, time for two), Q15 (weekly status update), Q35 (follow-up after launch).
- Book 9 Part 3 (Leadership Principles, Negotiation Process and the HR Round): Q1 (ownership beyond your job), Q28 (long-term over quick win).
- Book 9 Part 4 (Soft-Skill Scenarios): S5 (Friday deliverable will slip), S13 (requirements change three times), S30 (manager away, vague ask), S33 (overwrote a shared table).
- Book 6 Part 6 (Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions) has data-science trade-offs with numbers; refer to it instead of repeating them.
- Make it yours: use one real piece of work and one real priority call. Say honestly what was slow or went wrong; do not claim a result you did not deliver.
- AI-era note: ownership stories are checked with details (who agreed, what slipped, what you changed). Generic or AI-written stories fall apart under those follow-ups.

**Common traps:**
- Saying yes to everything and letting everything slip quietly.
- Saying "I prioritise by impact" with no example of a real call.
- Hiding a slip until the deadline.
- Claiming ownership of a team result without saying your own part.
- Overbuilding the framework; a simple sorted list beats a perfect scoring sheet.
- Treating "done" as "shipped" and never checking use.
- Using a framework to dodge a hard conversation instead of having it.

**Measures of progress:**
- You have sent the weekly update for four weeks in a row.
- Your top-three list matches what you actually did at least most weeks.
- You raised a risk earlier than before and the other person had options.
- You have a story you can tell with a named decision and a named thing you dropped.
- Someone confirmed that the thing you shipped was used or that its follow-up was done.
- You can say "not now, here is the alternative" without apologising at length.
