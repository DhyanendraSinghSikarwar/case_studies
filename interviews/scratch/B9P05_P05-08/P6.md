**What good looks like:**
A good speaker in a data interview does five things you can practise: opens with the point, paces for the audience, pauses instead of filling silence, handles interruptions without getting defensive, and cuts to fit the time. Filler words (um, like, you know) are a symptom; the cure is knowing your opening line and being comfortable with a short silence. Nobody expects a polished orator. They expect a clear talk they can follow, and calm answers to questions you did not plan for.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always) on each line.
1. I can state the one message of a talk in a sentence before I build slides.
2. I know my first 30 seconds almost word for word.
3. I have recorded myself and watched or listened back in the last month.
4. I pause at the end of a point instead of saying "so... um".
5. I can cut a 10-minute talk to 3 minutes without losing the message.
6. I check the audience (face, chat, questions) and adjust.
7. When I get a hard question, I pause, restate it, then answer.
8. I have a plan for when a demo or slide fails.
9. I have presented to people outside my team at least once recently.
10. I ask someone for specific feedback after I speak.

10-24: begin at Week 1; 25-37: concentrate on recording and cutting; 38-50: seek harder audiences (meetups, cross-team demos). It is a personal baseline only.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Say your 30-second "what I am working on" aloud once, in one take, as if to a senior person. (3 min) |
| Daily | Take one idea from your day and explain it aloud in 60 seconds with no filler; pause instead. (5 min) |
| Weekly | Record one 3-minute talk (phone audio is enough). Listen at normal speed, note filler words and where you rushed. (15 min) |
| Weekly | Present something real: a standup update, a team demo, a lightning talk at a meetup. (varies) |
| Weekly | Ask one person for two comments: one thing that was clear, one thing to change. Ask an AI tool to critique your outline or your slide order if you like; never to write your talk or give you a script to read. (10 min) |

Opening you can adapt:
```
I want to leave you with one thing today: [the message].
Here is why [one sentence], and here is what I need from you: [decision or feedback].
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline and structure | Day 1: record a 3-minute talk on a recent project and just listen. Days 2-5: write the one-sentence message, the opening, three points, the close. Say it aloud daily. |
| 2 | Pace and pauses | Practise a deliberate pause at the end of each point (count silently to two). Record twice more. Compare filler words and pace across the recordings. |
| 3 | Cutting and questions | Cut your talk to 10, then 5, then 2 minutes. Practise "pause, restate, answer" on five tough questions a day, asking a friend or an AI partner to throw them at you. |
| 4 | Real audience | Present to a real group (team demo, meetup lightning talk, study group). Practise the failure plan: what you say if the demo breaks. Ask for feedback and note one change. |

**How to show it in interviews:**
Presentation rounds, panels and executive Q&A reward a clear opening and calm questions handling. Practise these items.
- Book 9 Part 2 (Communication, Data Storytelling and Stakeholder Management): Q5 (thirty-second update to a leader), Q6 (opening a presentation), Q30 (one slide), Q36 (interruptions and hostile questions), Q40 (video calls), Q42 (whiteboard explanation), Q43 (live demo fails), Q44 (slot cut to ten minutes), Q17 (defending an analysis calmly).
- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q1 (two-minute walk through your background is a speaking test in miniature).
- Book 9 Part 4 (Soft-Skill Scenarios): S1 (mind goes blank), S3 (poor connection), S15 (CXO question you cannot yet answer), S28 (panel of four), S54 (camera off).
- Make it yours: record your actual project talk and pick the one or two moments where you were strongest. Do not memorise a script word for word; interviewers who interrupt will expose a recited answer, and an AI-written script will not help you answer follow-ups.
- Other books: for what executives ask about a technical result, see Book 6 Part 6 (Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions).

**Common traps:**
- Memorising a script and freezing when interrupted.
- Racing to fit everything in; the fix is cutting, not speeding up.
- Filling every pause with "um"; a two-second pause sounds more confident than it feels.
- Reading slides aloud.
- Apologising ("sorry, this is a bit messy") before starting.
- Taking a hard question as an attack; pause, restate, then answer.
- Practising only alone. At least one real audience matters.

**Measures of progress:**
- Your recordings show fewer filler words than your first one (compare your own recordings; no external benchmark needed).
- You can deliver your core message in 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes.
- You have given at least one talk to a real audience and collected specific feedback.
- You can restate a hard question before answering it without being prompted.
- A listener can repeat your one-sentence message afterwards.
- When something went wrong (slide, demo, connection), you had a ready sentence for it.
