**What good looks like:**
Influencing without authority means the other person ends up wanting to do the thing, because you understood what they care about first. Negotiation, in the sense this playbook uses, is the same skill with a decision attached: you work out interests, options and alternatives before you ever speak. A strong candidate shows four habits.
- They ask what the other side needs before they argue for their own view (interests, not positions).
- They prepare: their own goals, the other side's likely goals, the options that serve both, and what they do if there is no agreement (a BATNA-style fallback, the best alternative you have without this deal).
- They talk to people one-to-one before the meeting (pre-wiring), so nobody is surprised in public.
- They stay honest. They never bluff about alternatives they do not have, and they treat a "no" as information.
This playbook is process only. It has no pay figures, because the habit is the same whether the topic is a model launch, a roadmap slot or an offer.

**Self-assessment:**
Score each line 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always), then add up.
1. Before a hard conversation I write down what the other person wants, in their words, not mine.
2. I can name the single outcome I need and two or three things I am flexible on.
3. I can say my fallback if this person says no (what I will do next, not a threat).
4. I ask a question before I make a case.
5. I talk to the key people before a decision meeting, not during it.
6. When someone pushes back, I restate their concern fairly before I respond.
7. I offer at least two options instead of one take-it-or-leave-it plan.
8. I can show evidence for my recommendation in the other person's terms (their metric, their risk).
9. I can disagree, then commit, and mean it.
10. I can tell the difference between persuading and pressuring, and I do not cross the line.

Reading your total: 10-24 means start with Week 1 below; 25-37 means focus on preparation sheets and pre-wiring; 38-50 means practise with harder, higher-stakes mock conversations. The total is a personal baseline, not a benchmark.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Before your next meeting with a disagreement in it, fill the three-line sheet: their interest, my interest, one option for both (5 min). |
| Daily | After the meeting, write one line: what did I learn about what they care about? (2 min) |
| Weekly | One pre-wiring conversation: ask a key person for their view on a proposal a day before the meeting (15 min). |
| Weekly | One mock negotiation with a friend or an AI tool playing the other side. Ask the AI to push back and to critique your draft script. Do not ask it to write your story. (20 min) |
| Weekly | Re-read your notes and mark one place you argued a position when you should have asked an interest question. (10 min) |

Say it like this when you practise a pre-wire:
```
Before Thursday's review I wanted to hear how you see [the proposal].
What would need to be true for you to be comfortable with [the decision]?
Is there anything you would want changed before it goes to the group?
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day, week by week.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Interests, not positions | Each day take one disagreement you saw or had. Write the stated position and the likely interest behind it. Practise two "what matters most to you here?" questions out loud. |
| 2 | Preparation sheet | Build a one-page template: my goal, their goal, shared goals, options, my fallback, evidence in their terms. Fill it for one real or past situation every day for five days. |
| 3 | Pre-wiring and scripts | Write scripts with placeholders for three situations: asking for a decision, asking another team for help, pushing back on a request. Do two real pre-wire conversations. Rehearse the scripts aloud. |
| 4 | Mock and review | Three mock conversations (friend, mentor or AI partner who pushes back). Record one, listen back, and note where you gave in too fast or argued too long. Pick your best real example for interviews and write it as STAR. |

**How to show it in interviews:**
Use a real story where you changed an outcome without owning the decision. Name the other person's interest in one sentence, say what you did before the meeting, and say what happened. Close with what you would do again.
- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q27 (got people to act on your recommendation with no authority), Q9 (disagreed with a stakeholder), Q37 (disagreed with your manager), Q48 (staff level, influenced across teams), Q43 (pushed back on a senior person).
- Book 9 Part 2 (Communication, Data Storytelling and Stakeholder Management): Q12 (saying no), Q34 (getting a decision out of busy leaders).
- Book 9 Part 3 (Leadership Principles, Negotiation Process and the HR Round): Q9 (disagree and commit), Q4 and Q5 (expectations and the range question, no numbers), Q13, Q14, Q31 and Q32 (offer-stage process).
- Book 9 Part 4 (Soft-Skill Scenarios): S17, S34, S35, S36, S49 and S59 (influence without authority), S10 (stakeholder conflict).
- Book 6 Part 6 (Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions) has worked data-science trade-offs; use it rather than repeating them here.
- Make it yours: pick one real example where you changed someone's mind or got a decision. Do not invent a win. If the result was partial, say so; that reads as honest.
- AI-era note: interviewers follow up on details (who said what, what you tried first). A story you actually lived survives those follow-ups; a rehearsed or AI-written one does not.

**Common traps:**
- Opening with your solution before you know their concern.
- Treating the other person as an obstacle, then telling a story where you "won".
- Bluffing about alternatives (other offers, other options) you do not have. Be truthful; it protects trust.
- Surprising people in a group meeting with a proposal that affects them.
- Going straight to one option; offering two or three shows flexibility.
- Using persuasion techniques as tricks. Pressure or manipulation may win a meeting and lose the relationship.
- Giving legal or contract advice or assumptions in negotiations. For anything about terms, ask the recruiter or HR and check official sources; this book gives no legal advice.

**Measures of progress:**
Observable signals, not statistics.
- You fill the preparation sheet before important conversations without being reminded.
- In a recent disagreement you asked at least two interest questions before stating your case.
- Someone you pre-wired said they were not surprised in the meeting.
- You can tell a real influence story in under two minutes with the other person's interest named.
- In mock conversations, your partner says you offered options, not ultimatums.
- You said "I will think about it and come back" at least once instead of agreeing or refusing on the spot.
