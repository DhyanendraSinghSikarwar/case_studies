**What good looks like:**
Good writing at work lets a busy reader act without a meeting. The bottom line comes first (BLUF: bottom line up front), then the evidence, then the detail. A good design doc states the problem, the options considered, the recommendation and what you need decided. A good pull-request description says what changed, why, how it was tested and what a reviewer should look at. A good executive email fits on one screen, has one ask and a date. Written communication in interviews shows up in take-homes, written exercises, and senior rounds where you are asked to summarise or explain a decision in writing.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always).
1. My first sentence of any email or doc states the point or the ask.
2. I write for a named reader and know what they will do after reading.
3. My documents say what decision is needed and by when.
4. I list at least one alternative I considered and why I did not choose it.
5. I state assumptions and uncertainty plainly (what I know, what I do not).
6. My PR descriptions explain why, not just what.
7. I cut a draft by a third before sending.
8. I can summarise a long thread or document in three lines.
9. I check numbers and names in what I send, including text an AI tool drafted.
10. I ask a reader to tell me what they understood and what they would do.

10-24: start at Week 1; 25-37: work on decisions and alternatives; 38-50: practise longer docs and review other people's drafts. Personal baseline only.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Rewrite one real message you sent today with the point in the first line. (5 min) |
| Daily | Summarise something you read (thread, article, PR) in three lines. (5 min) |
| Weekly | Write one full artefact: a one-page design note, a PR description, or an executive email. (20 min) |
| Weekly | Swap with a peer or ask an AI tool to critique for clarity, missing decision, buried ask. Do not paste confidential material into tools your company has not approved; check the company's current policy. Do not let the tool write it for you. (10 min) |
| Weekly | Read one good design doc or RFC from open source or your team and note its structure. (15 min) |

Executive email template:
```
Subject: [Decision needed by DATE] [topic]
Bottom line: I recommend [option] because [one reason].
What I need from you: [approve / choose / answer X] by [DATE].
Evidence: [two or three bullets, plain numbers from your own data].
Risks and what I am unsure about: [one or two lines].
Alternatives considered: [A, B] and why not.
```
Pull-request description template:
```
What: [one sentence]
Why: [the problem or ticket]
How I tested: [commands, checks, data used]
Reviewer focus: [the risky part]
Not in this PR: [follow-ups]
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bottom line first | Take five emails a day (sent or draft) and rewrite the first line as the point. By day 5, do it before sending. |
| 2 | Decisions and alternatives | Write a one-page design note on a real or past problem: problem, options, recommendation, ask. Rewrite it shorter each day for three days. |
| 3 | PRs and executive emails | Rewrite two old PR descriptions and write two executive emails with the templates above. Get feedback from a peer. |
| 4 | Review and timed practice | Write one timed (30 minutes) take-home-style summary of an analysis. Review someone else's draft. Collect your best example for interviews. |

**How to show it in interviews:**
Bring a short writing sample you are allowed to share (nothing confidential), or describe your habits with one concrete example.
- Book 9 Part 2 (Communication, Data Storytelling and Stakeholder Management): Q10 (executive summary), Q15 (weekly status update), Q21 (email sharing a result and asking for a decision), Q22 (documenting assumptions and caveats), Q31 (README or handover note), Q38 (incident report summary), Q41 (Slack escalation), Q45 (summarising a long design doc).
- Book 9 Part 4 (Soft-Skill Scenarios): S6 (standup when nothing is finished), S41 (angry reply to a neutral message), S42 (fourteen-reply thread), S43 (written pre-reads).
- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q39 (bad news to a manager) and Q46 (senior level, setting direction).
- Make it yours: choose one real document, update or email where your writing changed a decision, and describe what you did in a sentence. Do not invent one.
- AI-era note: interviewers may ask you to talk through your doc live. If an AI drafted it and you cannot explain the choices, the live follow-up shows it. Say honestly how you used AI tools (see Book 9 Part 2 Q46) and own the checking.

**Common traps:**
- Burying the ask at the end of a long email.
- Writing a history of what you did instead of what the reader should decide.
- Hiding uncertainty or listing caveats without saying what they change.
- Dense paragraphs with no structure; use headings and short bullets where they help.
- Sending AI-drafted text without checking facts, numbers and tone.
- Pasting confidential or customer data into an unapproved tool. Ask your team or compliance what is allowed; check official company policy.
- Writing only what, never why, in PR descriptions.

**Measures of progress:**
- Readers answer your email with a decision instead of a clarifying question.
- Your PRs need fewer "what is this for?" comments (observe your own review threads).
- Your one-page notes fit on one page and a colleague can state the recommendation after reading.
- You can summarise any thread in three lines.
- You cut at least a third from your last draft before sending.
- A peer or manager can name the ask in your last three emails.
