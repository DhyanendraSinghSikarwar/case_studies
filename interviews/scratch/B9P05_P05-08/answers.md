### P5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 Influencing and negotiation
**Asked at:** Influence-without-authority questions appear in a Yardstick page and a ClimbTheLadder question list; principled-negotiation theory (interests not positions, BATNA) appears in Harvard ombuds and Getting-to-Yes summary results; mixed-quality sources, process only, snippets only, not opened; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** The process of influencing without authority and of principled negotiation (interests, options, alternatives, pre-wiring); no pay figures here; tested in behavioural, hiring-manager and offer-stage conversations.

**What good looks like:**
Influencing without authority means the other person ends up wanting to do the thing, because you understood what they care about first. Negotiation, in the sense this playbook uses, is the same skill with a decision attached: you work out interests, options and alternatives before you ever speak. A strong candidate shows four habits.
- They ask what the other side needs before they argue for their own view (interests, not positions).
- They prepare: their own goals, the other side's likely goals, the options that serve both, and what they do if there is no agreement (a BATNA-style fallback, the best alternative you have without this deal).
- They talk to people one-to-one before the meeting (pre-wiring), so nobody is surprised in public.
- They stay honest. They never bluff about alternatives they do not have, and they treat a "no" as information.
This playbook is process only. It has no pay figures, because the habit is the same whether the topic is a model launch, a roadmap slot or an offer.

**Self-assessment:**
Score each line 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always), then add up.
1. Before a hard conversation I write down what the other person wants, in their words, not mine.
2. I can name the single outcome I need and two or three things I am flexible on.
3. I can say my fallback if this person says no (what I will do next, not a threat).
4. I ask a question before I make a case.
5. I talk to the key people before a decision meeting, not during it.
6. When someone pushes back, I restate their concern fairly before I respond.
7. I offer at least two options instead of one take-it-or-leave-it plan.
8. I can show evidence for my recommendation in the other person's terms (their metric, their risk).
9. I can disagree, then commit, and mean it.
10. I can tell the difference between persuading and pressuring, and I do not cross the line.

Reading your total: 10-24 means start with Week 1 below; 25-37 means focus on preparation sheets and pre-wiring; 38-50 means practise with harder, higher-stakes mock conversations. The total is a personal baseline, not a benchmark.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Before your next meeting with a disagreement in it, fill the three-line sheet: their interest, my interest, one option for both (5 min). |
| Daily | After the meeting, write one line: what did I learn about what they care about? (2 min) |
| Weekly | One pre-wiring conversation: ask a key person for their view on a proposal a day before the meeting (15 min). |
| Weekly | One mock negotiation with a friend or an AI tool playing the other side. Ask the AI to push back and to critique your draft script. Do not ask it to write your story. (20 min) |
| Weekly | Re-read your notes and mark one place you argued a position when you should have asked an interest question. (10 min) |

Say it like this when you practise a pre-wire:
```
Before Thursday's review I wanted to hear how you see [the proposal].
What would need to be true for you to be comfortable with [the decision]?
Is there anything you would want changed before it goes to the group?
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day, week by week.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Interests, not positions | Each day take one disagreement you saw or had. Write the stated position and the likely interest behind it. Practise two "what matters most to you here?" questions out loud. |
| 2 | Preparation sheet | Build a one-page template: my goal, their goal, shared goals, options, my fallback, evidence in their terms. Fill it for one real or past situation every day for five days. |
| 3 | Pre-wiring and scripts | Write scripts with placeholders for three situations: asking for a decision, asking another team for help, pushing back on a request. Do two real pre-wire conversations. Rehearse the scripts aloud. |
| 4 | Mock and review | Three mock conversations (friend, mentor or AI partner who pushes back). Record one, listen back, and note where you gave in too fast or argued too long. Pick your best real example for interviews and write it as STAR. |

They are snippet-level evidence of the themes, not interview rubrics.

**How to show it in interviews:**
Use a real story where you changed an outcome without owning the decision. Name the other person's interest in one sentence, say what you did before the meeting, and say what happened. Close with what you would do again.
- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q27 (got people to act on your recommendation with no authority), Q9 (disagreed with a stakeholder), Q37 (disagreed with your manager), Q48 (staff level, influenced across teams), Q43 (pushed back on a senior person).

- AI-era note: interviewers follow up on details (who said what, what you tried first). A story you actually lived survives those follow-ups; a rehearsed or AI-written one does not.

**Common traps:**
- Opening with your solution before you know their concern.
- Treating the other person as an obstacle, then telling a story where you "won".
- Bluffing about alternatives (other offers, other options) you do not have. Be truthful; it protects trust.
- Surprising people in a group meeting with a proposal that affects them.
- Going straight to one option; offering two or three shows flexibility.
- Using persuasion techniques as tricks. Pressure or manipulation may win a meeting and lose the relationship.
- Giving legal or contract advice or assumptions in negotiations. For anything about terms, ask the recruiter or HR and check official sources; this book gives no legal advice.

**Measures of progress:**
Observable signals, not statistics.
- You fill the preparation sheet before important conversations without being reminded.
- In a recent disagreement you asked at least two interest questions before stating your case.
- Someone you pre-wired said they were not surprised in the meeting.
- You can tell a real influence story in under two minutes with the other person's interest named.
- In mock conversations, your partner says you offered options, not ultimatums.
- You said "I will think about it and come back" at least once instead of agreeing or refusing on the spot.

### P6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Presentation and public speaking
**Asked at:** Filler-word and public-speaking practice advice is common (Harvard Extension, Storytelling with Data, LinkedIn Learning, Mental Floss, CSUSB tip-sheet results; snippets say to record yourself, slow down, pause instead of filling and ask for feedback); general speaking advice, not data-science interview rubrics, snippets only, not opened; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to build, rehearse and deliver a talk or demo with pace, pauses and fewer fillers (recording, meetups, internal demos); tested in onsite presentation rounds, panels and executive Q&A.

**What good looks like:**
A good speaker in a data interview does five things you can practise: opens with the point, paces for the audience, pauses instead of filling silence, handles interruptions without getting defensive, and cuts to fit the time. Filler words (um, like, you know) are a symptom; the cure is knowing your opening line and being comfortable with a short silence. Nobody expects a polished orator. They expect a clear talk they can follow, and calm answers to questions you did not plan for.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always) on each line.
1. I can state the one message of a talk in a sentence before I build slides.
2. I know my first 30 seconds almost word for word.
3. I have recorded myself and watched or listened back in the last month.
4. I pause at the end of a point instead of saying "so... um".
5. I can cut a 10-minute talk to 3 minutes without losing the message.
6. I check the audience (face, chat, questions) and adjust.
7. When I get a hard question, I pause, restate it, then answer.
8. I have a plan for when a demo or slide fails.
9. I have presented to people outside my team at least once recently.
10. I ask someone for specific feedback after I speak.

10-24: begin at Week 1; 25-37: concentrate on recording and cutting; 38-50: seek harder audiences (meetups, cross-team demos). It is a personal baseline only.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Say your 30-second "what I am working on" aloud once, in one take, as if to a senior person. (3 min) |
| Daily | Take one idea from your day and explain it aloud in 60 seconds with no filler; pause instead. (5 min) |
| Weekly | Record one 3-minute talk (phone audio is enough). Listen at normal speed, note filler words and where you rushed. (15 min) |
| Weekly | Present something real: a standup update, a team demo, a lightning talk at a meetup. (varies) |
| Weekly | Ask one person for two comments: one thing that was clear, one thing to change. Ask an AI tool to critique your outline or your slide order if you like; never to write your talk or give you a script to read. (10 min) |

Opening you can adapt:
```
I want to leave you with one thing today: [the message].
Here is why [one sentence], and here is what I need from you: [decision or feedback].
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline and structure | Day 1: record a 3-minute talk on a recent project and just listen. Days 2-5: write the one-sentence message, the opening, three points, the close. Say it aloud daily. |
| 2 | Pace and pauses | Practise a deliberate pause at the end of each point (count silently to two). Record twice more. Compare filler words and pace across the recordings. |
| 3 | Cutting and questions | Cut your talk to 10, then 5, then 2 minutes. Practise "pause, restate, answer" on five tough questions a day, asking a friend or an AI partner to throw them at you. |
| 4 | Real audience | Present to a real group (team demo, meetup lightning talk, study group). Practise the failure plan: what you say if the demo breaks. Ask for feedback and note one change. |

**Resources:**
Editor knowledge; check titles before use.
- Storytelling with Data, Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (the "big idea" and decluttering exercises help a talk's focus).
- Made to Stick, Chip and Dan Heath (making a message simple and memorable).
- Toastmasters-style speech evaluation (structured feedback after each talk; name only).
- Recorded rehearsal and listening back; meetup lightning talks; internal demo days (exercises, name only).
- Pause, restate, answer (a recovery routine, name only); box breathing (name only).
- Public speaking tips found in search results (Harvard Extension, Storytelling with Data, LinkedIn Learning, Mental Floss) agree on the themes: record yourself, slow down, pause instead of filling, ask for feedback. They are general advice, not data-science interview rubrics.

**How to show it in interviews:**
Presentation rounds, panels and executive Q&A reward a clear opening and calm questions handling. Practise these items.
- Book 9 Part 2 (Communication, Data Storytelling and Stakeholder Management): Q5 (thirty-second update to a leader), Q6 (opening a presentation), Q30 (one slide), Q36 (interruptions and hostile questions), Q40 (video calls), Q42 (whiteboard explanation), Q43 (live demo fails), Q44 (slot cut to ten minutes), Q17 (defending an analysis calmly).
- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q1 (two-minute walk through your background is a speaking test in miniature).
- Book 9 Part 4 (Soft-Skill Scenarios): S1 (mind goes blank), S3 (poor connection), S15 (CXO question you cannot yet answer), S28 (panel of four), S54 (camera off).
- Make it yours: record your actual project talk and pick the one or two moments where you were strongest. Do not memorise a script word for word; interviewers who interrupt will expose a recited answer, and an AI-written script will not help you answer follow-ups.
- Other books: for what executives ask about a technical result, see Book 6 Part 6 (Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions).

**Common traps:**
- Memorising a script and freezing when interrupted.
- Racing to fit everything in; the fix is cutting, not speeding up.
- Filling every pause with "um"; a two-second pause sounds more confident than it feels.
- Reading slides aloud.
- Apologising ("sorry, this is a bit messy") before starting.
- Taking a hard question as an attack; pause, restate, then answer.
- Practising only alone. At least one real audience matters.

**Measures of progress:**
- Your recordings show fewer filler words than your first one (compare your own recordings; no external benchmark needed).
- You can deliver your core message in 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes.
- You have given at least one talk to a real audience and collected specific feedback.
- You can restate a hard question before answering it without being prompted.
- A listener can repeat your one-sentence message afterwards.
- When something went wrong (slide, demo, connection), you had a ready sentence for it.

### P7 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Written communication
**Asked at:** Design-doc and RFC practice is covered by Pragmatic Engineer pages and engineering documentation guides, and BLUF (bottom line up front) by a Substack explainer in the results; no page was found on PR descriptions or executive-email rubrics, so those parts are editor-designed; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to write design docs, pull-request descriptions and executive emails that a busy reader can act on (bottom line first, decision, alternatives); tested in take-homes, written exercises and senior rounds.

**What good looks like:**
Good writing at work lets a busy reader act without a meeting. The bottom line comes first (BLUF: bottom line up front), then the evidence, then the detail. A good design doc states the problem, the options considered, the recommendation and what you need decided. A good pull-request description says what changed, why, how it was tested and what a reviewer should look at. A good executive email fits on one screen, has one ask and a date. Written communication in interviews shows up in take-homes, written exercises, and senior rounds where you are asked to summarise or explain a decision in writing.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always).
1. My first sentence of any email or doc states the point or the ask.
2. I write for a named reader and know what they will do after reading.
3. My documents say what decision is needed and by when.
4. I list at least one alternative I considered and why I did not choose it.
5. I state assumptions and uncertainty plainly (what I know, what I do not).
6. My PR descriptions explain why, not just what.
7. I cut a draft by a third before sending.
8. I can summarise a long thread or document in three lines.
9. I check numbers and names in what I send, including text an AI tool drafted.
10. I ask a reader to tell me what they understood and what they would do.

10-24: start at Week 1; 25-37: work on decisions and alternatives; 38-50: practise longer docs and review other people's drafts. Personal baseline only.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Rewrite one real message you sent today with the point in the first line. (5 min) |
| Daily | Summarise something you read (thread, article, PR) in three lines. (5 min) |
| Weekly | Write one full artefact: a one-page design note, a PR description, or an executive email. (20 min) |
| Weekly | Swap with a peer or ask an AI tool to critique for clarity, missing decision, buried ask. Do not paste confidential material into tools your company has not approved; check the company's current policy. Do not let the tool write it for you. (10 min) |
| Weekly | Read one good design doc or RFC from open source or your team and note its structure. (15 min) |

Executive email template:
```
Subject: [Decision needed by DATE] [topic]
Bottom line: I recommend [option] because [one reason].
What I need from you: [approve / choose / answer X] by [DATE].
Evidence: [two or three bullets, plain numbers from your own data].
Risks and what I am unsure about: [one or two lines].
Alternatives considered: [A, B] and why not.
```
Pull-request description template:
```
What: [one sentence]
Why: [the problem or ticket]
How I tested: [commands, checks, data used]
Reviewer focus: [the risky part]
Not in this PR: [follow-ups]
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bottom line first | Take five emails a day (sent or draft) and rewrite the first line as the point. By day 5, do it before sending. |
| 2 | Decisions and alternatives | Write a one-page design note on a real or past problem: problem, options, recommendation, ask. Rewrite it shorter each day for three days. |
| 3 | PRs and executive emails | Rewrite two old PR descriptions and write two executive emails with the templates above. Get feedback from a peer. |
| 4 | Review and timed practice | Write one timed (30 minutes) take-home-style summary of an analysis. Review someone else's draft. Collect your best example for interviews. |

**Resources:**
Editor knowledge; check titles before use.
- The Pyramid Principle, Barbara Minto (answer-first writing); SCQA (situation, complication, question, answer; name only).
- Design docs and RFCs as practised in engineering teams (name only); the search results for this topic included Pragmatic Engineer articles on RFCs and design docs, and a Substack explainer on BLUF.
- Blameless post-mortem (a written format, name only).
- Exercises, name only: rewriting your own messages shorter, and reading good public design docs.

**How to show it in interviews:**
Bring a short writing sample you are allowed to share (nothing confidential), or describe your habits with one concrete example.
- Book 9 Part 2 (Communication, Data Storytelling and Stakeholder Management): Q10 (executive summary), Q15 (weekly status update), Q21 (email sharing a result and asking for a decision), Q22 (documenting assumptions and caveats), Q31 (README or handover note), Q38 (incident report summary), Q41 (Slack escalation), Q45 (summarising a long design doc).

- Book 9 Part 1 (Behavioural Interviews, Levels and the STAR/CARL Framework): Q39 (bad news to a manager) and Q46 (senior level, setting direction).
- Make it yours: choose one real document, update or email where your writing changed a decision, and describe what you did in a sentence. Do not invent one.
- AI-era note: interviewers may ask you to talk through your doc live. If an AI drafted it and you cannot explain the choices, the live follow-up shows it. Say honestly how you used AI tools (see Book 9 Part 2 Q46) and own the checking.

**Common traps:**
- Burying the ask at the end of a long email.
- Writing a history of what you did instead of what the reader should decide.
- Hiding uncertainty or listing caveats without saying what they change.
- Dense paragraphs with no structure; use headings and short bullets where they help.
- Sending AI-drafted text without checking facts, numbers and tone.
- Pasting confidential or customer data into an unapproved tool. Ask your team or compliance what is allowed; check official company policy.
- Writing only what, never why, in PR descriptions.

**Measures of progress:**
- Readers answer your email with a decision instead of a clarifying question.
- Your PRs need fewer "what is this for?" comments (observe your own review threads).
- Your one-page notes fit on one page and a colleague can state the recommendation after reading.
- You can summarise any thread in three lines.
- You cut at least a third from your last draft before sending.
- A peer or manager can name the ask in your last three emails.

### P8 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Ownership and prioritisation
**Asked at:** Ownership questions for data-scientist roles appear in a Yardstick page, and the impact-effort matrix in tool-vendor and project-management pages (Wrike, 6sigma.us, Zoho results); the playbook design is editor-designed on top of those; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to choose what to do first (impact and effort, written weekly updates) and to carry work to the end; tested in behavioural rounds on ownership, trade-offs and competing requests.

**What good looks like:**
Ownership means you carry work to the end and tell people the truth about its state. Prioritisation means you choose what to do first, say what you are not doing, and write it down so others can disagree early. Strong candidates show four habits.
- They score requests on impact and effort (or another simple framework) rather than on who shouted last.
- They write a short weekly update: done, next, at risk, decisions needed.
- They say no or "not yet" with a reason and an alternative.
- They finish: handover, documentation, follow-up after launch, and a note on what they learned.
Ownership is also about the small, boring end of the job: checking that someone actually uses what you shipped.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always).
1. I keep one visible list of my commitments with owners and dates.
2. Each week I decide the top two or three things and say what I am not doing.
3. I can explain my priorities using impact and effort, or a similar framework.
4. I send a short written update every week, even when nothing is on fire.
5. I raise a risk when I first see it, not when it has already slipped.
6. I say "not this quarter, but here is what I can do" instead of a flat no or a silent yes.
7. I follow up after launch to see whether the work is used.
8. I close loops: handover notes, tickets, tidy-up.
9. When I cannot deliver, I tell the person early and offer options.
10. I can tell the story of one thing I carried through to the end, including what went wrong.

10-24: Week 1; 25-37: focus on the written update and no-scripts; 38-50: practise prioritising for others. Personal baseline only.

**Daily and weekly practice:**
| When | What (time) |
|---|---|
| Daily | Write your top three for tomorrow, and one thing you will not do. (5 min) |
| Daily | Before accepting a new request, ask: who needs it, by when, what happens if it waits? (2 min) |
| Weekly | Send a four-line update: done / next / at risk / need from you. (10 min) |
| Weekly | Sort your backlog on a 2x2 of impact and effort; mark quick wins and things to drop or defer. An AI tool can critique your list for missing risks; the choices stay yours. (15 min) |
| Weekly | Follow up on one thing you shipped: is it used, is anything broken? (10 min) |

Weekly update template:
```
Done: [item, one line]
Next: [item, date]
At risk: [item, why, what I will do by DATE]
Need from you: [decision or help, by DATE]
Not doing for now: [item, reason]
```
Saying no with an alternative:
```
I can't take [request] this [period] because [priority X] has the bigger impact on [goal].
What I can do is [smaller option] by [date], or [request] next [period] if [X] is done.
Which of those works for you?
```

**30-day plan:**
15-30 minutes a day.
| Week | Focus | Daily task |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | See your work | List everything you own or promised. Give each an owner, a date and a one-line outcome. Daily: write your top three and one "not doing". |
| 2 | Impact and effort | Score each item low, medium or high on impact and effort. Pick the order. Practise explaining the order aloud in 60 seconds. Try the Eisenhower matrix (urgent versus important) on the same list and note where they disagree. |
| 3 | Updates and saying no | Send the weekly update twice. Practise the no-with-alternative script on three imagined or real requests. Raise one risk early. |
| 4 | Finish and follow up | Close the loops on one piece of work: handover note, follow-up with users, a short "what I learned" note. Write your best ownership story as STAR from real events. |
