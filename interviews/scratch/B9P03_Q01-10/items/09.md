**Say this first (1 line):** I made my case with evidence, accepted the decision when it went the other way, and then worked to make it succeed.

**Structure:** STAR with two clear beats.
1. Situation: the decision and your disagreement, in one line.
2. Task: why you believed it mattered.
3. Action (a): how you made your case, with evidence, to the right person.
4. Action (b): what you did after the decision, to help it succeed.
5. Result and reflection: what happened, and what you learned about disagreeing well.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative template; replace with your own story)
"My team lead chose [approach A] for [project]. I thought [approach B] would be safer, because [reason]. I wrote a one-page comparison with [evidence], shared it in the planning meeting, and listened to why A was preferred: [their reason, for example a deadline]. They decided on A. I said I disagreed but would support it. I then built the monitoring for A and flagged early risks. A worked well enough, and I was wrong about [one thing]. The thing I would keep is putting my case in writing early, then backing the decision fully once made."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I told them it was wrong; they ignored me, so I did it my way anyway." Or the opposite: "I just went along with it." The first undermines the team; the second shows no view at all.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- You spoke up with evidence, respectfully.
- You genuinely committed afterwards, not sulked or sabotaged.
- Openness to being wrong.
- Fit between the stakes and how hard you pushed.

**Make it yours:**
- Pick a real disagreement where you lost or compromised, not one where you won.
- Write what evidence you brought.
- Describe what you did to support the decision.
- State honestly who was right in the end.
- Do not name or blame the person; describe the decision.

**Follow-ups:**
- *What if it had been unsafe or unethical?* "Then I would escalate. Commit applies to reasonable differences of judgement."
- *Did you ever change your mind?* "Yes, in this case on [point]. I said so."

**Common mistakes:**
- A story with no real disagreement.
- Making the other person look foolish.
- Saying you committed while showing you did not.
- A tiny stake that nobody would disagree about.
