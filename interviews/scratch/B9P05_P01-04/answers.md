### P1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Structured communication
**Asked at:** Pyramid-principle and answer-first guidance is common in consulting and case-interview coaching pages (PrepLounge, MyConsultingOffer, Hacking the Case Interview, a Towards Data Science communication article); data-science rubrics were not found, so the data-science use is an inference from business communication guidance; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to answer first and then support (pyramid principle, SCQA, one-minute summaries, record and review); tested in every round, above all the phone screen, behavioural and case rounds.
**What good looks like:**
Interviewers want to hear your answer in the first ten seconds, then the reasons, then the detail only if they ask. Good looks like this:
- The first sentence answers the question or states the recommendation. No warm-up.
- You give two or three supports, and you say the count ("three reasons").
- Your answer has a shape the listener can follow: they always know where you are.
- You stop. A one-minute answer ends at one minute, with an offer ("happy to go deeper on any of these").
- When you are interrupted, you can jump to the point that matters without losing your thread.

In an AI-era loop this matters more, because live follow-ups test whether the structure is yours. A clear spine lets you handle a surprise follow-up; a memorised script falls apart.

**Self-assessment:**
Score yourself 1 to 5 on each row, then ask a friend to listen to one recorded answer and score you too. Compare the gaps.

| Score | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| 1 | You think aloud and the answer appears at the end, if at all. Listeners ask "so what is your point?" |
| 2 | The answer is in there, but after a long background. You often add "and also" after you thought you had finished. |
| 3 | You usually lead with the answer on prepared questions, but slip on surprise questions or when nervous. |
| 4 | Answer first on almost every question, with a stated count of points and a clean stop. Slips are rare and you recover. |
| 5 | Answer first even when interrupted or challenged; you adapt the length to the listener's signals; colleagues quote your summaries. |

Eight quick questions (yes or no):
1. Can I say my main point of any answer in one sentence before I speak?
2. Do I say how many points I will make?
3. Do I finish inside the time I planned?
4. Do I avoid starting with "so basically" and a history lesson?
5. Can I cut my answer to 30 seconds when asked?
6. Do I stop talking when I have finished?
7. If interrupted at point two, can I resume without restarting?
8. Do I check in ("is that the level of detail you want?") rather than guess?

**Daily and weekly practice:**
Daily (15 minutes):
- Pick one question from the Book 9 Part 1 or Part 2 lists. Write the one-sentence answer first. Then say the full answer aloud with a timer.
- Record it on your phone. Listen once, only for one thing: where did the answer arrive?

Weekly:
- Once: re-record the same question you did on day one and compare.
- Once: explain a real piece of your current work to a friend outside your field in 60 seconds, answer first.
- Once: summarise a meeting, article or email thread in three sentences using SCQA (situation, complication, question, answer; framework name only). Notice how the order changes the impact.

Practice scaffold you can reuse:
```
Answer: [one sentence]
Because: 1) [reason]  2) [reason]  3) [reason]
Example: [one real example for the strongest reason]
Close: [stop; offer to go deeper]
```
AI note: you may paste your draft into an AI tool and ask for critique ("where does the answer first appear, and what could I cut?"). Do not ask it to write the answer; interviewers' follow-ups will find the gaps. If asked how you prepare, say so honestly.

**30-day plan:**
15 to 30 minutes a day. Template to adapt to your schedule.

| Week | Focus | Daily 15-30 minutes | End-of-week check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Notice your habits | Record one 2-minute answer a day. Mark the timestamp where the answer appears. Do not fix anything yet. | Average timestamp of the answer. Pick your worst habit (long background, rambling close, no count). |
| 2 | Answer first | Write the one-sentence answer before every practice. Use the scaffold above. Aim to speak the answer within the first ten seconds. | Re-record week-1 questions; compare the timestamps. |
| 3 | Time boxes and cutting | Practise each answer at 30 seconds, 1 minute and 2 minutes. Practise stopping on the last word. Add SCQA for context questions. | A friend listens to three recordings and says the main point back to you. |
| 4 | Pressure and surprise | Mock interview with a friend or an AI practice partner: random questions, interruptions, "so what is the bottom line?" Practise recovery: pause, restate, answer. | Re-score the self-assessment. Pick the next skill. |

**Resources:**
(Editor knowledge, titles to be checked before you buy; no web pages were opened for this book.)
- Book: The Pyramid Principle, by Barbara Minto (the answer-first idea and grouping supports).
- Framework: SCQA (situation, complication, question, answer). Name only.
- Exercise: record and review (phone voice memo, listen once for one thing).
- Exercise: the "elevator summary" of your own project at 30 seconds, 1 minute and 3 minutes.
- Public guidance: consulting case-interview prep pages (PrepLounge, MyConsultingOffer, Hacking the Case Interview) and a Towards Data Science article on communication for data scientists describe answer-first structure (search snippets only; data-science use is an inference).

**How to show it in interviews:**
It is demonstrated by how you answer, not by announcing it. Open each answer with the point, say "three reasons" and keep to time. For practice material, use these Book 9 items:
- Part 2 Q5 (thirty-second answer to a senior leader) and Q6 (opening a presentation).
- Part 2 Q10 (executive summary: what order, how long).
- Part 1 Q1 (tell me about yourself in two minutes).
- Part 4 S15 (a CXO asks why a metric dropped and you do not yet know), S1 (your mind goes blank halfway through an answer) and S6 (standup update when nothing is finished).
For case-style rounds, see the case and trade-off material in Book 6 (Part 6, "Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions"). Make it yours: use real projects from your own work, never an invented one.

**Common traps:**
- Burying the answer under context because it feels polite.
- Memorising a script word for word: it sounds flat and breaks on follow-ups.
- Saying "three points" and then giving five.
- Over-structuring a simple question ("Yes. Here is why in one line.").
- Using AI to polish the answer until it no longer sounds like you.
- Never listening to your recordings.

**Measures of progress:**
Observable, no benchmarks:
- Your recordings show the answer arriving earlier; track the timestamp each week.
- You finish in the time you planned, more often.
- A listener can repeat your main point after one hearing.
- You can cut any answer to 30 seconds on request.
- In mocks, you recover from interruption without restarting.
- Colleagues or mock interviewers start saying "clear" instead of asking you to repeat the point.

### P2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Data storytelling
**Asked at:** Data storytelling is a named skill in hiring-guide families (Yardstick, TestGorilla, Adaface, Maven Analytics and TechTarget results; snippets mention storytelling questions, take-home storytelling tasks and a skills test covering visual design, narrative and audience); mostly hiring-side question banks, not rubrics; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to turn an analysis into a short narrative for a non-technical audience (audience, one message, evidence, next step; the 5-slide rewrite drill); tested in presentation, take-home and stakeholder rounds.
**What good looks like:**
A good data story has one message, one audience and one next step. You do not walk through everything you did; you lead with what the listener should know or decide, show only the evidence that supports it, and say what you are unsure about. Good looks like:
- A one-sentence "big idea" a busy listener could repeat.
- Each slide or paragraph has a headline that states the finding, not the topic ("Churn is concentrated in new customers", not "Churn analysis").
- A small number of clean visuals; everything else goes to an appendix.
- Honest uncertainty, in plain words.
- A specific ask or next step at the end.
- You can switch from the story to the detail when someone pushes: the story sits on top of real understanding.

AI-era angle: AI tools can draft slides fast, which makes weak stories easy to produce. Interviewers probe the "why this chart, why this number" under live follow-ups. The story must be yours.

**Self-assessment:**
Rate yourself 1 to 5 against a recent analysis, then ask a non-technical friend to read your last summary and tell you the point in one sentence.

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | You show every method and chart in the order you did them. The audience has to find the point. |
| 2 | There is a conclusion, but at the end. Many charts, some without a clear purpose. |
| 3 | Clear headline and recommendation; some clutter; the ask is vague; uncertainty is mentioned but not explained. |
| 4 | One message, finding-style headlines, clean visuals, a clear ask. You adjust it for different audiences. |
| 5 | Others reuse your stories. You can give it in one minute, five minutes or twenty, and handle hostile questions without losing the message. |

Eight questions:
1. Who exactly is the audience, and what do they need to decide?
2. Can I state the big idea in one sentence?
3. Does each headline state a finding?
4. Could I delete half the charts and keep the message?
5. Is the next step specific, with an owner?
6. Do I state how sure I am, and what would change my mind?
7. Have I tested it on someone outside the work?
8. Can I explain any number on the slide if asked?

**Daily and weekly practice:**
Daily (15 minutes):
- Take one chart or result from your own work (or a public dataset). Write: audience, one message, evidence, next step. Four lines.
- Write the headline as a sentence with a verb.

Weekly:
- The 5-slide rewrite drill: take a past analysis and compress it into five slides: (1) the message and the ask, (2) the key evidence, (3) the second piece of evidence or a segment, (4) risks and uncertainty, (5) next steps. Move everything else to an appendix. Do it once a week for a different analysis.
- Declutter one chart: remove gridlines, borders, extra colours, legend if you can label directly; add a headline.
- Explain the story to a non-technical friend and note where their eyes glaze.

Template you can reuse:
```
Audience: [who, what they care about, what they decide]
Big idea: [one sentence: what is true and why it matters]
Evidence: [1] [2] [3]  (one chart each)
How sure: [plain words, what could be wrong]
Ask: [decision, owner, date]
```
AI note: use an AI tool to critique ("what is the main message of this summary, and what is unclear?"), not to write the narrative. If you used AI for charts or drafts, be ready to say what you checked yourself.

**30-day plan:**
15 to 30 minutes a day. Adapt to your schedule.

| Week | Focus | Daily | End-of-week check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Audience and message | One analysis a day: write audience, big idea (one sentence), ask. Read one good chart or article and write what its headline does. | Seven big-idea sentences; pick the best two and test them on a friend. |
| 2 | Headlines and decluttering | Rewrite titles as findings. Declutter one chart a day. Delete one chart from a past deck and see if the message survives. | One before-and-after chart pair you are proud of. |
| 3 | The 5-slide rewrite | Do the 5-slide drill on one past analysis (two sittings). Practise saying it in two minutes aloud. | A friend or mentor reads it cold and states the point. |
| 4 | Delivery and pushback | Rehearse the five-minute version, then the one-minute version. Practise hostile questions ("how do you know?") with a mock partner. Add an uncertainty line. | Re-score; record one version and review. |

**Resources:**
(Editor knowledge; check titles before use. No pages were opened.)
- Book: Storytelling with Data, by Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (the "big idea" exercise and decluttering).
- Book: Made to Stick, by Chip Heath and Dan Heath (making a message memorable).
- Framework: SCQA (situation, complication, question, answer). Name only.
- Exercise: the 5-slide rewrite drill above; declutter drill.
- Public guidance: hiring-guide pages from Yardstick, TestGorilla, Adaface, Maven Analytics and TechTarget mention data-storytelling questions and tasks (search snippets only; not rubrics).

**How to show it in interviews:**
Use the story shape in every answer: audience, point, evidence, next step. Practise with:
- Part 2 Q9 (structuring a churn story for leadership), Q10 (executive summary), Q30 (one slide for executives) and Q18 (choosing a chart).
- Part 2 Q13 (presenting a no-effect result) and Q7 (A/B result for a shipping decision).
- Part 4 S15 (a CXO asks why a metric dropped) for live pressure.
For take-home tasks, put the answer and ask on the first page, evidence after, method in an appendix. For the technical depth underneath (metrics, experiments), see Book 1 and Book 6 by part title; verify the exact part before citing. Make it yours: use a real project, never an invented result.

**Common traps:**
- "The journey": presenting in the order you worked.
- Many charts, no message.
- Hiding uncertainty, or drowning the message in caveats.
- A chart title that names a topic rather than a finding.
- A closing slide saying "Questions?" instead of an ask.
- AI-written narratives you cannot defend line by line.
- Telling a causal story from a correlation.

**Measures of progress:**
- Your headlines state findings.
- A non-technical reader states your point in one sentence after reading your summary.
- You can present the same analysis at one, five and fifteen minutes.
- You cut slides without loss, and the appendix grows instead.
- Stakeholders reply with decisions rather than clarification questions.
- You get fewer "what am I looking at?" questions.

### P3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Stakeholder management
**Asked at:** Stakeholder mapping (power-interest grid) is covered by general project-management pages (Umbrex, Infomineo, 6sigma.us, Rosemet, Fibery results); no page tied it to data-science interview rubrics, so the interview use is editor-designed; stakeholder conflict as an interview theme appears in Towards Data Science and KDnuggets results seen for Part 4; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to map stakeholders, set expectations and keep them informed with templates and a cadence; tested in behavioural, hiring-manager and cross-functional rounds.
**What good looks like:**
Good stakeholder management is boring and predictable: people know what you are doing, when they will hear from you and what you need from them. Good looks like:
- You know who is affected, who decides, who can block, and who is only interested.
- You agree on the question, the deliverable and the date before you start.
- Nobody is surprised: bad news travels early, with options.
- You keep a regular rhythm of short updates, matched to each person's need.
- When requests conflict, you surface the trade-off and let the owner decide, rather than quietly absorbing it.
- You can disagree without damaging the relationship.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 to 5 against your current project:

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | You work in silence and share at the end. Stakeholders are surprised often. |
| 2 | You update when asked. You know the main stakeholder but not the others. |
| 3 | You have a list of stakeholders and share regular updates, but expectations are loosely agreed. |
| 4 | Mapped stakeholders, written scope, a cadence, early bad news with options. Few surprises. |
| 5 | Others come to you for how to handle a tricky stakeholder. You prevent conflicts by pre-wiring and by clear decision rights. |

Eight questions:
1. Can I list everyone affected by my current project?
2. Do I know who the decision owner is?
3. Is the deliverable and date written down and agreed?
4. Does each stakeholder get the updates they need, no more?
5. Do I share bad news early, with options?
6. Do I say no to requests with a reason and an alternative?
7. Do I know what each person is worried about?
8. Do I ask for feedback on how I communicate?

**Daily and weekly practice:**
Daily (15 minutes):
- Pick one stakeholder. Write: what they care about, what they worry about, what they need from you this week.
- Send one short, useful message (a decision needed, a risk, a result). Make it easy to act on.

Weekly:
- Map stakeholders on a power-interest grid (high or low power, high or low interest; framework name only). Decide for each: keep close, keep satisfied, keep informed, monitor.
- Write a RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) for one project. Share it for correction.
- Send a weekly status note: done, next, risks, decisions needed.
- Pre-wire one decision: speak to the key person before the meeting.

Templates:
```
Weekly update ([project], [date])
Done: [1-3 lines]
Next: [1-3 lines]
Risks: [what could slip, my plan]
Need from you: [decision or input, by when]
```
```
Expectation note (before starting)
Question: [what we will answer]
Deliverable: [what you will receive]
Date: [when, and what could move it]
Out of scope: [what this will not cover]
Please confirm or correct by [date].
```
AI note: use AI to critique tone or length of a message; do not let it speak for you in sensitive conversations. Check anything sent in your name.

**30-day plan:**
15 to 30 minutes a day. Adapt to your role; if you are between jobs, use a past or volunteer project.

| Week | Focus | Daily | End-of-week check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Map | List stakeholders for one project; place them on the grid; write needs and worries for one person a day. | A one-page map; ask your manager to correct it. |
| 2 | Agree | Write the expectation note and RACI; share it; collect corrections. Practise saying no with an alternative aloud. | Written scope confirmed by at least one stakeholder. |
| 3 | Cadence | Send a short update each week plus one on-demand message; pre-wire one decision. Practise delivering bad news with options (Part 2 Q14). | Did anyone say "I did not know"? Note what to change. |
| 4 | Conflict and review | Role-play a clash with a friend: two requests, one slot; a stakeholder who pushes back. Ask for feedback on your updates. | Re-score. Write one STAR story from your own real example. |

**Resources:**
(Editor knowledge; check titles before use.)
- Frameworks: power-interest grid; RACI. Names only.
- Book: Crucial Conversations, by Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan and Al Switzler (high-stakes disagreements).
- Exercise: pre-wiring (one-to-one conversations before a decision meeting).
- Public guidance: general project-management pages on stakeholder mapping (Umbrex, Infomineo, 6sigma.us, Rosemet, Fibery; snippets only). None was tied to data-science interviews, so the interview use is editor-designed.

**How to show it in interviews:**
Tell a story where you mapped stakeholders, agreed scope, kept a cadence and handled one conflict. Use your own real example. Practice items:
- Part 1 Q9 (disagreed with a stakeholder), Q11 (worked with engineers and product), Q14 (five requests and time for two), Q28 (sceptical non-technical partner), Q39 (giving bad news).
- Part 2 Q11 (turning a vague request into a question), Q12 (saying no), Q14 (project slips), Q15 (weekly status), Q32 and Q33 (competing priorities), Q34 (decisions that keep being postponed).
- Part 4 S10, S11, S21, S29 and S57 (stakeholder conflict scenarios).
For data-science trade-off scenarios with numbers, see Book 6 Part 6, "Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions", rather than repeating them. For escalation or policy questions, say you would ask your manager, HR or compliance and check official sources.

**Common traps:**
- Treating the loudest stakeholder as the only one.
- Saying yes to everything, then missing everything.
- Updates that are too long, or only when asked.
- Hiding bad news until it is unavoidable.
- Going around someone's manager without telling them.
- Blaming a stakeholder in an interview story.
- Telling a story where you "won" and they "lost" instead of how the group reached a decision.

**Measures of progress:**
- Written scope exists for each project and was confirmed.
- You can name the decision owner of each of your projects.
- Fewer surprises: stakeholders say "I knew that already".
- Requests arrive through an agreed route instead of side messages.
- Updates get replies with decisions, not clarification questions.
- You can give one specific example of a disagreement and how it ended.

### P4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 Business acumen
**Asked at:** Business-case and business-impact questions for data scientists are covered by Towards Data Science and Interview Query guide results and a DataTalks.Club episode and a Dice article (snippets: understand the industry, its metrics and strategy; explain model impact in business terms); practitioner opinion, snippets only, not opened; search snippets only, not opened
**Scope:** How to connect data work to the metrics, costs and strategy of a business (earnings calls, metric trees, business-case drills); tested in business-case, product-sense and senior behavioural rounds.
**What good looks like:**
Business acumen means you understand how the company makes and spends money, who its customers are, what its metrics are and how your work moves them. Good looks like:
- You can explain the business in a few sentences: customers, product, how money comes in, main costs.
- You pick a model or analysis because it moves a business metric, not because it is interesting.
- You can link a model metric (accuracy, recall) to a business outcome (cost, revenue, risk), with assumptions stated.
- You ask "what decision will this change?" before starting.
- You can reason about a trade-off with costs on both sides, and say what you would measure.
- You are honest about what you do not know about the business, and ask.

This is a slow skill (months). Do not fake industry knowledge; show curiosity and method.

**Self-assessment:**
Score 1 to 5:

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | You describe your work only in technical terms. You cannot say how the company earns money. |
| 2 | You know the business model broadly but not the key metrics. Impact is "improved accuracy". |
| 3 | You know the main metrics and can name a business outcome for your work, loosely. |
| 4 | You use a metric tree, state assumptions, and link a model change to a business outcome with a range. |
| 5 | You shape what the team works on from business priorities, challenge a request that would not move the metric, and others ask your view on strategy. |

Eight questions:
1. How does my company (or target company) make money?
2. Who are its main customers, and what do they value?
3. What are three metrics leaders watch, and what moves them?
4. What is the North Star metric of my product, and what feeds it?
5. What decision does my current work change?
6. What does a wrong prediction cost, and who pays?
7. Which assumptions would change my recommendation?
8. What would I stop working on if the business priority changed?

**Daily and weekly practice:**
Daily (15 minutes):
- Read one business source for your target industry (a company's public earnings call transcript or annual summary, an industry newsletter, a product announcement). Write three lines: what the business is trying to do, which metric matters, what a data team could do about it.

Weekly:
- Metric-tree drill: pick a headline metric (revenue, retention, cost per ticket) and break it into drivers two or three levels deep. Mark which ones data work can influence.
- Business-case drill: take a model you built or know and write: decision supported, who uses it, cost of a false positive and false negative, how you would measure value, key assumptions. Use placeholders, not invented figures.
- Ask one colleague outside data: "what keeps you up at night in your area?" and write down the answer.

Template:
```
Decision this supports: [who decides what]
Metric it should move: [metric tree path: input -> driver -> outcome]
Cost of errors: false positive = [what happens]; false negative = [what happens]
How I would measure value: [experiment, before and after, holdout]
Assumptions that could be wrong: [1] [2]
```
AI note: you can ask an AI tool to quiz you with business cases or critique your metric tree, but it can state industry facts wrongly. Check anything factual against the company's own public material. Interviewers probe assumptions, so keep them your own.

**30-day plan:**
15 to 30 minutes a day. Pick one real target company or your current company.

| Week | Focus | Daily | End-of-week check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The business | Read public material on one company: products, customers, how it earns, risks. Three lines a day. | A half-page business summary in your own words; mark what you are unsure of. |
| 2 | Metrics | Build metric trees for three metrics; name the North Star idea; list which data projects would move each branch. | Show one tree to someone in the business or a mentor and correct it. |
| 3 | Link models to outcomes | Business-case template for three models (yours or common ones: churn, fraud, recommendations). Practise saying the cost of each error type. | Say one aloud in two minutes, assumptions included. |
| 4 | Cases and questions | Mock business-case questions with a friend; practise asking clarifying questions about goals before answering. Prepare two questions about the business to ask interviewers. | Re-score; write your own real example of connecting work to a business result. |

**Resources:**
(Editor knowledge; check titles before use.)
- Frameworks: metric tree; North Star metric. Names only.
- Exercise: reading a public company's earnings-call transcript or annual report to learn its stated priorities (editor-designed drill).
- Book: Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments, by Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu (linking experiments to business decisions).
- Public guidance: Towards Data Science articles on business-case questions and on bridging data and business, a DataTalks.Club episode on business acumen and a 2021 Dice article (search snippets only; practitioner opinion, not rubrics).

**How to show it in interviews:**
Start with the business goal, then the metric, then the approach, then the measure. Ask clarifying questions about the goal and the cost of errors before proposing a model. Use your own real project and state your own contribution honestly. Practice items:
- Part 1 Q4 (a project you are proud of: what changed because of it), Q2 (why this role and team), Q50 (defining a strategy and what not to work on).
- Part 2 Q8 (how accurate is your model, how often will it be wrong), Q48 (CFO wants one number), Q35 (following up after launch).
- Part 3 Q6 (why do you want to join our company): research the business first.
- Part 4 S45 (a stakeholder expects a classifier in a month and you have no labels).
For company facts, use the company profiles in Book 7 (by company and part) with their caveats; do not state business claims about a company that you have not verified. For data-science trade-offs, see Book 6 Part 6, "Stakeholder Conflicts and Trade-off Decisions".

**Common traps:**
- Quoting industry buzzwords without understanding the numbers behind them.
- Optimising a model metric nobody in the business cares about.
- Inventing figures to sound impactful: use placeholders in practice and real figures only from your own work.
- Stating facts about a company you have not checked.
- Skipping the cost of a wrong decision.
- Treating the business as "someone else's job".
- Letting AI summarise a company for you and repeating its mistakes.

**Measures of progress:**
- You can describe how your own company earns money in three sentences.
- You can draw a metric tree for your product and place your project on it.
- Your project descriptions name the decision and the metric, not just the model.
- You ask about goals and costs of errors first in case questions.
- Colleagues in the business start asking for your view on priorities.
- You can say what you would stop doing if the business changed direction.
