### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about yourself: give me a two-minute walk through your background in data, ML or GenAI work and what you are doing now.
**Asked at:** General HR-style questions listed on aggregator pages (Glassdoor data science question pages, Prepfully and InterviewPal job-change-reason pages, an edX data-industry sample list); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I will give you a two-minute walk: where I started, the work I am proudest of, and why I am here now.

**Structure:** Use a "present, past, future" arc, not a CV read-out. Framework note: this is a summary, so use the six-line template in the STAR and CARL section above only for the one project you pick; the rest is a timeline.
- Present (20 seconds): your current role, the kind of problems you work on, one concrete scope fact.
- Past (60 seconds): two or three steps that explain how you got here, each with one result you can defend.
- Future (20 seconds): why this role is the logical next step, tied to the job posting.
- Stop on a question the interviewer can pick up, not on "that is all".

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace every detail with your own.*
"I'm a data scientist on a [team type] team, and right now I build and monitor [kind of model] that [business decision it supports]. Before that I spent [period] in [earlier role], where I learned how messy real data is, and I led [one real project] that [measured outcome or plain result]. That project is what pushed me toward [ML in production, experimentation, GenAI evaluation, whichever is true]. In the last year I've taken on [new scope], including mentoring one new joiner. I'm interested in this role because [one specific reason from the posting], and it matches the direction I've been moving in. Happy to go deeper on any of that, especially the [project name] work."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I did my degree in 2019, then joined a company, then another, then I learned Python, SQL, TensorFlow, Spark..." It is a chronological CV recital with a tool list. It has no scope, no result and no link to this role, so the interviewer learns nothing to ask about and the answer runs past three minutes.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Can you organise your thoughts and stay inside the time?
- Is your scope clear (what you owned, for whom)?
- Is there at least one concrete result, stated honestly?
- Does the story point at this role, not a generic one?
- Is it a tone they would want in a colleague: calm, specific, no self-promotion?

**Make it yours:**
- Write your last three roles or projects as one sentence each: problem, your part, outcome.
- Pick the one result you can defend if asked "how do you know?" and lead with it.
- Find one line in the job posting that you can honestly connect to your history.
- Time yourself speaking it aloud, not reading it. Under two minutes.
- Do not paste an AI-written intro; interviewers ask follow-ups on any line, and a polished line you cannot explain shows quickly.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
Present: "I'm a [title] at [company type], working on [problem area]. My main scope is [what you own]."
Past: "Before that, [step 1 and one result]. Then [step 2 and one result], which led me to [direction]."
Future: "That's why [this role] interests me: [specific reason from the posting]."
Close: "I'm happy to go deeper on [project], if useful."
```

**Follow-ups:**
- "Which of those roles taught you most?" Name one, give the specific lesson and where you used it later.
- "Why did you move from X to Y?" Give a forward-looking reason (what you wanted to learn), not a complaint. See Q18 for the why-leave answer.
- "Tell me more about [project]." Switch to STAR for that one project and keep your own decisions separate from the team's.

**Common mistakes:**
- Reading out your CV in date order.
- Listing tools without a problem and a result.
- Going over three minutes and losing the thread.
- Ending with no hook or a vague "I love data".
- Memorising a script word for word and sounding recited; keep three bullet anchors instead.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why do you want this role and this team, and what about the work makes it a good fit for you?
**Asked at:** General HR-style questions listed on aggregator pages (Glassdoor data science question pages, Prepfully and InterviewPal job-change-reason pages, an edX data-industry sample list); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I want this role because of the specific problems the team works on, and because my own experience in [area] lets me contribute from early on.

**Structure:** Three beats, each tied to evidence you gathered before the interview. This is a reasoning answer, not a story, so STAR is only needed if they ask for an example (see the STAR and CARL section above).
- The work: one or two specifics about the team's product, data or problems (from the posting, the company's own pages, a talk or a product you used).
- The match: one real experience of yours that connects directly.
- The growth: what you want to learn here, which shows you thought about the team and not only about the title.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own research and experience.*
"Three things drew me to this team. First, from the posting and [a talk, blog post or product I used], I understand you're working on [specific problem], and that's the kind of problem I enjoy: [why, one sentence]. Second, I've done something close to it. At [my current place] I [real experience], so I'd be useful quickly on [specific part]. Third, I'd like to learn [specific skill or area] from a team that does it at scale. I do have questions about how the team is organised, which I'd like to ask at the end, because I want to be sure it's a good fit on both sides."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "It's a great company with a strong brand, and I've always wanted to work here. The pay and growth look good too." This could be said about any employer. It shows no research, no link to the work and no sign that you understand the role, so it sounds like you are applying everywhere.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Evidence of real research on the team, not only the company.
- A believable match between your experience and the work.
- Motivation that survives a hard first six months (interest in the problem, not only the brand).
- Honesty: no flattery you cannot back up.

**Make it yours:**
- Spend 30 minutes before the interview reading the posting, the team's public output and any product you can try. Write down two specifics.
- Pick one real project of yours that matches. If nothing matches closely, say so and name the adjacent skill.
- Write the one thing you want to learn and why this team can teach it.
- Check the company's Book 7 profile (Book 7 Part 1 covers several companies) for how their rounds work, but do not repeat claims there as your own knowledge of the team.
- Do not use AI to invent enthusiasm. If you cannot give a real reason, that is useful to know.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What do you know about our product?" Share what you actually looked at and one question; say what you do not know.
- "What would worry you about this role?" Name one honest concern (for example, how much ownership you get) and say you plan to ask about it.
- "Why us and not [competitor]?" Compare on the work and the learning, not on brand or pay.

**Common mistakes:**
- Talking only about the company brand.
- Mentioning pay or perks as the main reason.
- Reciting the job description back to the interviewer.
- Saying you want "any data role".
- Over-flattery that sounds scripted.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What are your two biggest strengths and one real weakness as a data or ML practitioner, and what are you doing about the weakness?
**Asked at:** General HR-style questions listed on aggregator pages (Glassdoor data science question pages, Prepfully and InterviewPal job-change-reason pages, an edX data-industry sample list); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** My strengths are [strength 1] and [strength 2], each with an example, and my real weakness is [genuine, fixable weakness], which I am working on in a specific way.

**Structure:** Claim, proof, and for the weakness a trail of action. Use CARL's Learning step from the STAR and CARL section above for the weakness.
- Strength 1: one claim and one real, short example.
- Strength 2: same, ideally a different kind (one technical, one working-with-people).
- Weakness: something real that does not break the core of the job; what it cost you once; what you do about it now; evidence it is improving.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own strengths and weakness.*
"Two strengths. One, I'm careful about problem framing: on [a real project] I spent the first week checking what decision the model would feed, and we changed the target because of that. Two, I explain results plainly: I've presented [type of analysis] to [non-technical audience] and they acted on it. A real weakness is that I tend to polish analyses too long. On [a project] I spent days on a chart nobody had asked for. What I do now is agree a 'good enough' version with the stakeholder up front and send a rough draft early. It's working: my last two deliveries went out on the agreed date, and I ask for feedback sooner."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "My strength is that I work hard and I'm a perfectionist, and my weakness is that I care too much." The strengths have no proof, and the weakness is a disguised compliment. Interviewers hear it constantly and it signals low self-awareness.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Self-awareness: is the weakness real and specific?
- Evidence behind the strengths, not adjectives.
- Whether the weakness is something you are actively fixing, with a visible habit.
- Fit: do the strengths match what this role needs?
- Honesty about limits without damaging the core of the role (not "I can't code").

**Make it yours:**
- Ask two colleagues or a manager what you are known for; compare with your own list.
- For each strength, find a real example you could tell in 20 seconds.
- Choose a real weakness from feedback you have received (see Q13 for the feedback story) and write down the one habit you changed.
- Check the weakness is not a core requirement of the role you are applying for.
- Do not ask an AI tool to pick a safe weakness for you; the follow-up will test whether it is real.

**Follow-ups:**
- "Can you give an example of that weakness costing you?" Have one real, small example ready, plus what you changed.
- "How would your manager describe your weakness?" Give the honest version; if feedback was similar, say so.
- "How do you know it is improving?" Name a signal: shorter turnaround, fewer review comments on that point, or feedback from a peer.

**Common mistakes:**
- A fake weakness such as "I work too hard".
- Choosing a weakness that disqualifies you for the role.
- Giving strengths as adjectives with no example.
- No plan to improve.
- Listing five strengths instead of two with proof.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a project you are proud of. What was the problem, what did you personally do, and what changed because of it?
**Asked at:** Public data science behavioural guides (Analytics Vidhya, DataLemur, Interview Query and Coursera guides list teamwork, conflict, ambiguity, failure and influencing-without-authority prompts); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** One project I am proud of is [project name], where I owned [your part], and the outcome was [real result].

**Structure:** STAR, with the weight on Action. Use the six-line template and timings from the STAR and CARL section above; pick a project where your own choices were visible.
- Situation and Task: the problem, who needed it, why it mattered, and what was specifically yours (25 seconds).
- Action: two or three decisions, including one trade-off or dead end (60 to 70 seconds).
- Result: what changed, measured or described honestly, plus one thing that did not go perfectly (25 seconds).
- Optional: what you would do differently.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own project and numbers.*
"At [my team], support agents were spending too long routing tickets, and I was asked to look at automating it. My part was the whole analysis and the first model. I started by talking to three agents, and found the labels were inconsistent, so I spent the first two weeks cleaning labels with them before modelling. I chose a simple baseline first because we needed something reviewable, and I only moved to a heavier model when the baseline hit a clear ceiling. We released it as suggestions, not automatic routing. Agents accepted the suggestion about [rate] of the time, and handling time dropped by about [measured amount]. What I'd do differently is involve the agents even earlier in the label review."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "We built a recommendation system with transformers and Spark and it improved things a lot. The team was great and it went really well." It says "we" throughout, gives no personal action, no number, and no trade-off. The interviewer cannot tell what you did.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Your individual contribution, separated from the team's.
- Judgement: what you chose and what you rejected.
- Impact on a real decision or metric, with honest measurement.
- Level fit: scope and decisions that match the level you are applying for (see the level table above).
- Reflection, including what did not work.

**Make it yours:**
- List three projects; keep the one where you can say "I decided" most often.
- Write the one-sentence result and how it was measured; if it was not measured, say what you observed instead.
- Add the decision you are least sure about; interviewers value that.
- Prepare a second layer of detail for follow-ups: data size, tools, who disagreed.
- Do not borrow another person's project or have an AI polish it into something you did not do; live follow-ups expose that fast.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What was your exact contribution versus the team's?" List your three decisions; name the others' contributions generously.
- "How did you measure the result?" Describe the comparison and what you would not claim from it.
- "What would you do differently?" One specific change, not "communicate more".

**Common mistakes:**
- Saying "we" for everything.
- Picking a project with no result.
- Spending most of the time on background and tooling.
- Overstating impact; claims you cannot defend are risky under follow-up.
- Choosing a project far below or above the level of the role.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you had to learn a new tool, method or domain quickly to deliver something. How did you go about it?
**Asked at:** Public data science behavioural guides (Analytics Vidhya, DataLemur, Interview Query and Coursera guides list teamwork, conflict, ambiguity, failure and influencing-without-authority prompts); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I learn fast by setting a small real goal, building the smallest working version, and getting an expert to check my understanding early.

**Structure:** CARL suits this question because the learning method is the point (see the CARL column in the STAR and CARL section above).
- Context: what you had to deliver and by when, in one or two sentences.
- Action: how you learned: what you read or tried first, how you narrowed scope, who you asked, how you checked you were right.
- Result: what you delivered and how sound it was.
- Learning: what you now do for any new tool, and one gap you still have.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own example.*
"My team needed [a deliverable] in [timeframe], and it needed [a method or tool I hadn't used]. I did three things. First, I wrote down the smallest version that would be useful and ignored the rest. Second, I rebuilt a known example from the documentation on our own data, so I'd see where my understanding was wrong. Third, I booked 30 minutes with [a colleague who knew it] and showed them my attempt rather than asking an open question. We delivered on time, with one known limitation I flagged in the handover. What I took from it is that I now start with a small example on real data and get a review early, before I've built much on a shaky understanding."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I watched a lot of tutorials and read the docs and picked it up quickly. I'm a fast learner." There is no deadline, no choice, no check on correctness and no result. "Fast learner" is a claim, not evidence.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- A repeatable learning method, not luck.
- Judgement on scope: did you cut to what mattered?
- Willingness to ask for help at the right moment.
- Verification: how you knew you had understood correctly.
- Honesty about remaining gaps.

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a real case where something was new and a date was fixed.
- List what you actually did, in order, and who helped.
- State one thing you got wrong at first and how you found out.
- Add the deliverable and how you checked it.
- Say it plainly if you used AI tools to learn; describe how you verified what they told you (see Q17 for the AI-tools answer), and do not claim understanding you do not have.

**Follow-ups:**
- "How did you know you had it right?" Describe the test, the review or the known example you compared against.
- "What would you do if no expert were available?" Use documentation, small reproducible examples and a written note of assumptions for later review.
- "What are you learning now?" Give a real current example and the next step.

**Common mistakes:**
- Describing courses instead of delivery.
- Claiming mastery after a short time.
- Never mentioning who you asked.
- No check on correctness.
- Picking a story where nothing was at stake.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 At the end of the interview we ask what questions you have for us. Which two or three questions would you ask, and why those?
**Asked at:** General HR-style questions listed on aggregator pages (Glassdoor data science question pages, Prepfully and InterviewPal job-change-reason pages, an edX data-industry sample list); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I would ask about how the team measures success, what the first months look like, and what makes people struggle in this role, because those help me decide and show how I think.

**Structure:** Prepare five, ask two or three, and pick by who you are talking to. Not a STAR answer, so use categories (framework note: STAR and CARL above do not apply here).
- Work: what problems, what data, how decisions get made.
- Success: how this role is judged at 6 and 12 months.
- Team: how the team works with engineering and product, how feedback happens.
- Honest risk: what is hardest about the job.
- Process: what happens next and when.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; adapt to the interviewer and the role.*
"Three questions, if that's okay. First: how would you know, in six months, that someone in this role has done well? I'd like to understand what good looks like here. Second: what does the path from an idea to something live look like for this team, and where does it usually get stuck? Third, and I ask this of everyone: what is the hardest part of this role that the posting doesn't say? I'm asking because I'm choosing as well as being chosen, and I'd rather know the real picture now than discover it later."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "No, I think you covered everything." Or the opposite: "What is the pay? How many holidays? When do I get promoted?" The first signals low interest; the second leads with things better handled with the recruiter later and shows no interest in the work.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Curiosity about the work and the team.
- Whether you prepared (questions that use something specific to the role).
- Judgement about what to ask whom (manager, peer, recruiter).
- Seriousness: you are evaluating the fit too.
- Respect for time: concise questions.

**Make it yours:**
- Write five questions after reading the posting and the team's public output.
- Tailor each to the interviewer: a peer for day-to-day, a manager for success and growth, a recruiter for process and timing.
- Cross out anything answered by the posting or website.
- Add one question that follows up on something said earlier in this interview; that shows you listened.
- Keep pay, notice and visa-type process questions for the recruiter or HR, and check official sources for rules; this book gives no legal advice.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
1. "What would success look like in this role after six months?"
2. "How does a model or analysis go from idea to being used by the business here, and where does it usually get stuck?"
3. "What is the hardest part of this job that people tend not to expect?"
4. "How does the team handle disagreement about what to work on?"
5. "What are the next steps in the process, and is there anything about my background you'd like me to clarify?"
```

**Follow-ups:**
- "Any concerns about your fit?" is not a question to ask nervously, but you can ask it: it invites objections you can answer.
- If the interviewer answers a question you prepared: ask a deeper follow-up instead of repeating.
- If time is short: ask one, the success-in-six-months question.

**Common mistakes:**
- Having no questions.
- Asking about pay or leave first.
- Asking something the website answers.
- Asking everyone the same question and ignoring their role.
- Treating it as a test of flattery instead of real curiosity.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a mistake you made in an analysis or model. How did you find it, what did you do, and what do you do differently now?
**Asked at:** Public data science behavioural guides (Analytics Vidhya, DataLemur, Interview Query and Coursera guides list teamwork, conflict, ambiguity, failure and influencing-without-authority prompts); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I will tell you about a real mistake: how I found it, who I told, how I fixed it, and the check I added so it cannot happen the same way again.

**Structure:** CARL fits best; the Learning step is what is scored (see the CARL column in the STAR and CARL section above). Own it with "I", and keep the technical cause visible.
- Context: what the analysis or model was for, and the mistake in one plain sentence.
- Action: how it was discovered, how fast you told people, how you fixed it, and how you checked the fix.
- Result: the real impact (small or large) and the repair.
- Learning: one specific process or check you now use, ideally one you can show.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own mistake. Pick a real one of modest size.*
"On a [type] analysis I reported [a result], and a week later, while reusing the table for another question, I noticed that a join had duplicated some rows, which inflated the totals. I told my manager the same day and sent a short correction to the two people who had used the number, saying what was wrong, the corrected figure and which decision it touched. Nothing had shipped on it yet. I fixed the join, then added two checks to my template: a row-count check after every join and a reconciliation against a source total. I now run those every time. The mistake was mine, and I'd rather it was caught by a check than by a stakeholder."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I once made a small mistake in a query but it did not matter, and the team fixed it. I learned to double-check my work." There is no ownership, no real consequence, no process change. Or the opposite: blaming bad data or a teammate. Both signal low accountability.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Ownership without excuses.
- Speed and honesty in telling affected people.
- Technical understanding of the cause.
- A concrete preventive change, not "be more careful".
- Calm: you can talk about a failure without defensiveness.

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a real mistake that you found or owned, with modest to moderate consequences. Do not choose a trivial one, and do not invent a dramatic one.
- Write the timeline: when it happened, when you noticed, whom you told, when you fixed it.
- State the exact check or habit you added since.
- Be ready to say what the impact would have been if it had not been caught.
- Do not let an AI tool generate a "perfect" mistake; follow-ups will probe real details.

**Follow-ups:**
- "How did you tell people?" Describe the message: what was wrong, the corrected figure, what it affects. Same day if possible.
- "Could you have caught it earlier?" Yes, then name the earlier check.
- "Has it happened since?" Answer honestly; give the check's track record.

**Common mistakes:**
- Choosing a mistake with no stakes.
- Blaming data, tools or other people.
- Saying "I am a perfectionist" instead of a real error.
- Skipping the fix and prevention.
- Hiding the mistake until someone else found it, then not saying so.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you had a conflict with a teammate. What was it about and how did it end?
**Asked at:** Public data science behavioural guides (Analytics Vidhya, DataLemur, Interview Query and Coursera guides list teamwork, conflict, ambiguity, failure and influencing-without-authority prompts); commonly reported in public guides; behavioural rounds appear in the Book 7 Part 1 profiles (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta; see those profiles for their evidence tags and caveats); search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I will describe a disagreement with a teammate where we both cared about the outcome, how I tried to understand their view first, and what we agreed.

**Structure:** STAR, with the weight on how you handled the person, not on who was right (see the STAR and CARL section above).
- Situation and Task: who, what the disagreement was about (a work issue, not personality), and what was at stake.
- Action: what you did to understand their view, what you proposed, how you moved from opinion to evidence or a decision rule, and whether you escalated.
- Result: what was decided, how the relationship is now, and what the project got.
- Learning: what you do earlier now.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own real example.*
"A teammate and I disagreed on [a technical choice, such as how to split the data for evaluation]. I thought [my view]; she thought [hers], and the discussion was getting repetitive. I asked her to explain what she was worried about, and it turned out she'd seen a leak in an earlier project that I hadn't known about. That changed my view of the risk. We agreed on a quick test, ran both splits on a sample, and looked at the difference together. The results favoured a mix of both, so we used her split for evaluation and mine for faster iteration. We shipped on time, and we now write down the decision rule for splits at the start of a project. We still work together well."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "A teammate was lazy and kept missing deadlines, so I told the manager and he handled it." Or: "I convinced them I was right." The first blames and skips your own part; the second shows no listening. Neither shows how you resolve conflict.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Maturity: you disagree on the issue, not the person.
- Listening: you tried to understand before persuading.
- Use of evidence or a neutral decision rule.
- Knowing when to escalate and doing so respectfully.
- The relationship afterwards.

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a conflict that had real substance, with a result you can describe, not a trivial one.
- Write their position fairly, in the way they would put it.
- List the exact steps you took, in order.
- Include something you got wrong or learned from their side.
- Avoid stories that make a named colleague look bad; talk about the situation.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if they had not agreed?" Say what the decision path was (manager, a test, a documented decision) and that you would commit to it.
- "Did you escalate?" Only after trying directly; say how you framed it neutrally.
- "Would you do anything differently?" Name one earlier step.

**Common mistakes:**
- Painting the other person as the problem.
- A story where you simply "won".
- No concrete actions, only feelings.
- Choosing a conflict with no real stakes.
- Skipping the outcome and the relationship afterwards.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about what the data showed or what to do next, and how you handled it.
**Asked at:** Public data science behavioural guides (an Analytics Vidhya list and a Coursera guide frame stakeholder, explanation and business-decision stories; a KDNuggets piece says the technical reasoning should stay visible inside the story); commonly reported in public guides; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I will describe a time a stakeholder read the data differently from me, how I made sure I understood their reason, and how we agreed on a way forward based on evidence.

**Structure:** STAR, with the technical reasoning kept visible inside Action (one public guide makes this point; see the STAR and CARL section above). For numeric stakeholder trade-off cases, see Book 6 Part 6, for example its question about a sales VP who calls a lead-scoring model useless; this item is the general story version.
- Situation and Task: the stakeholder, the claim each side held, the decision at stake.
- Action: ask what they have seen that you have not; check your own analysis for gaps; show the evidence plainly; offer a test or a decision rule; agree the next step.
- Result: what was decided, what happened.
- Learning: what you do earlier in stakeholder discussions now.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own example.*
"A product manager believed [a feature] had improved retention. My analysis showed the gain came mostly from [a segment that was already high-retention]. Before I presented, I asked him what he'd seen, and he'd noticed a pattern from customer calls that my data didn't capture. So I reran the analysis with that segment split out, and told him honestly that I couldn't rule out a small effect. I showed one chart and said what it did and didn't prove. We agreed to run a short holdout test before expanding. The test showed a smaller effect than he'd hoped but a real one for one group, so we expanded only there. He now asks for the evidence up front."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "He was wrong, so I sent him the data and the numbers proved my point. He eventually accepted it." There is no listening, no check of your own analysis and no shared next step. It frames the stakeholder as an obstacle, which interviewers read as poor collaboration.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Respect for the stakeholder's knowledge.
- Rigour: you re-checked your own analysis.
- Clear, honest communication of uncertainty.
- A path to resolve it by evidence (test, pilot, rule) rather than by rank.
- The relationship afterwards.

**Make it yours:**
- Pick a real disagreement about data or direction, not about personalities.
- Write each side's claim and the evidence behind it.
- State what you checked in your own work before presenting.
- Note what you did not claim, and the next step you agreed.
- Do not invent a stakeholder who "was convinced by my chart"; real outcomes are often partial, and you can say so.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if the stakeholder had outranked you?" Present evidence and the risk, record the decision, and carry it out while stating your concern in writing.
- "How did you handle uncertainty?" Give ranges, say what you do not know, and propose a test.
- "Did you change your own view?" Say honestly if you partly did.

**Common mistakes:**
- Treating it as a win-or-lose debate.
- Presenting data without checking your own method.
- Overclaiming certainty.
- Skipping the agreed next step.
- Leaving out the technical detail that makes the story credible.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you were given a vague or shifting problem. How did you decide what to work on first?
**Asked at:** Public ML engineer question banks (Yardstick, BrightHire and Jobrise pages list model-failure-in-production, data-quality, ambiguity and changed-approach prompts); commonly reported in public guides; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** When a problem was vague, I turned it into a question and a decision, agreed a small first slice with the people asking, and ordered the work by value and by what would teach us the most.

**Structure:** STAR; Action should show your sequence of steps. For turning a vague stakeholder request into something buildable with numbers, see Book 6 Part 6 (for example its question about a director who wants an AI that tells the team which customers are unhappy); this item is the general story version (framework in the STAR and CARL section above).
- Situation and Task: the vague or changing ask, who gave it, the deadline.
- Action: clarifying questions; defining the decision, the metric and the first deliverable; ranking options by value, effort and risk; agreeing what you would not do yet.
- Result: what you delivered first, and how the problem was clarified as you went.
- Learning: how you handle vague asks now.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own example.*
"My manager asked me to 'understand why customers are leaving', with no more detail and changing priorities. I started by asking what decision this would feed, and who would act on it. That narrowed it to the retention team's next campaign. I listed three possible first analyses, ranked them by how much they'd change that decision and how long each took, and picked the one that took four days. I wrote a half-page plan with what I would not do yet, and my manager agreed. The first analysis showed [a clear finding], which sharpened the next questions, so the vague brief became two defined pieces of work. I now start any vague task with a half-page plan."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I just started with the data, explored everything I could find, and eventually found some insights." That is unprioritised exploration with no agreement on what success is. Interviewers hear a risk of wasted weeks, with no decision at the end.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Comfort with ambiguity: you did not freeze or flail.
- Asking the right clarifying questions (decision, audience, deadline).
- A prioritisation logic you can explain.
- Alignment with stakeholders on scope before building.
- Small first delivery that reduces uncertainty.

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a case where the request really was unclear or changed mid-way.
- Write the first three questions you asked, in your own words.
- List the options you considered and why you picked one.
- Note what you postponed and who agreed.
- Be honest if part of it went in the wrong direction; say what you learned.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if the priorities changed again?" Revisit the plan in writing, state the cost of the change, and agree what moves.
- "How did you decide what to do first?" Value to the decision, effort, and how much it reduces uncertainty.
- "What if stakeholders disagreed on the goal?" Escalate to the decision owner with options and trade-offs, not a vote.

**Common mistakes:**
- Starting to model before asking what decision it serves.
- Trying to do everything.
- No written agreement on scope.
- Complaining that the request was bad rather than showing what you did.
- A story with no outcome.
