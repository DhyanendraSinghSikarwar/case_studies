**Say this first (1 line):** I will describe a time a stakeholder read the data differently from me, how I made sure I understood their reason, and how we agreed on a way forward based on evidence.

**Structure:** STAR, with the technical reasoning kept visible inside Action (one public guide makes this point; see the STAR and CARL section above). For numeric stakeholder trade-off cases, see Book 6 Part 6, for example its question about a sales VP who calls a lead-scoring model useless; this item is the general story version.
- Situation and Task: the stakeholder, the claim each side held, the decision at stake.
- Action: ask what they have seen that you have not; check your own analysis for gaps; show the evidence plainly; offer a test or a decision rule; agree the next step.
- Result: what was decided, what happened.
- Learning: what you do earlier in stakeholder discussions now.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):**
*Illustrative; replace with your own example.*
"A product manager believed [a feature] had improved retention. My analysis showed the gain came mostly from [a segment that was already high-retention]. Before I presented, I asked him what he'd seen, and he'd noticed a pattern from customer calls that my data didn't capture. So I reran the analysis with that segment split out, and told him honestly that I couldn't rule out a small effect. I showed one chart and said what it did and didn't prove. We agreed to run a short holdout test before expanding. The test showed a smaller effect than he'd hoped but a real one for one group, so we expanded only there. He now asks for the evidence up front."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "He was wrong, so I sent him the data and the numbers proved my point. He eventually accepted it." There is no listening, no check of your own analysis and no shared next step. It frames the stakeholder as an obstacle, which interviewers read as poor collaboration.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Respect for the stakeholder's knowledge.
- Rigour: you re-checked your own analysis.
- Clear, honest communication of uncertainty.
- A path to resolve it by evidence (test, pilot, rule) rather than by rank.
- The relationship afterwards.

**Make it yours:**
- Pick a real disagreement about data or direction, not about personalities.
- Write each side's claim and the evidence behind it.
- State what you checked in your own work before presenting.
- Note what you did not claim, and the next step you agreed.
- Do not invent a stakeholder who "was convinced by my chart"; real outcomes are often partial, and you can say so.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if the stakeholder had outranked you?" Present evidence and the risk, record the decision, and carry it out while stating your concern in writing.
- "How did you handle uncertainty?" Give ranges, say what you do not know, and propose a test.
- "Did you change your own view?" Say honestly if you partly did.

**Common mistakes:**
- Treating it as a win-or-lose debate.
- Presenting data without checking your own method.
- Overclaiming certainty.
- Skipping the agreed next step.
- Leaving out the technical detail that makes the story credible.
