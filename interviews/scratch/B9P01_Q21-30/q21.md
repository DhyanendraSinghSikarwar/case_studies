**Say this first (1 line):** I do my best work with a manager who sets clear goals and leaves me room on the method, and I have learned to adapt when the style is different by asking for what I need instead of waiting.

**Structure:** Use CARL; the learning is what this question is really about (see the STAR and CARL section above).
- Part one, my best conditions (about 30 seconds): two or three concrete things, such as goal clarity, how often I want check-ins, how I like feedback and how I work with peers. Say what each looks like in practice.
- Part two, one real adaptation (about 75 seconds). Context: a manager or team style that was different from your preference, in one sentence. Action: what you changed and what you asked for. Result: what happened. Learning: what you now do on day one with a new manager.
- Close by saying the style is a preference, not a condition for good work.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (Illustrative; replace with your own.)
"I work best when I know the goal and the deadline, and I get freedom on how to get there. I also like a short written update rather than long meetings.

At one point I joined a team where my manager wanted a daily check-in and reviewed every step. At first it felt like too much. Instead of pulling away, I asked what worry sat behind it. She said the last analysis went out with an error nobody saw. So I offered a short written plan at the start of each task and a review at two fixed points. The daily call dropped to twice a week within a month.

What I learned is that a style that feels tight often has a reason. Now with any new manager I ask in the first week how they like updates and what has gone wrong before. Then I adjust to that."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I like autonomy and I do not like micromanagers." It names no real situation, blames the manager and tells the interviewer you may be hard to manage. A story with no adaptation answers only half the question.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Self-awareness: you know your conditions and can state them without drama.
- Flexibility: you changed your behaviour, not only your opinion of the other person.
- Maturity in how you talk about past managers and teams.
- Fit signal: whether your described style is workable in their team (this is also your chance to check them).

**Make it yours:**
- Which two conditions really help you most? Check them against your last two teams, not an ideal.
- Pick a real case where the style clashed. What did you say or do first?
- What did the other person say their reason was, if you asked?
- What do you now do at the start with a new manager or team?
- If you have not had a clash, use a smaller real case, such as a change in review habits or meeting rhythm.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if your manager will not change?" I follow the agreed way, tell them what I need to do it well and raise it once if it costs quality.
- "What style do you struggle with most?" Name one honestly, and say how you handle it (for example asking for the goal in writing).
- "How would you describe the style you give your own juniors?" Tie it to the same idea: clear goals, room on method.

**Common mistakes:**
- Describing an ideal that the role cannot offer.
- Blaming a former manager.
- Giving a generic adaptation ("I am flexible") with no example.
- Making the style sound rigid, so it reads as a warning.
