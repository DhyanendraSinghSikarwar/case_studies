**Say this first:** On a distributed team I learned that most breakdowns come from silence and delay, so I made decisions and handoffs written and explicit; here is one case.

**Structure:** Use CARL; the change you made is the core (see the STAR and CARL section above). This is process-level; time zones and team size differ, so use your own facts.
- Context: where the team was spread, how the work moved between people, one clear breakdown.
- Action: what you diagnosed (handoffs, unclear owners, meeting times, slow answers), what you changed (written handoffs, shared decision log, overlap hours, async review).
- Result: what improved, measured by waiting time or fewer repeats if honest.
- Learning: what you do now on any distributed team.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (Illustrative; replace with your own.)
"My team was spread across a few time zones, with only about two hours of overlap. We kept losing a day: someone would ask a question at the end of their day and wait for the answer until the next.

I looked at where the waits happened. Most were in model reviews and in unclear data questions. I suggested three changes: a short written handoff at the end of each day with the state and the next step, a shared decision log so nobody re-asked what was settled, and a review request that carried the context in it, not just a link.

I also moved my own calls to the overlap window. Within a month, review waits dropped from about two days to about one by our own tracking.

I learned to make everything readable without me in the room."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "It was hard but we managed with more meetings." More meetings in odd hours is not a fix, names no breakdown and no change, and suggests that you did not diagnose anything. Complaining about colleagues' time zones is also a warning sign.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Awareness of how distance changes work (delay, context, trust).
- Written communication and clear ownership.
- Fairness about whose time is used for meetings.
- A specific change and a result.

**Make it yours:**
- Which real remote or cross-time-zone project do you know best?
- What was the clearest breakdown: wait, misunderstanding, missed handoff?
- What exactly did you change, and who agreed to it?
- How do you know it helped? Use a rough measure only if you really tracked it.
- If your team was only partly remote, say that accurately.

**Follow-ups:**
- "How do you build relationships remotely?" Short one-to-one calls early, and being responsive in writing.
- "What do you put in a handoff note?" State, blockers, next step, who owns what.
- "How do you share meeting times fairly?" Rotate inconvenient slots and record decisions in writing.

**Common mistakes:**
- Saying you have no problems with remote work.
- Solving it with only more meetings.
- Blaming other regions or colleagues.
- No example of a written practice.
