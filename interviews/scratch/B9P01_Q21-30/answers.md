### Q21 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Describe the team and manager style in which you do your best work, and one situation where you adapted to a different style.

**Structure:** Use CARL; the learning is what this question is really about (see the STAR and CARL section above).

- Part two, one real adaptation (about 75 seconds). Context: a manager or team style that was different from your preference, in one sentence. Action: what you changed and what you asked for. Result: what happened. Learning: what you now do on day one with a new manager.
- Close by saying the style is a preference, not a condition for good work.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (Illustrative; replace with your own.)
"I work best when I know the goal and the deadline, and I get freedom on how to get there. I also like a short written update rather than long meetings.

What I learned is that a style that feels tight often has a reason. Now with any new manager I ask in the first week how they like updates and what has gone wrong before. Then I adjust to that."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I like autonomy and I do not like micromanagers." It names no real situation, blames the manager and tells the interviewer you may be hard to manage. A story with no adaptation answers only half the question.

- Self-awareness: you know your conditions and can state them without drama.
- Flexibility: you changed your behaviour, not only your opinion of the other person.
- Maturity in how you talk about past managers and teams.
- Fit signal: whether your described style is workable in their team (this is also your chance to check them).

**Make it yours:**

- Pick a real case where the style clashed. What did you say or do first?
- What did the other person say their reason was, if you asked?
- What do you now do at the start with a new manager or team?
- If you have not had a clash, use a smaller real case, such as a change in review habits or meeting rhythm.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if your manager will not change?" I follow the agreed way, tell them what I need to do it well and raise it once if it costs quality.
- "What style do you struggle with most?" Name one honestly, and say how you handle it (for example asking for the goal in writing).
- "How would you describe the style you give your own juniors?" Tie it to the same idea: clear goals, room on method.

**Common mistakes:**
- Describing an ideal that the role cannot offer.
- Blaming a former manager.
- Giving a generic adaptation ("I am flexible") with no example.
- Making the style sound rigid, so it reads as a warning.

- Doing the work for the person and calling it help.

- Context: what you believed and why it was a reasonable belief.
- Action: what new fact or test showed otherwise, how you checked it was not noise, and how you told people.

- A trivial example (changing a chart colour).

- Attacking the person, not the work.

"We were building a model to prioritise customer outreach. While checking the features, I saw a field that acted as a close stand-in for [a sensitive attribute]. I also saw that error rates were higher for one customer group in the validation data.

First I checked I was right: I measured the error by group and confirmed the gap. Then I wrote it up in a short note with the numbers and two options, to remove the feature or keep it with extra review, and I took it to my manager, then to the team that handles privacy and data use in our company.

They asked us to drop the field and add a group-level check to the release steps. The gap narrowed but did not disappear, and I said that in the release note.

I now check data fields and group errors before modelling, not after."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I noticed a problem but it was not my area, so I mentioned it to someone." There is no follow-through. At the other extreme, "I refused to continue" without a check or a proposal can sound like drama. Claiming to know what the rules require is also unsafe.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Whether you notice such issues at all.
- Verification before escalation.
- The route you used: the right people, in the right order.
- Constructive options, and honesty about what stayed unresolved.

**Make it yours:**
- Which real concern did you see (privacy, consent, bias, misuse)? If none, use a smaller real case and say how small it was; do not invent one.
- How did you check it was real?
- Whom did you tell, and what did you ask them?
- What was decided, and what did you do if you disagreed?
- What do you check earlier now?
- Ask your recruiter or HR how the company handles such reviews, and check official sources for any rules; this book gives no legal advice.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What if your manager said to ignore it?" I would put the concern and the evidence in writing and ask who owns the decision, then use the company's own channel for such concerns.
- "How do you check fairness?" Compare error and outcome rates across groups, and discuss which measure fits the use.
- "Did you slow the project?" Say what it cost honestly and how you kept it small.

**Common mistakes:**
- Inventing a dramatic case.
- Raising a concern without evidence.
- Going outside the company before using internal routes.
- Stating legal conclusions as facts.
