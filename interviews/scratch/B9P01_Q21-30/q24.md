**Say this first:** I changed my mind when the evidence changed, and I will describe what made me test my own view and what I did after I was wrong.

**Structure:** Use CARL; it fits because the learning is the point (see the STAR and CARL section above).
- Context: what you believed and why it was a reasonable belief.
- Action: what new fact or test showed otherwise, how you checked it was not noise, and how you told people.
- Result: the changed decision and what it did to the project, even if costly.
- Learning: a habit that makes you catch this sooner.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (Illustrative; replace with your own.)
"I was sure a gradient boosted model was the right choice for a [prediction task], so I spent two weeks tuning it. A colleague suggested a plain logistic baseline as a check. I did it quickly, mostly to show it was worse.

It was close, within the noise of cross-validation. I did not want to just accept that, so I ran it on a later time window. The simple model held up better, and it was easier to explain to the business.

I told the team the same day, including that I had been attached to the first approach. We switched. The model was easier to monitor and we saved the tuning time.

Now I build the simple baseline first and treat the complex model as something that has to earn its place."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I never really change my mind unless the data is clear," or a story where someone else forced you to change. The first suggests rigidity. The second shows no reasoning of your own.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Intellectual honesty: you can say "I was wrong" without defensiveness.
- The evidence that moved you: a test, not pressure or fatigue.
- How you communicated the change to others.
- Whether you learned a habit from it.

**Make it yours:**
- Which real decision or conclusion of yours was reversed?
- What did you believe, and what made that belief reasonable at the time?
- What exactly changed your view, and how did you check it?
- Who did you tell, and how soon?
- What do you do differently now?

**Follow-ups:**
- "How do you avoid flip-flopping?" I change when there is new evidence I can show, and I say what evidence would change it again.
- "Was it embarrassing?" It was, a little; saying so early made it smaller.
- "What if the team disagreed with your new view?" I share the test and invite them to try to break it.

**Common mistakes:**
- A trivial example (changing a chart colour).
- Changing mind only because of seniority or pressure.
- Not saying what you believed before.
- Pretending you were right all along.
