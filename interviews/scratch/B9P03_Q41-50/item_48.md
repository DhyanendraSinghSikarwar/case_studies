**Say this first (1 line):** I would stay curious and positive, ask how the level was decided and what would be expected at the next one, and then state clearly what I am hoping for.

**Structure:** Ask, share, propose (editor guidance [G]).
1. Thank them and say you are still interested.
2. Ask how the level was determined and what the level means here: scope, expectations, how people progress.
3. Share the evidence for your case briefly: scope you have owned, work at that level, what you expect to own.
4. Propose options: the higher level now, a review of level after a set period, or a clear path to it, with goals in writing.
5. Accept the answer calmly; titles vary by company, so compare the actual work and not just the label.
A guide notes that the title in an offer letter is not always fixed (snippet only); ask the recruiter how level decisions are handled.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** Illustrative; replace with your own. "Thank you for the offer, I am excited about the team. Could you help me understand how the level was decided and what it means here? In my last role I [real example of scope, for example led the design of a model pipeline and mentored two people], and I was hoping for a role at [expected level]. I may be reading the levels differently than you do, so I would like to compare. Is there room to discuss the level now, or an agreed review after [period] with clear goals? What I care about most is the scope of the work and a path to grow."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "That is below what I deserve, I will not accept it." It opens with a demand and no reasoning, and treats the title as a verdict. The reverse mistake is accepting a level that does not fit the work and resenting it later.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Calm, constructive tone.
- Evidence of scope, not a claim of status.
- Understanding that levels differ between companies.
- Willingness to agree a path rather than demand.

**Make it yours:**
- List the scope you have really owned: size of team, projects, decisions, mentoring.
- Check the company's level descriptions where public, and compare honestly.
- Decide what matters to you: title, scope, review date or something else.
- Do not claim responsibilities you did not have.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
Thank you, I am glad to have this offer and keen on the team.
Could you walk me through how the level of [level] was decided?
My work at [last role] included [scope], so I had expected [level].
Would it be possible to discuss that, or agree a review at [date] with written goals?
```

**Follow-ups:**
- What if they will not move it? "I look at the scope and the review path, and decide on those."
- Is the title the issue? "The work and the growth path matter more; I want those clear."
- Would you take a lower level for a better team? "Possibly; I would want a written review point."

**Common mistakes:**
- Treating the level as an insult.
- Comparing titles across companies as if equal.
- Overstating your past scope.
- Raising it after accepting.
