**Say this first (1 line):** I carried out the decision while raising my concern through the right channel, and I can say clearly where my line was and what I did at that point.

**Structure:** STAR with a values step (editor guidance [G]).
- Situation: a decision made above you that you disagreed with on principle, not just on taste.
- Task: your role in carrying it out.
- Action: what you said, to whom, in what order, what you proposed instead, and what you did when the decision stayed.
- Result: the outcome and what you learned about where your line sits.
- Keep it to something you can discuss calmly. Choose a case where you did not break rules.
Company-specific note: Book 7 Part 3 (Atlassian, Thin) describes a values-focused interview from third-party guides and company documents; check the company's current stated values before tailoring.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** Illustrative; replace with your own. "My manager asked our team to [ship a report / launch a feature] on a date, using a metric I thought could mislead customers because [reason]. I did not want to refuse outright, so I first wrote down my concern with an example and asked for a short call. I proposed [add a caveat / change the label] as a small change. My manager heard me and kept the date but agreed to the caveat. I did the work and checked the caveat was visible. What I learned is to raise concerns early, with a specific alternative. If it had gone further, I would have escalated through [the proper channel, for example the data owner or HR], not stayed quiet."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I just did what I was told; it was not my call," or "I refused and told them they were wrong." The first shows no values; the second shows no judgement or process. A made-up dramatic case also fails at the first follow-up.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Whether you have values you can state.
- Calm, specific communication of the concern.
- Use of proper channels and a workable alternative.
- Honesty about what happened, including partial outcomes.
- Judgement on where the line is.

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a real case, small or large, where you had to act against your preference.
- Write down the exact concern in one sentence and who heard it.
- Say what you proposed and what you finally did.
- If you cannot recall such a case, say so honestly and describe how you would handle it; do not invent one.
- Do not name confidential details of a past employer.

**Follow-ups:**
- Where is your line? "Where someone could be materially misled or harmed; I raise it and escalate to the right person."
- Would you have left? "Only after I had raised it and seen it unaddressed; I would think about it carefully."
- Did you tell anyone else? "Only through the channels my employer provides, and I checked the policy first."

**Common mistakes:**
- Telling the story as pure heroism.
- Skipping the step where you raised it.
- Sharing confidential details.
- Having no line at all.
