**Say this first (1 line):** I gave up a fast win because it would have cost us more later, and here is how I decided and how I brought people along.

**Structure:** (STAR)
1. Situation: the quick win on offer and who wanted it.
2. Task: your decision or recommendation, and the long-term risk you saw.
3. Action: how you weighed the options, what evidence you used, what you proposed (often a smaller step now plus the durable fix), and how you communicated the trade-off.
4. Result: what happened later, honestly, including any cost of waiting.
5. Reflection: when you would choose the quick win instead.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative; replace with your own story)
"A stakeholder wanted a dashboard number by Friday, and I could hit it with a manual query. I knew that number would be reused and the query had a flaw in how it handled duplicate records. I told them I could give a rough figure by Friday, labelled as provisional, but that I recommended two more weeks to build a proper pipeline. I showed them how the shortcut gave different answers on two samples. They accepted the provisional figure and the delay. The pipeline later stopped two reports from disagreeing. It did cost us a slower first week, and in a true emergency I would have chosen the quick version and said so clearly."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I always think long term, so I refused the shortcut." It sounds rigid and provides no trade-off, no stakeholder and no cost. Do not claim you never take shortcuts; interviewers know that is untrue.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Judgement about when a shortcut is safe and when it is not.
- Communicating the trade-off to the person who wanted speed.
- Offering a middle path, not just a refusal.
- Honesty about the cost and the outcome.

Company note: long-term thinking is an Amazon-style principle name in public guides (editor knowledge; check the company's current list; Book 7 Part 1, Amazon, caveat on 14 versus 16 principles).

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a real decision where speed and durability conflicted.
- State what the quick option was and the specific risk you saw.
- Say who you told, and what words you used.
- Include a downside you accepted.
- For data-science trade-offs with numbers, see Book 6 Part 6; here the focus is judgement and communication.

**Follow-ups:**
- *What if the deadline could not move?* "I would deliver the provisional version with clear labels and the follow-up plan, and make the risk visible."
- *Was it worth it?* "[Real answer]. Yes, mostly, though the delay cost us [real cost]."

**Common mistakes:**
- Painting the quick win as foolish.
- Blaming a stakeholder.
- No cost of waiting mentioned.
- Claiming a perfect outcome you cannot show.
