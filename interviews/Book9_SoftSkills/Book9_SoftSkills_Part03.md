## How to talk about pay without numbers in this book

Every pay and negotiation script in this book is written with placeholders, never with amounts. Where you would say a figure, the script shows a bracket such as [your target range], [their number] or [your minimum]. Three reasons. First, any number printed here would be wrong for your role, city, level and year, and would date quickly. Second, the skill the interviewer or recruiter sees is the process: how you ask, listen, compare and respond. Third, this is coaching on process and wording, not advice on what you are owed or what an employer may or may not do.

How to use the convention: before any call, fill each bracket with your own researched figure from sources you trust, and keep it in your notes, not in the script. Say it aloud only when you have chosen to. Where a question touches rules (for example how current pay, notice periods or checks are handled), the scripts say "ask the recruiter or HR how this is handled" and you should check official sources yourself; this book gives no legal advice. Be honest at every step: do not invent offers, deadlines or experience.

### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you took ownership of something that was not strictly your job.
**Asked at:** Amazon-style principle guides group questions by principle, ownership among them (levels.fyi, carrus.io and igotanoffer pages; the snippets showed principle lists and one ownership-adjacent prompt, not this exact wording); Book 7 Part 1 (Amazon, C3) reports behavioural questions tied to the Leadership Principles using real past examples, anecdotal and third-party; Amazon's list differs between guides (14 versus 16), so check the current one; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I saw a gap nobody owned, I took it on without being asked, and I made sure it stayed fixed.

**Structure:** STAR (see "STAR and CARL" in Book 9 Part 1), tilted toward ownership.
1. Situation: the gap and why it was not your job (one or two sentences).
2. Task: why you decided it was yours to act on, and who you told.
3. Action: what you personally did, including the dull follow-through.
4. Result: what changed, plus what you did so it would not break again.
5. Reflection: one thing you would do differently.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative template; replace with your own story)
"On [project], our weekly [report or model refresh] kept failing quietly, and nobody owned the job because it sat between the data team and the platform team. I was a consumer of it, so it was not my job. But my [stakeholder] was making decisions from stale numbers. I told my manager I would take it for a month. I traced the failure to [cause], wrote a check that alerts on a missing refresh, and documented the steps. Then I agreed with both teams who would own it afterwards and handed it over. The failures stopped, and [stakeholder] stopped asking whether the numbers were current. What I would do differently is raise the ownership question earlier, rather than waiting until it hurt."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I always go above and beyond. Once I fixed a bug nobody else wanted to touch." No stakes, no reason it was unowned, and no sign of follow-through. It also sounds like doing other people's work for credit.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Whether you acted without being told, yet told the right people.
- Follow-through past the first fix, not only the heroic moment.
- A clear "I", with credit to others where it is due.
- Judgement: you did not drop your own duties to do it.

**Make it yours:**
- List two or three moments you stepped into an unowned problem; pick the one with a visible result.
- Write what you were responsible for at the time, to show it really was outside your role.
- Name the person you informed before or while acting.
- State what changed afterwards, in your own words and your own evidence.
- Practise the "what I would change" line aloud; it is the part most candidates skip.
- Do not use AI to write the story: live follow-ups such as "what exactly did you do on day two?" expose borrowed detail.

**Follow-ups:**
- *Did your manager know?* "Yes. I told them what I was taking on and for how long, so my main work was not at risk."
- *What if it had gone wrong?* "I would have said so early and asked for help, because I had told people I owned it."

**Common mistakes:**
- Picking a story that was clearly part of your job.
- Claiming ownership of a team effort without saying what you did.
- Skipping the handover or prevention step.
- Heroics that ignore your own commitments.
- Company note: Amazon-style loops are reported to ask for past examples against principles (Book 7 Part 1, Amazon, C3; anecdotal, third-party). Principle names here are editor knowledge, so check the company's current list.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you put a customer's or end user's need ahead of what was easiest for your team.
**Asked at:** Amazon-style principle guides list customer-focus prompts first (a LeetCode discussion list begins with it; the snippet did not show the questions); Book 7 Part 1 (Amazon, C3) reports behavioural questions tied to the Leadership Principles using real past examples, anecdotal and third-party; Amazon's list differs between guides (14 versus 16), so check the current one; Book 7 Part 1 (Google, C1) lists user focus among Googleyness attributes, third-party; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I chose the harder path because the person using our work would have been worse off with the easy one.

**Structure:** STAR, with the trade-off made visible.
1. Situation: who the customer or end user was and what they needed.
2. Task: the easier option for the team and what it would have cost the user.
3. Action: how you made the case, what extra work you took on, and who agreed.
4. Result: the effect on the user, and the cost to the team, said honestly.
5. Reflection: how you now weigh user need against effort.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative template; replace with your own story)
"We were about to ship a [model or dashboard] that met the brief, but when I sat in on two sessions with [the users], I saw they could not act on the output. It was easy to ship as specified. I showed my lead a short recording and proposed a smaller scope with a clearer output for the first release, which meant dropping [feature] for now. The team was not thrilled about the extra [two weeks] of rework, so I took the redesign of the output myself and asked the users to test it twice. After release, [the users] actually used it, and support questions dropped. I would involve users earlier next time, so the rework is not a late surprise."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "The customer is always right, so I did whatever they asked." That is order-taking, not judgement. It names no trade-off, no cost, and no evidence about what the user needed.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- You found the real need by talking to users or looking at behaviour.
- You were honest about the cost to the team.
- You brought evidence, not just opinion, to the disagreement.
- The outcome is measured by user benefit, not by effort spent.

**Make it yours:**
- Choose a project where you met or observed real users, not only a ticket.
- Write the easy path and the harder path as two short lines.
- Name what the harder path cost and who bore it.
- Pick the evidence you used, such as a session, a log or a complaint.
- Say what the user could do afterwards that they could not before.
- Keep it to your real project; interviewers probe the smallest details.

**Follow-ups:**
- *How did you know what they needed?* "I watched them use it and asked what they did next with the output."
- *What did the team lose?* "Time and one feature for the first release. I said so up front."

**Common mistakes:**
- Treating internal stakeholders as the only customers, or ignoring them completely.
- No trade-off, so nothing was really chosen.
- Claiming user benefit with no evidence.
- Blaming the team for taking the easy path.
- Book 7 Part 1 (Google, C1) lists user focus among the attributes in its Googleyness round (third-party); Amazon-style loops are in Book 7 Part 1 (Amazon, C3). Check each company's current wording.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you had to act quickly without having all the information.
**Asked at:** Amazon-style guides list a decide-with-incomplete-information prompt under the speed principles (one guide snippet); no public page found for the exact wording; Book 7 Part 1 (Amazon, C3) reports behavioural questions tied to the Leadership Principles using real past examples, anecdotal and third-party; Amazon's list differs between guides (14 versus 16), so check the current one; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I decided on what I knew, named what I did not, and set up a way to correct course quickly.

**Structure:** STAR, with the decision rule shown.
1. Situation: the deadline or cost of waiting.
2. Task: the decision that was yours, and what was missing.
3. Action: what you checked in the time available, the assumption you stated, and the safeguard (a small rollout, a check, a rollback).
4. Result: what happened, including any part you got wrong.
5. Reflection: how you decide how much information is enough.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative template; replace with your own story)
"Our [pipeline or model] started giving odd output the evening before [an important review]. I could not find the cause that night. I had two choices: delay and miss the review, or use the last good version. I checked that the previous version still passed our basic checks, told [my lead and the stakeholders] exactly which version they were seeing and that one input was under investigation, and set an alert on the suspect input. The review went ahead. The next morning we found [the cause], and the earlier numbers were fine apart from [one small difference] that I flagged. I would now keep a known-good fallback ready as standard, so the choice is quicker."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I trust my gut and move fast; speed matters more than perfection." It shows no thinking about risk, no communication, and no way to recover. It can sound reckless.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- A reasoned view of what was reversible and what was not.
- Stating assumptions aloud, not hiding the gaps.
- A safeguard or fast feedback loop.
- Honesty about what turned out wrong.

**Make it yours:**
- Find a real decision with a clock on it, ideally a smaller one you remember well.
- Write down what you knew and what you guessed, in two lists.
- State the safeguard you used, even if it was only telling people.
- Include one thing that did not go perfectly.
- Say what you would do now with the same pressure.

**Follow-ups:**
- *What if the fallback had failed too?* "I would have told people the truth early and offered a smaller deliverable."
- *How much information is enough?* "More when the choice is hard to undo; less when it is cheap to reverse."

**Common mistakes:**
- Describing a decision with no real deadline.
- Presenting a guess as certainty to others.
- No mention of risk or reversibility.
- A story where it all worked and nothing was learned.
- Amazon-style guides place this under speed-related principles (Book 7 Part 1, Amazon, C3; anecdotal). Check the company's current list.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A recruiter asks early on, 'What are your expectations?' How do you respond without naming a number?
**Asked at:** Public guides on the expectations question agree on the pattern of deferring, asking about the role and the range, then giving a researched range if pressed (exec.com, builtin.com, Robert Half, Coursera pages); commonly reported in public guides; the book uses placeholders only; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I would like to understand the role and the range first, and I am happy to share my own range once we know it fits.

**Structure:** Defer, ask, then give a researched range only if pressed.
1. Show interest in the role first, in one line.
2. Ask what range the company has set for it, and for which level.
3. If they share it, say whether it is in your zone, without drama.
4. If they ask again, give your researched range as a range, tied to the role's scope. Keep it as the whole package, not one item.
5. Stay open: pay is one part of the package.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative; replace with your own wording)
"I am excited about this role, and I would like to understand it better before talking about pay, so I do not give you a number that does not fit. Could you share the range you have for this position and level? ... Thanks, that helps. Based on my research for similar roles and my experience with [skills], I am looking at [your target range], and I am open to discussing the whole package, including [other factors that matter to you]. Does that fit what you have in mind?"

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "Whatever you think is fair." It gives away your position and signals you have not researched. The opposite also fails: "I will not tell you anything." It reads as rigid and stalls the process.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Preparation: you have done your research.
- Composure and respect for the process.
- Flexibility on the package, not only one figure.
- Honesty: a range you could actually accept.

**Make it yours:**
- Research the role from sources you trust (job posts that show ranges, peers, public pay-range listings and peers) and write the range in your private notes.
- Decide your minimum before the call, and keep it private.
- List two package items you care about besides pay, such as [learning support] or [remote days].
- Say the sample aloud three times in your own words.
- Do not let AI write the script and then recite it; a recruiter can tell when a reply does not match your voice on a follow-up.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
Thanks for the call. I am really interested in [role]. Before we talk about pay,
could you share the range the team has for this role and level?
(If pressed) From my research and my experience in [skill area], I am looking
at [your target range], and I am open to discussing the full package.
Does that fit your range, [their range]?
```

**Follow-ups:**
- *I need a number to proceed.* "Understood. Given what I know now, [your target range] is where I would look. If the scope is different, I would adjust."
- *What is the lowest you would take?* "I would rather compare the whole package once I have the details."

**Common mistakes:**
- Naming one rigid figure too early.
- Giving a range whose bottom you would not accept.
- Treating pay as the only item.
- Being evasive without ever offering something.
- Be honest: never claim another offer or number that does not exist.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you ask a recruiter about the pay range for a role, and at what point in the process do you ask?
**Asked at:** Career guides say to ask what range the company has budgeted, and many recruiters will share it (one search summary); commonly reported in public guides; recruiter-screen topic; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** Could you tell me the range for this role and level, and how pay is decided for it?

**Structure:** Ask early, plainly, and as process.
1. Timing: ask in the first recruiter screen or when they ask about expectations, so neither side wastes time.
2. Ask for the range for the role and level, not for a promise.
3. Ask what else is in the package (bonus structure, learning support, flexibility) as separate items.
4. Say thanks, note it, and do not react either way on the call.
5. Follow up in writing if the range was vague.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative; replace with your own wording)
"Before we go further, it would help to know the range for this role and level, so we can check we are in the same place. Could you share what the team has budgeted? ... Thank you. And besides the headline pay, how is the package built, for example any bonus, learning support or flexibility? I will keep that in mind and we can come back to it once we know whether the role is a good match both ways."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** Asking first thing in a cold email, "What is the pay?", before any conversation. It can read as only interested in pay. Waiting until the final round and then being surprised also fails: it wastes everyone's time.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Professional tone: it is a normal, calm question.
- Awareness that pay has several parts.
- Respect for the process, with no pressure.
- Clear notes and follow-through.

**Make it yours:**
- Decide the point you will ask: usually the first recruiter conversation.
- Write your question in one sentence, in your own words.
- List three package items you want to understand.
- Note the answer in your tracker, with date and who said it.
- If they decline to share, ask when they can, and keep going politely.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
Hi [recruiter name], thanks for the time. To make sure we are aligned, could you
share the range for [role] at [level]? I would also like to understand how the
rest of the package is structured. If it is easier, I am happy to take it by
email by [date].
```

**Follow-ups:**
- *We do not share ranges at this stage.* "That is fine. When in the process would you be able to? I want to plan my time well."
- *What are you expecting?* "I would like to hear your range first; then I can tell you whether it works. See Q4 for the full answer."

**Common mistakes:**
- Reacting with surprise, delight or disappointment on the call.
- Asking only about the headline pay.
- Never asking and then assuming.
- Pushing hard when the recruiter has already said they cannot share it yet.
- How a company handles range disclosure differs; ask the recruiter or HR and check official sources. This book gives no legal advice.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why do you want to join our company?
**Asked at:** Why-this-company is a standard HR-round question (Naukri's data-scientist HR list includes why you want to work in the organisation; hyring and goodspace HR guides); Book 7 Part 3 lists why-Visa among Visa's behavioural topics (third-party); commonly reported in public guides; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** Here is what I know about the work and the team, here is how it connects to what I have done, and here is what I want to learn.

**Structure:** Three parts, each with something specific.
1. The work: one real thing about the product, problem or team that you looked up.
2. The match: one experience of yours that fits it.
3. The growth: what you want to learn there, honestly.
4. Optional: a question that shows you want details.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative; replace with your own research)
"Three things. First, I read about [a real product, paper or engineering post], and the way you handle [problem] interests me, because I have worked on [a similar problem]. Second, the role looks like it combines [two skills I use], which is where I do my best work. Third, I want to grow in [specific area], and this team seems to have people doing that daily. I also noticed [one thing from your careers or team page], and I would like to ask how that shows up in a typical week for this role."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "You are a great company with a good brand and a great culture." It fits any company, shows no research, and an interviewer cannot tell it from the previous candidate's answer.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Evidence you researched this company, not just any.
- A believable link between your experience and their work.
- Realistic motivation, not flattery.
- Curiosity: a question of your own.

**Make it yours:**
- Spend 30 minutes on the company's careers page, engineering writing or product pages; note two specific facts.
- Write one project of yours that links to each fact.
- Name something you honestly want to learn.
- If you only know the company from a recruiter message, say so and ask a question.
- Check the company's own pages for anything you quote; never repeat a rumour as fact.
- Book 7 gives profiles for some companies; use them as a starting list, not a script. Why-this-company is reported as a topic in Visa's behavioural round (Book 7 Part 3, Visa; third-party).

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
I am drawn to [team or product] because of [specific thing I read]. It connects
to my work on [your project]. I would like to grow in [area], and I would
like to ask how the team approaches [topic].
```

**Follow-ups:**
- *Why not our competitor?* "I am talking to a few places. What stood out here is [specific thing]. I would judge by the work and team."
- *What do you know about us?* "Mainly [two facts]. I would like to learn more about [topic]."

**Common mistakes:**
- Praise with no specifics.
- Mentioning only pay, location or brand.
- Reading out the company's mission statement.
- Criticising your current employer to make the new one look good.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why are you looking to move now?
**Asked at:** Reason-for-leaving is a standard HR-round question (Naukri, hyring and goodspace HR guides, third-party); commonly reported in public guides; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I am moving toward something specific, and I am saying it without blaming anyone.

**Structure:** Toward, not away.
1. One line on what you have achieved or learned in your current role.
2. What you want next that this role offers (scope, domain, depth).
3. A neutral fact about why now, if there is one.
4. A bridge back to this role.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative; replace with your own reasons)
"I have learned a lot at [current company]. Over the last [period] I [one real result]. What I want next is [deeper work in an area or bigger problems], and the role here offers that more directly than my current path does. The timing is because [a neutral reason, for example the project I joined for has finished]. I am not leaving because of one bad thing; I am looking for [specific growth]. That is why this position stood out."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "My manager is poor and the team is toxic, and there is no growth." Even if true, it sounds like blame and makes the interviewer wonder how you will speak about them later. A vague "just a change" also fails.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Maturity: no blame, no drama.
- A positive, specific reason to move.
- Consistency with the rest of your story.
- Honesty, without oversharing.

**Make it yours:**
- Write your real reasons privately, then pick the true ones that are fair to say aloud.
- Link the reason to something this role really offers.
- Check it fits your CV timeline.
- If the reason is a layoff or break, state it simply and move to what you did; see the layoff and gap items in this part.
- Say it aloud; if it feels bitter, rework it.
- Never invent a reason; a follow-up question will expose it.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
I have enjoyed [current role] and learned [skill]. I want to move into
[type of work], and this role is a direct step. The timing is [neutral reason].
```

**Follow-ups:**
- *Did you try to grow where you are?* "Yes, I raised it with [manager] and took on [thing]. The next step I want is not available there."
- *Would you stay for the right package?* "Package matters, but I am mainly looking for [the work]."

**Common mistakes:**
- Blaming a manager or team.
- Making it only about pay.
- A story that changes between interview rounds.
- Over-sharing personal detail.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is your notice period, and how soon can you realistically join?
**Asked at:** India HR-round guides list notice period, buyout and joining date among the joinability points, and say to give a specific last working day (a goodspace, hyring or jobrise-type guide; third-party); commonly reported in public guides; terms vary by employer, so check your own contract with HR; no legal advice; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** My notice period is [your notice], and here is the earliest realistic last working day and what I am doing to make a clean handover.

**Structure:** Fact, plan, flexibility.
1. State your notice period as it stands in your own paperwork; check it with HR if unsure.
2. Give a specific last working day estimate, not just a duration.
3. Say what you will do for handover.
4. Say whether any adjustment is possible and that you would ask your employer; do not promise what you do not control.
5. Offer to confirm in writing once you have an offer.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative; replace with your own details)
"My notice period is [your notice period]. If I accept an offer around [date], my last working day would be about [date]. I would use the time to hand over [projects] properly. Some employers allow an early release or a buyout, but I do not know whether mine would, so I would ask HR once there is a written offer and tell you what I learn. I will not commit to a date I cannot keep."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "I can join immediately" when you cannot, or "Whatever you need." Promising a date you do not control, then missing it, harms trust. The honest answer with a plan works better.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Reliability: a realistic date.
- Professional exit and handover habits.
- Clear distinction between fact and what you will ask.
- Flexibility without false promises.

**Make it yours:**
- Read your own contract or ask HR how your notice is handled; terms vary by employer. Check official sources. This book gives no legal advice.
- Work out the earliest and the likely last working day.
- Write the handover steps you would take, from your real projects.
- Ask yourself whether you want to raise early release before or after you hold an offer.
- Keep your answer the same across all rounds.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
My notice period is [your notice period]. If we reach an offer by [date], my
last working day would be around [date]. I would like to hand over
[projects] properly. I will confirm any early release with my employer once I
have a written offer, and tell you straight away.
```

**Follow-ups:**
- *Can you join sooner?* "I can ask my employer once there is a written offer. I will not promise it before I know."
- *Will your employer try to keep you?* "They may; I would decide on the role, not on that. See the counter-offer item in this part."

**Common mistakes:**
- Guessing the terms instead of checking your contract or asking HR.
- Giving a date you cannot keep.
- Offering to skip handover.
- Telling the new employer something different from what your current one has been told.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision, made your case, and then committed to it once it was made.
**Asked at:** Disagree-and-commit is an Amazon-style principle prompt; the search found no concrete example question for it, so the wording is editor-designed; Book 7 Part 1 (Amazon, C3) reports behavioural questions tied to the Leadership Principles using real past examples, anecdotal and third-party; Amazon's list differs between guides (14 versus 16), so check the current one; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I made my case with evidence, accepted the decision when it went the other way, and then worked to make it succeed.

**Structure:** STAR with two clear beats.
1. Situation: the decision and your disagreement, in one line.
2. Task: why you believed it mattered.
3. Action (a): how you made your case, with evidence, to the right person.
4. Action (b): what you did after the decision, to help it succeed.
5. Result and reflection: what happened, and what you learned about disagreeing well.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** (illustrative template; replace with your own story)
"My team lead chose [approach A] for [project]. I thought [approach B] would be safer, because [reason]. ### Q21 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you ask for time to consider an offer, especially when the deadline is tight?

2. Name the real reason you need time (for example, to read the written terms, or to finish another process that is already scheduled).

4. Offer something useful in return, such as questions you can send today.


**Common mistakes:**
- Asking without a date.
- Sounding lukewarm.
- Saying something untrue about other offers or deadlines.

I may be reading the levels differently than you do, so I would like to compare. Is there room to discuss the level now, or an agreed review after [period] with clear goals? What I care about most is the scope of the work and a path to grow."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "That is below what I deserve, I will not accept it." It opens with a demand and no reasoning, and treats the title as a verdict. The reverse mistake is accepting a level that does not fit the work and resenting it later.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Calm, constructive tone.
- Evidence of scope, not a claim of status.
- Understanding that levels differ between companies.
- Willingness to agree a path rather than demand.

**Make it yours:**
- List the scope you have really owned: size of team, projects, decisions, mentoring.
- Check the company's level descriptions where public, and compare honestly.
- Decide what matters to you: title, scope, review date or something else.
- Do not claim responsibilities you did not have.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
Thank you, I am glad to have this offer and keen on the team.
Could you walk me through how the level of [level] was decided?
My work at [last role] included [scope], so I had expected [level].
Would it be possible to discuss that, or agree a review at [date] with written goals?
```

**Follow-ups:**
- What if they will not move it? "I look at the scope and the review path, and decide on those."
- Is the title the issue? "The work and the growth path matter more; I want those clear."
- Would you take a lower level for a better team? "Possibly; I would want a written review point."

**Common mistakes:**
- Treating the level as an insult.
- Comparing titles across companies as if equal.
- Overstating your past scope.
- Raising it after accepting.

### Q49 ⭐ 🔴 You were laid off. How do you explain it in the HR round?
**Asked at:** Layoff-explanation guides (designgurus, 4 Corner Resources; career blogs) advise one or two factual sentences without blame; commonly reported in public guides; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I say it in one or two factual sentences, without blame, and move straight to what I did and what I want next.

**Structure:** Fact, brief, forward (editor guidance [G]; layoff-explanation guides advise one or two factual sentences without blame, snippet only).
1. State what happened: the company [reduced the team / closed the project], and it was a company decision.
2. Say one thing about your work or outcome that is true.
3. Say what you did since: learning, projects, interviews.
4. Link to why this role fits.
5. Stop and let them ask; do not over-explain.
Ask HR how references and reasons for leaving are recorded; this book gives no legal advice.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** Illustrative; replace with your own. "In [month], [company] reduced the size of the [team] as part of a company-wide restructure, and my role was one of those affected. I was disappointed, but it was not about performance; my manager can speak to my work on [project]. Since then I have [real action: completed a course, built a project, helped a former colleague]. I am looking for a role where I can [what you want to do], and this role on [team] does that. I am happy to answer anything you would like to know about it."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** A long, bitter account of the company or leaders, or hiding the facts and giving a vague answer. Blame makes the interviewer wonder how you would speak about them; vagueness invites suspicion.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Honesty and composure.
- No blame, no bitterness.
- How you used the time since.
- Clarity about what you want next.

**Make it yours:**
- Write the facts in two sentences using your own dates and wording.
- Name one real thing you have done since, not something you plan to do.
- Check whether a former manager or colleague is willing to speak for you, and ask them first.
- Practise saying it calmly in front of a friend; the first time you say it aloud should not be in the interview.

**Script (copy and adapt):**
```
[Company] [reduced / closed] [team or project] in [month], and my role was affected.
Before that I had worked on [one line of real work].
Since then I have [real action].
I am looking for [what you want], which is why this role interests me.
```

**Follow-ups:**
- Were you the only one? "No, [a number of colleagues] were affected, as far as I know."
- Will your manager give a reference? "I will check with them; I can give other contacts as well."
- How did it change you? "I [real change, for example keep my skills current, keep a record of my work]."

**Common mistakes:**
- Speaking badly about the former employer.
- Over-apologising.
- A long answer.
- Stating facts you cannot support.

### Q50 ⭐ 🔴 You were placed on a performance improvement plan at your last job. How do you discuss it honestly?
**Asked at:** No public page found for discussing a performance improvement plan; editor-designed, senior or niche; check the employer's reference practice with HR; search snippets only, not opened
**Say this first (1 line):** I would answer plainly: yes, I was on a plan, here is what the feedback was, here is what I did about it, and here is how I work now.

**Structure:** Fact, ownership, change, evidence (editor guidance [G]).
1. State it in one sentence, in neutral words, without arguing the case.
2. Name the specific feedback you accepted, in your own terms.
3. Say what you did: asked for clear expectations, changed a habit, sought help.
4. Give an outcome, as it actually was, and what is different now.
5. Offer to explain more and point to current evidence of your work.
Ask HR how references and past performance records are handled at this employer, and check official sources; this book gives no legal advice.

**Strong sample answer (spoken):** Illustrative; replace with your own. "Yes. In [year] I was placed on a performance plan. The feedback was that [specific thing, for example my updates were late and my models were not tested well enough]. I thought about it, and part of it was fair. I asked my manager for clear weekly targets, set up [practice, for example a checklist before hand-over], and asked a senior colleague to review my work. By the end of the plan [honest outcome, for example my work met the targets / I decided to move on]. Since then I use the same checklist, and I have [later evidence]. I would be glad to talk about what I have learned and what I do differently now."

**Weak version (and why it fails):** "My manager did not like me," or "it was just politics." It sounds like blame. Pretending it did not happen is also risky, because it may come up through references or checks; do not lie if asked directly.

**What the interviewer scores:**
- Honesty and maturity.
- Accountability without self-blame spiralling.
- Concrete changes in habits.
- Evidence you are doing well now.

**Make it yours:**
- Write the feedback in one factual line, as written or said to you.
- List what was fair and what you disagree with, and decide how to say the first part.
- Find one recent piece of evidence (a project, a review, a reference).
- You are not obliged to volunteer details unprompted; if asked directly, answer truthfully, and ask the recruiter or HR how it is handled.

**Follow-ups:**
- Do you agree with the feedback? "Partly; [which part] I accepted and acted on."
- Will your manager say the same? "I think they would say [honest line]."
- What did you learn? "[A habit you can show], which I still use."

**Common mistakes:**
- Blaming the manager or company.
- Denying it happened when asked.
- Having no change to point to.
- A long, emotional account.
