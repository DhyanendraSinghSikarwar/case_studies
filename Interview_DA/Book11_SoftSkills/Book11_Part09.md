### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Your mind goes completely blank in the middle of an answer. What do you say and do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); general guides describe blanking as common (unverified; no frequency data).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you stay composed and recover honestly, because blanking happens to almost everyone.
**Say this in the interview:** Pause, breathe once, say calmly that you need a moment, then restart from the last point you remember or from the simplest part of the answer.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** Silence for twenty seconds, then a nervous laugh and "Sorry, sorry, I forgot everything, I am so bad at this."
**Why it is weak:** Heavy apology and panic tell the interviewer more than the blank itself.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Give me a second, I want to put this in order." (Breathe, look at your notes if you have them.) "Let me come back to the main point: [the last thing you said]. From there, [continue with the next point]." If it does not come: "I have lost my thread on that part. The core of my answer is [one-line main point]. Shall I give an example instead?"
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Mid-answer on a hiring tracker project you forget the next step. You say: "One moment. I was explaining how I cleaned the tracker. The next step was checking duplicate candidate IDs, and then I built a weekly summary." Short pause, no apology, answer finishes. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** (Structure for a recovery) Point: acknowledge briefly. Reason: a short pause is normal and shows control. Example: restart from your last sentence or your core point. Link back: finish the answer and move on without commenting on it again.
**If you have no direct experience:** You do not need a past blank to answer. Say how you would handle it: "I pause, breathe, and restart from my last point. I also keep a three-word outline in my head for every answer: point, example, result."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Has this happened to you before?* Answer only truthfully; if yes, say how you recovered; if no, say how you prepare for it. 2) *Do you panic under pressure?* Answer truthfully; for example, "I feel it, so I slow down first, then speak."
**Common mistakes:** Long silence with no signal; over-apologising; starting a new unrelated answer; making up something just to fill the gap.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 The interviewer says, "You look nervous. Are you okay?" How do you reply?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); general career guides cover this remark (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, warmth and composure; whether you can name a feeling without being derailed by it.
**Say this in the interview:** Admit it lightly, show you are fine, and move forward.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "No, no, I am not nervous at all!" while speaking fast, or "Yes, I am very nervous, I really need this job."
**Why it is weak:** Denial sounds false, and over-sharing hands the interviewer your anxiety.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "A little, yes. I care about this role, so I want to do it justice. I am settling in now. Please go ahead." Then slow your next answer down and keep your voice steady.
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Interviewer: "You look a bit nervous." You: "Slightly, because this is a switch from HR into analytics and it matters to me. I am fine, though. Could I start with a quick walk through the attendance dashboard I built?" Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** (Structure) Point: yes, a little. Reason: it matters to me. Example: one calm sentence, no story. Link back: return to the question with a steady pace.
**If you have no direct experience:** Say only what is true. If you honestly are not nervous, say: "I am a bit more careful than usual, I want to give good answers." Do not claim a feeling or a project you do not have.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What helps you stay calm?* A short breath, writing a note, slowing down. 2) *Will this affect your work?* Say only what is true for you, for example "I usually settle once I start."
**Common mistakes:** Denying it; long confessions; joking too much; speaking faster after the question.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You did not understand the question. How do you ask the interviewer to repeat or clarify it without looking weak?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); clarifying before answering is a standard tip in analytics interview guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you clarify before analysing, which is a core analyst habit.
**Say this in the interview:** Restate what you understood and ask about the part you are unsure of.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** Guessing and answering a different question, or "Sorry, what?" three times.
**Why it is weak:** A wrong answer wastes time; vague requests make it look like you were not listening.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Just so I answer the right thing: are you asking about [your understanding]? I was not sure whether you meant [option A] or [option B]." Or for a long question: "I caught the first part, [part]. Could you repeat the last part, please?"
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Question: "How would you handle attrition metrics for a skewed department?" You: "Let me confirm: do you mean how I would measure attrition when one department has very few employees, so small changes look large? If so, I would report counts next to percentages." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** (Structure) Point: restate in one sentence. Reason: so I answer what you actually need. Example: name the two possible meanings. Link back: answer the confirmed version.
**If you have no direct example:** Use the steps above; clarifying is a skill you can show in the room without any past story. Practise one restating line.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why did you ask?* Analysts clarify the question before building anything. 2) *Do you often not understand?* Answer truthfully; for example, "I check the key words when a question is open."
**Common mistakes:** Apologising too much; asking the interviewer to repeat all of it when one word was unclear; answering something else to avoid asking.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 The interviewer asks about a tool you have never used, such as Tableau when you know only Power BI. How do you respond?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the "I do not know that tool yet" advice appears in career guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, transferable skills and how quickly you can learn.
**Say this in the interview:** Say plainly you have not used it, name the closest tool you know, and show how you would learn it.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Yes, I have used Tableau." (when you have not) or "No, I only know Power BI."
**Why it is weak:** Bluffing gets exposed on the next question; a flat no shows no effort.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I have not worked in [Tool B] yet. I know [Tool A] well, and the ideas of [data model, filters, charts, dashboards] carry over. I would [practise on a sample file, use the free version, follow the official tutorial] in my first [short time period]. Could you tell me how your team uses it?"
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I have not used Tableau. In Power BI I built an attendance dashboard with slicers and a monthly trend chart. I would rebuild the same dashboard in a free version of Tableau (from memory, unverified: check what is currently free) to learn it. A slicer is a filter button on a dashboard. Which features does your team use most?" Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: honest gap. Reason: related skills transfer. Example: one real thing you built in the tool you know. Link back: learning plan plus a question about how they use it.
**If you have no direct experience:** If you know neither tool well, name what you do know (for example Excel pivots and charts) and say you have started learning. Never claim exposure you do not have.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long would you need?* Give an honest estimate for basics, not mastery. 2) *Have you tried it at all?* Answer truthfully; if you did a tutorial, say so.
**Common mistakes:** Bluffing; criticising the tool; over-apologising; forgetting to link back to your real skills.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you stay calm and think clearly in a technical interview when you feel nervous?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); nerve-management guides from universities and recruiters are the source (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a practical method, not only a wish to be calm.
**Say this in the interview:** Describe two or three real habits: slow breathing, thinking out loud, and breaking a problem into small steps.
**Explain simply:**
- Nerves are normal; the aim is to work with them, not remove them.
- A slow exhale calms the body faster than telling yourself to relax.
- Thinking aloud turns panic into visible reasoning.
- Preparation lowers nerves: mock interviews and repeated practice on basics.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Before: sleep, eat, and do one mock interview with a friend or recorder.
2. At the start: three slow breaths, feet on the floor, sip of water.
3. On a question: restate it, write one line of assumptions, solve the easy case first.
4. When stuck: say "Let me break this down" and move to the next step.
5. After a miss: move on; do not carry it into the next question.
**Worked example or script:** "Let me restate: you want the top three departments by overtime. I will first check the columns, then group by department, then sort. If I get stuck on the syntax I will tell you the logic first." (This is a model script, not a claim about you.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What do you do when you freeze?* Pause, breathe, restate, begin with the simplest step. 2) *Does this method work?* Say honestly whether you have tried it; if you are still building the habit, say so.
**Common mistakes:** Rushing to answer; staying silent while thinking; memorising scripts; fighting the nervous feeling instead of slowing down.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 The interviewer asks, "What is a window function in SQL?" and you have never used one. What do you say?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); window functions are a commonly named SQL topic in analytics interview prep (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, your reasoning ability and whether you can learn from a hint.
**Say this in the interview:** Say you have not used it in work, share what you do understand, and show how you would learn it.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** A confident but wrong definition, or just "I do not know" and silence.
**Why it is weak:** A bluff sounds worse when probed; a bare "I do not know" shows no thinking.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I have not used window functions in a project. My understanding is that they calculate something across a set of rows related to the current row, without collapsing rows like GROUP BY does, for example a running total or a rank. I have used [GROUP BY / pivot tables] for similar work. I am practising [ROW_NUMBER, RANK, SUM OVER] on sample data."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "In Excel I built a running headcount by month. In SQL I believe a window function would do it: SUM(headcount) OVER (ORDER BY month). I have not written that in production, but I have tried a few sample queries." (Syntax from memory; check it before you quote it.) Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: honest gap. Reason: partial understanding shared. Example: a similar thing you did in Excel or GROUP BY. Link back: learning plan.
**If you have no direct experience:** If you have not even tried one, say so and describe the idea you do know. Offer to try a quick example if the interviewer lets you.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Difference from GROUP BY?* GROUP BY collapses rows; a window function keeps every row and adds a calculated value. 2) *Name one.* ROW_NUMBER or RANK, if you know it.
**Common mistakes:** Bluffing; saying "it is the same as GROUP BY"; claiming experience you do not have; giving up after one question.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You give an answer and the interviewer replies, "Are you sure?" How do you decide whether to hold your answer or change it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the "Are you sure?" probe is a common pressure test described in interview guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you reason under doubt, not whether you cave in or dig in.
**Say this in the interview:** Quickly recheck your logic aloud, then hold it with the reason or correct it openly.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** Instantly changing to the opposite answer, or repeating the same answer louder.
**Why it is weak:** The first shows no confidence; the second shows no flexibility.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Let me check that. [Walk through the logic quickly.] I still think it is [answer] because [reason]. If I have missed something, tell me and I will adjust." Or: "Looking again, I see my mistake. The correct answer is [new answer], because [reason]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** You said average attendance was 92%. "Are you sure?" You: "Let me recheck. 46 present out of 50 on one day is 92%. I used one day, though. For the month it would be different. Let me recompute with all days." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: pause and recheck. Reason: say the logic out loud. Example: test with a small case. Link back: hold with reasons or correct without defensiveness.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use the method above as how you would behave; no past story is needed. Practise with a friend who says "Are you sure?" to every answer.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you were right?* Hold calmly with the reason. 2) *What if you cannot tell?* Say what you are certain of and what you would verify.
**Common mistakes:** Changing the answer just because it was questioned; getting defensive; going silent; not explaining why you changed.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You realise five minutes later that an earlier answer was wrong. Do you go back to it, and how?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); advice on correcting mistakes appears in general career guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Integrity and self-awareness: do you own mistakes?
**Say this in the interview:** Yes, correct it briefly at a natural pause, give the right answer and move on.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** Say nothing and hope they did not notice, or apologise for a full minute.
**Why it is weak:** A silent error stays on record; a long apology makes a small slip look big.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "May I go back to an earlier question? I said [wrong answer]. On reflection, the correct answer is [right answer], because [one-line reason]. Thank you." Then continue.
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Earlier I said an INNER JOIN would keep unmatched employees. That was wrong: it drops them, and a LEFT JOIN keeps them. Thank you for letting me correct it." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: yes, correct it. Reason: accuracy matters more than looking perfect. Example: one clear sentence with the correct answer. Link back: continue without dwelling.
**If you have no direct experience:** If you have corrected a mistake at work (an HR report figure, a payroll entry), mention it only if true. Otherwise describe how you would behave: tell the person, fix it, and note why it happened.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the interviewer already moved on?* Correct at the next pause or at the end. 2) *Is it not risky?* Owning a mistake usually builds trust.
**Common mistakes:** Silence; over-apologising; making the correction long; correcting in a way that blames the question.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Your internet drops or your audio cuts out in the middle of a video interview. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); India and data blogs advise a hotspot backup (unverified). No India-specific policy on dropped connections was found.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Calm problem-solving and preparedness under things you cannot control.
**Say this in the interview:** Stay calm, try to reconnect fast, use a backup, and inform the interviewer clearly.
**Explain simply:**
- Drops happen; interviewers expect them occasionally.
- Speed and calm matter more than perfection.
- A backup plan prepared earlier is the real answer.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Wait about ten seconds; check if it is only audio (try muting and unmuting).
2. Rejoin the same link; if it fails, switch to a mobile hotspot.
3. Message the recruiter by email or chat: "Connection dropped, rejoining now. I can also dial in by phone if needed."
4. When back: "Sorry for the interruption. My last point was [point]. Shall I continue?"
5. If it will take long, politely ask to reschedule or continue by phone.
**Worked example or script:** "Apologies, my connection dropped. I have switched to my hotspot. The last thing I said was that I compared attrition by month. Shall I continue from there?" (Model script.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if it keeps happening?* Offer a phone call or a reschedule. 2) *Will this hurt you?* Rejoining calmly rarely does; I cannot guarantee how any company reacts.
**Common mistakes:** Panicking; vanishing without a message; not having a hotspot or phone number ready; blaming the interviewer.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 The interviewer points out a mistake in the SQL query or Excel formula you wrote live. How do you react?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); live-coding guides advise narrating and accepting feedback (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** How you take feedback and debug, not only whether the first attempt is perfect.
**Say this in the interview:** Thank them, read the error, fix it step by step, and explain your fix.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "No, it should work" (arguing), or freezing and apologising repeatedly.
**Why it is weak:** Defensiveness or panic hides your actual debugging ability.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Good catch, thank you. Let me look. I see the issue: [cause, for example the join column or the range]. I will change [part] to [fix] and rerun. Let me test it on [a small case] to be sure."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** The data is in rows 2 to 13 (12 rows of overtime hours) and you wrote =SUM(B2:B10), which covers only 9 rows. Interviewer points it out. You: "Thanks. My range stops at row 10, so it misses three rows. I will extend it to B13 and compare with the filtered total." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: accept the feedback. Reason: find the cause. Example: fix it and test. Link back: tell what you will check next time.
**If you have no direct experience:** If you have not coded live, say how you check your own work: read errors, test on a small sample, compare totals.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you avoid this normally?* I test on a small sample and compare totals. 2) *What if you cannot find it?* Ask for a hint after trying.
**Common mistakes:** Arguing; silence; random changes without reasoning; blaming the data or the tool.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 The interviewer keeps asking one technical question after another and you have said "I do not know" three times in a row. How do you handle the rest of the interview?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); this is a hard situational scenario drawn from career guides on not knowing answers (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Resilience, honesty and whether you can recover and show strengths after a rough stretch.
**Say this in the interview:** Reset, stop the pattern of bare "I do not know", and show reasoning plus what you do know.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** A fourth "I do not know" in a flat voice, or collapsing and ending the conversation early.
**Why it is weak:** It confirms the gap and shows no recovery.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I have not worked with that directly. Let me tell you how I would approach it: [first step, then second]. Could I also mention what I am stronger in? [Your real strengths, for example Excel, HR data, reporting.] I am working on this area through [true learning step]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** After three misses on SQL topics: "These are gaps in my SQL right now, and I am working on them. What I can show is that I cleaned an attendance file, built pivots and explained findings to managers. May I walk you through one?" Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: acknowledge gaps once. Reason: reasoning shows how you think. Example: move to your strongest true example. Link back: show how you are closing the gaps.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a class, a course project or a small practice dataset. Do not claim any project you have not done. After the interview, note the topics and study them.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why so many gaps?* I am switching careers and building depth step by step; here is my plan. 2) *Do you think you did well?* Be honest and balanced.
**Common mistakes:** Giving up emotionally; repeating "I do not know" without reasoning; bluffing on the fourth; blaming the interviewer.

### Q12 ⭐⭐ 🟢 The interviewer says, "Take a minute to think." What do you do with that minute?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the pause-and-structure tip appears in nerve-management guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can structure your thinking and use time well.
**Say this in the interview:** Use the minute to restate, outline briefly and decide the first step, then answer clearly.
**Explain simply:**
- A minute is long in a room; it is a gift, not a test of speed.
- Silence while thinking is accepted.
- A small outline makes the answer shorter and clearer.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Breathe once and restate the question in your mind.
2. Note the goal and any assumptions on paper.
3. Pick a simple structure: point, reason, example (or STAR for stories).
4. Choose your first sentence.
5. Say: "I am ready. Let me begin with..."
**Worked example or script:** For "How would you check if attrition is rising?": "(30 seconds to note) I would first define attrition, then pick a time window, then compare months, then check by department. Let me start with the definition." (Model script.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you still have no answer?* Share the part you know and ask a clarifying question. 2) *Can you use paper?* Yes, ask politely.
**Common mistakes:** Talking while thinking in circles; staring in panic; using the minute to rehearse a memorised script.

### Q13 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What do you do in the first two minutes of a video interview to settle yourself and make a good start?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); virtual-interview guides cover the opening minutes (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Professionalism, warmth and readiness.
**Say this in the interview:** Join a few minutes early, greet warmly, look at the camera and start slowly.
**Explain simply:**
- First impressions form in the opening minutes.
- A calm start sets the tone for the rest.
- Small rituals are more reliable than motivation.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Join 5 minutes early; check audio and video.
2. Sit upright, water nearby, notes just out of frame or on a second screen.
3. Take three slow breaths before the host admits you.
4. Smile, greet by name: "Good morning, thank you for your time."
5. Speak a little slower than normal for the first answer.
**Worked example or script:** "Good morning, Priya, can you hear me clearly? Thank you for the time today. I am looking forward to the conversation." (Model script; use your own words.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you are admitted late?* Greet and proceed; do not over-apologise. 2) *Where do you look?* Mostly at the camera.
**Common mistakes:** Joining at the last second; rushing; reading from the screen; checking your own preview too often.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you check your setup (camera, microphone, light, background, internet) before a virtual interview, and what is your backup plan?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); India and data blogs advise a tech check and hotspot backup (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Preparedness and respect for the interviewer's time.
**Say this in the interview:** Test everything the day before and again 15 minutes before, and have a hotspot and phone number ready.
**Explain simply:**
- Most disasters are preventable with a 10-minute check.
- Light and audio matter more than an expensive camera.
- A backup plan is part of the setup.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Day before: join a test call on the same platform; check camera, mic, speakers and screen share.
2. Light: face a window or lamp; avoid a bright window behind you.
3. Background: tidy and neutral; notifications off; close extra apps.
4. Internet: use a wired or strong wifi connection; test speed; charge the laptop and keep the charger plugged in.
5. Backup: mobile hotspot ready, phone charged, recruiter's number and email saved, link open in a second device if possible.
6. Fifteen minutes before: quick recheck and water nearby.
**Worked example or script:** If the connection drops: "My wifi failed; I have switched to my hotspot and I am back." (Model script.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if power fails?* Use phone with data and inform the recruiter. 2) *Do you wear headphones?* Yes if audio is clearer; test them.
**Common mistakes:** Testing only once; sitting with a window behind you; leaving notifications on; no backup connection.

### Q15 ⭐⭐ 🟢 The interviewer is on a phone or video call and joins ten minutes late, or the panel starts without introducing itself. How do you handle it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); this is a gap-fill situation, not a reported question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you stay calm and professional when things are messy, and whether you can politely take a little control.
**Say this in the interview:** "Stay relaxed and friendly, do not mention the delay as a complaint, and if names and roles are missing, ask politely at a natural moment."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "You are late, I have another meeting after this." Or sitting silent and confused while a panel fires questions.
**Why it is weak:** The first sounds rude; the second leaves you answering people you cannot identify.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "No problem at all, thank you for joining. [Pause, smile.] Before we start, could I quickly confirm who I am speaking with and what your roles are? It helps me answer in the right way." If time is short: "I understand we have [shorter time], so I will keep my answers focused. Please stop me if you want more detail on anything." While waiting, re-read your notes and breathe.
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "A panel of three joined eight minutes late and began with a technical question. I answered, then said, 'Sorry, could I confirm your names and roles so I can speak to each of you properly?' They introduced themselves, and one said she was the HR business partner, so I added a people-side example. (Invented example, for illustration only.)"
**STAR breakdown:** Point (stay warm, no complaint), Reason (the delay is usually not about you), Example (the polite ask for names and roles), Link back to the role (analysts deal with busy stakeholders all the time).
**If you have no direct experience:** You have probably not met this exact case; say "I have not faced this in an interview, but I have waited for late stakeholders in meetings and here is how I handle it," and describe only what is true for you. Say this only if it is true for you.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Would you ever leave?* (My suggestion, not a rule.) If the wait is very long with no message, send one polite email to the recruiter asking whether to reschedule. 2) *What if no one introduces themselves?* Ask once, politely, as above.
**Common mistakes:** Showing irritation; staying silent about not knowing who is who; apologising for something that is not your fault.

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you use the whiteboard, shared screen or notepad in a virtual round so that your thinking is visible?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); screen-share and narrate-while-sharing advice appears in a remote-interview page and prep guides in the sources file (snippet level, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Can they follow your reasoning, not just your final answer?
**Say this in the interview:** "I write my plan first in a few lines, think aloud while I work, and check the result before I say I am done."
**Explain simply:**
- Interviewers cannot see your head; they see only what you write and say.
- A visible plan (3 to 5 bullets) shows structure even if the code or formula has a small error.
- Narrate in short phrases: what you are doing and why, not every keystroke.
- Close the loop: test on a small example and say what you see.

**Plan / steps:**
1. Before the round: close extra windows, switch off notifications, increase font size, keep a blank notepad or text file open (check the platform allows it).
2. Restate the problem in one line and confirm it: "So I need the number of employees per department who joined this year, correct?"
3. Write the plan as bullets: columns needed, steps, expected output.
4. Build step by step, saying one line per step ("first I filter to this year").
5. Test with two or three rows or a quick total check; say what you expected.
6. Summarise: "This gives X; one edge case I would also check is blank departments."

**Worked example or script:**
"Let me write my plan first. One: filter employees to joining year 2026. Two: group by department. Three: count rows. [Types the query.] I expect each department to show a count; the total should equal the number of 2026 joiners, so I will check that." (Plan and example are for practice; the exact tool rules differ by company, so ask: "May I use a notepad while I think?")

### Q32 ⭐ 🟢 How do you prepare the night before and the morning of an interview to reduce nerves?

### Q37 ⭐ 🟡 You sent a thank-you email after the interview. What should it say, and should you mention a question you answered badly?
