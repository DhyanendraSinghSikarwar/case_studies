# Book 11 — Soft Skills, HR Round, GD & Career-Switch Story | Part 2: Why Data Analytics, Why This Company, Why Leave HR 🟢🟡🔴

**Target:** 40 questions | **Actual:** 45 questions
**Scope:** interview-room motivation questions: why data analytics, why this company, why leave HR, with follow-up probes, weak-vs-strong traps and role-play variants (outline-level career-switch strategy stays in Book 0).

### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why do you want to become a data analyst?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); appears in nearly every analyst question bank (unverified prep sites).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your reason is real, specific and about the work, not just pay or fashion.
**Say this in the interview:** "I kept finding that the part of my work I enjoyed most was answering a question with numbers. I want to do that full time, and I have been building the skills for it."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Data is the future and analysts earn well, so I want to move into it."
**Why it is weak:** It could be said by anyone, names no work you enjoy, and sounds like a trend or pay chase.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In my HR work, the tasks I liked best were [a true task, e.g. checking a report for errors or finding why a number moved]. I enjoyed [the specific part: cleaning the data, spotting the pattern, explaining it simply]. I realised I wanted that to be my main job, not a side task. So I have been learning [Excel / SQL (the language used to pull data from databases) / a BI tool (dashboard software such as Power BI)] and practising on [a true project or dataset]. I like that this role lets me turn data into something a manager can act on."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Every month I reconciled an attendance report for about 40 people by hand. It took three hours. I built a small sheet with formulas that did the same check in about 30 minutes, and I enjoyed finding the two rows that did not match. That is when I knew I liked this kind of problem."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: I like turning data into answers. Reason: one true task that showed it. Example: what I did and what changed. Link back to the role: why this job lets me do more of it.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a project or course. "I started a small project on [a public dataset] and noticed I lost track of time cleaning and questioning it. That told me this is the work I want, so I am building skills in [tools]." Do not claim HR report-building you did not do.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not stay in HR?* I am moving towards analysis work, not away from people topics. 2) *What have you done about it so far?* Name one true project or course (see Q21).
**Common mistakes:** Quoting pay or "data is the future"; giving a long life story; saying you "love numbers" with no example.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why do you want to join our company?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the "why us" question is common in Indian and global prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you researched them and picked them on purpose, rather than sending the same application everywhere.
**Say this in the interview:** "Two things stood out to me: [what the company does with data] and [what this team does]. I can see how my skills would help there."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "It is a big, well-known company with good brand value and a good location."
**Why it is weak:** Brand and location say nothing about the work or about you.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I read about [one true fact from their website, annual report, news or job post, e.g. what they sell or which markets they serve]. What interests me is [how data would matter there, e.g. tracking customers, operations or workforce]. This role works on [part of the job description], which matches what I have been practising in [your true skill or project]. I would like to grow here because [a true reason: learning from a larger team, real business data, a clear path]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I saw that the company serves [a type of customer] in several cities. I wondered how the team decides where to add capacity. The job post mentions weekly dashboards, and I have been building dashboards like that on practice data. I would like to do it on real business data."
Say this only if it is true for you. Check each fact before you say it; if you could not find a fact, do not guess one.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: why this company. Reason: one researched fact. Example: link to the job post. Link back: what I bring and what I want to learn.
**If you have no direct experience:** Your research is the evidence. Spend 30 minutes reading their site, job post and recent news, and note two real facts. Stay honest: "I am still learning about the industry, but here is what drew me."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What do you know about our products?* Answer only what you really read (Q4). 2) *Anywhere else you applied?* Be honest and tactful (Q39).
**Common mistakes:** Praising the brand only; reading the About page aloud; stating facts you did not verify.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why are you leaving HR to move into data analytics?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); career-switch blogs say switchers will hear this (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you are moving towards something with a plan, or running away, and whether you will stay.
**Say this in the interview:** "I am not leaving because of HR. I found that analysis is the work I enjoy most, and I want to do it full time. My HR background helps me understand the business questions behind the numbers."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "HR is boring and stressful, there is no growth, and I want a change."
**Why it is weak:** It is all complaint and no pull; it also suggests you may leave this job the same way.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In [number] years in HR, I kept being drawn to [a true task: reports, MIS, attrition numbers, payroll checks]. I enjoyed [the specific part]. Over [a true period] I learned [tools] and built [a true project]. So this is a planned move towards analytics, and I bring a good understanding of [people data, confidentiality, how managers ask questions]. I now want a role where analysis is the main job."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I was asked why attrition looked high in one team. I pulled the exit list, grouped it by tenure and saw most exits were within six months of joining. That finding changed how the team looked at onboarding. That question interested me more than any other task that month."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: moving towards analysis. Reason: what I enjoyed. Example: one true task plus my learning. Link back to the role: why this job fits.
**If you have no direct experience:** "I have mostly done HR operations work, so I started analytics on my own: [course, practice project]. That showed me I like the work, so this is a considered change."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Was anything wrong in HR?* See Q10. 2) *Will you go back?* See Q26.
**Common mistakes:** Criticising your employer or HR; sounding apologetic; giving only "I wanted a change".

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What do you know about our company and what we do with data?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "do you know us" probes appear in HR rounds in candidate reports (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Preparation and honesty, not a perfect recital.
**Say this in the interview:** "From what I could find, you [what the company does]. I am less sure how the data team works, so I would like to hear more, but I can guess it supports [a reasonable area]."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "You are a big MNC with many employees and good culture." Or inventing: "I know you use machine learning for everything."
**Why it is weak:** Generic, or a made-up claim the interviewer can check in seconds.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I read [source: their website, job post, a news story]. I understand you [business in one line] and serve [customers or markets]. For data, the job post mentions [tools or tasks, e.g. dashboards, SQL, reporting], so I expect the team supports [decisions, e.g. operations or finance]. I do not know the details of your data setup yet, and I would like to ask about it."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Suppose the job post says weekly sales reports and an MIS dashboard. I would say: 'The post mentions weekly reporting, so I assume the team gives managers a regular view of performance. I would like to learn which decisions those reports feed.'"
Say this only if it is true for you. Use only facts you actually found; never guess a product or tool.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: what they do. Reason: where I learned it. Example: what the data team likely does, marked as my reading. Link back: one question I still have.
**If you have no direct experience:** If you found little, say so and lean on the job post. Admitting "this is what I could find" beats a bluff.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Any recent news about us?* Mention one only if you read it. 2) *Which of our products do you like?* Describe one you have actually seen or used.
**Common mistakes:** Memorised Wikipedia lines; wrong facts; bluffing about their tech stack.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why are you moving into this role now, and not earlier in your career?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "why now" appears in career-change question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether there was a real trigger and whether you prepared, not a sudden impulse.
**Say this in the interview:** "I got interested over time, and I wanted to learn properly before I applied. Now I have the basics and a project to show, so this is the right time."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I just realised it recently and thought I would try something new."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds impulsive and unprepared.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I did not know early on how much of my work could be analysis. Over [a true period], [a true trigger: a report I built, a manager's question, a course] showed me I enjoy it. I then spent [months] learning [tools] before applying, because I did not want to apply unprepared. I would rather start now with real skills than keep waiting."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "A manager asked me for a hiring tracker summary. I built a simple pivot table (an Excel summary table) and enjoyed it. Over the next six months I learned SQL basics on weekends and finished a small project. I applied only after that."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: this is the right time. Reason: what triggered it. Example: what I prepared. Link back: I am ready now.
**If you have no direct experience:** Be plain: "Earlier I was building HR experience, and I learned about analytics later. Once I did, I prepared properly."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you regret the delay?* No, my HR years gave me useful business context. 2) *What changed?* Name the true trigger.
**Common mistakes:** Inventing a dramatic story; sounding regretful; saying "everyone is doing it".

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why data analytics and not HR analytics, where your HR background would be a direct advantage?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); gap-fill probe (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you are avoiding an easy route for a good reason, and whether you value your background.
**Say this in the interview:** "I am open to HR analytics, and my background would help there. I want broad analytics skills first, because they work in any function, and then I can bring people topics wherever they matter."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I want to get away from HR completely."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds like you reject your own experience and dislike the field.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "HR analytics is a good path and I respect it. I chose general data analytics because [a true reason: I want to learn from varied business problems such as sales or operations; this role gives wider tools and training]. My HR background still helps: I understand people data and confidentiality. Over time I could well use that in [a people-related area]. Right now I want to build a strong core."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "If I started in HR analytics, I would see mostly people metrics. I would like to see how data works across [finance, operations, customers] too, so my skills are not tied to one team."
Say this only if it is true for you. Do not say you dislike HR analytics if you have not explored it.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: broad core first. Reason: wider learning. Example: what I want to try. Link back: HR knowledge stays an asset.
**If you have no direct experience:** "I have not worked in HR analytics, so I looked at both and chose the broader route because [true reason]."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Would you take an HR analytics role?* Yes, if it is analysis-heavy. 2) *Will you go back to HR?* See Q26.
**Common mistakes:** Running down HR analytics; sounding like you were rejected from it; having no reason beyond "I want a change".

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why should I believe this is a lasting career decision and not a passing trend you will drop in a year?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the "will you stay" concern appears in career-change blogs (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Evidence of commitment: time already invested, not words.
**Say this in the interview:** "I have already put in [months] of effort without anyone asking me to, and I gave up [true trade-off] to do it. That is the best proof I can give."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I promise I will stay, I am very serious about this."
**Why it is weak:** A promise is free; the interviewer needs evidence.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Fair question. Three things I can point to: first, I have studied for [months] on my own, using [tools or courses]. Second, I built [a true project] and I can show it. Third, I accepted [a true trade-off, e.g. a smaller role or less pay] to do this work. Someone chasing a trend does not usually do that. I also see [a long-term path: senior analyst, domain expert] that I want to grow into."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "For the last four months I studied about an hour on weekday evenings and finished two small projects on a public dataset. I did that while working full time."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: this is lasting. Reason: three pieces of proof. Example: the project. Link back: where I want to grow.
**If you have no direct experience:** Do not claim sacrifice you did not make. Offer a plan: "I have started [a small step] and I will complete [a defined milestone] by [date]. Judge me on that."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the market slows?* I would keep building skills; core analysis stays useful. 2) *Where in five years?* See Q9.
**Common mistakes:** Over-promising; overclaiming effort; getting defensive.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why us and not another company hiring analysts in this city?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); gap-fill probe (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a real reason beyond "any job will do".
**Say this in the interview:** "I looked at a few options. What made this one stand out was [a real, specific reason], and I can say why it fits what I want to learn."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Honestly, I am applying to many places. Yours is one of them." Or: "Your salary is better."
**Why it is weak:** It shows no preference and sounds mercenary.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I compared roles on three things: the work, the learning and the team. Here, [a true specific: the job post lists X; the team does Y; training is mentioned]. That fits my goal of [a true goal]. Other places may be good too, but this one matches what I am looking for."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Several postings were only reporting. This one mentions both reporting and some analysis for managers, which is the step I want to take next."
Say this only if it is true for you. Never criticise other companies by name.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: why this one. Reason: three criteria. Example: one concrete match. Link back: what I want to learn.
**If you have no direct experience:** If you know little, say "from the job post I see [one fact]; I would like to learn more in this conversation," and ask a good question.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if another company offers more?* Say what you value beyond pay, honestly. 2) *Are you applying elsewhere?* See Q39.
**Common mistakes:** Criticising competitors; naming only pay or commute; sounding unprepared.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Where do you see yourself in five years?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); appears in career blogs and HR round reports (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Realistic ambition that fits this company, and that you plan to stay.
**Say this in the interview:** "In five years I would like to be a strong, trusted analyst who owns important reports, mentors newer people and understands the business deeply. Whether that is a senior analyst or a team lead, I will see as I grow."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I will be a data scientist or a manager by then." Or: "I have not thought about it."
**Why it is weak:** Too ambitious or empty; both hint at leaving or drifting.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In the first year I want to learn your data and do reliable work. In two to three years, I would like to handle bigger analyses and own a reporting area. By five years, I see myself as a senior analyst people come to for advice, maybe mentoring juniors. I also want to grow in [a true area: SQL depth, statistics, domain]. I would like to do that here, if the role grows."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Year 1: learn the data and automate two manual reports. Years 2 to 3: take on one analysis area. Year 5: senior analyst, helping new joiners."
Say this only if it is true for you. Pick goals you actually want.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: a clear path. Reason: why each step. Example: year 1, 3, 5. Link back: grow at this company.
**If you have no direct experience:** This is about the future, so no experience is needed. Keep it honest and modest.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Management or specialist?* Say what you lean towards, with openness. 2) *What if there is no growth here?* I would discuss it with my manager first.
**Common mistakes:** Naming a title that implies leaving; being vague; saying "your job".

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Was something wrong in your HR job that pushed you out, or are you moving towards something?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "why leave current position" is a standard analyst question (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, maturity, and whether you will speak badly of employers.
**Say this in the interview:** "Mostly moving towards something. I learned a lot in HR and I am grateful for it. I have found work I want to do more of."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "My manager did not value me and the workload was terrible."
**Why it is weak:** Blame sounds like how you will talk about this company later.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "It is mainly a pull. I enjoyed [the analysis part of my work] and want it to be my main job, which my current role cannot offer because [a neutral, true reason: the role is mostly operations]. I am not leaving on bad terms. I can say that honestly: [a true positive about HR, e.g. what you learned]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "My HR role is mostly employee operations. I enjoy the reporting side, but it is perhaps 10 percent of my week. I want a job where it is most of my week."
Say this only if it is true for you. If something real was wrong, say it briefly and neutrally, and never lie. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: towards, not away. Reason: the work I want. Example: a neutral fact about my current role. Link back: gratitude and fit.
**If you have no direct experience:** If there was a real push (a restructure, a stalled role), name it in one calm line, then pivot to what you want.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What did you dislike?* One mild, true point, no blame. 2) *Any conflict?* Only if true; be calm and brief.
**Common mistakes:** Bad-mouthing; lying about a reason the interviewer can verify; saying nothing was ever wrong in an unbelievable way.

### Q11 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What excites you most about working with data?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you enjoy the real work: cleaning, questioning, explaining.
**Say this in the interview:** "I like the moment a messy table turns into a clear answer someone can act on."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I love numbers and I find data very fascinating."
**Why it is weak:** Vague, no example, and could apply to anyone.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "What excites me is [the specific part: finding the cause behind a number; making a messy sheet reliable; showing a manager a simple chart that changes a decision]. For example, [a true small example]. I like that it combines careful checking with explaining things in plain words."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I once compared two headcount reports that disagreed by 12 people. Tracing it to a duplicate employee ID felt satisfying, and the fixed report was trusted afterwards."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: the part I enjoy. Reason: why. Example: one moment. Link back: this role offers more of it.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a practice dataset: "While analysing [a public dataset], the moment I found [a pattern] was my favourite part."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What part do you enjoy least?* See Q16. 2) *Do you prefer cleaning or insights?* Be honest; both matter.
**Common mistakes:** "I love numbers"; sounding rehearsed; focusing on tools rather than answers.

### Q12 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How did you first become interested in data analytics?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); origin-story probe common in career-switch rounds (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Authenticity: a real, simple origin, not a polished invention.
**Say this in the interview:** "It started with a small task at work. I got curious, tried a few things, and one thing led to another."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I read that data analytics is the highest-paying career and decided to try it."
**Why it is weak:** It points to money, not curiosity.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "It began when [a true trigger: a manager asked for a report; I saw a dashboard; a colleague showed me a pivot table]. I tried [a first step] and found [what surprised you]. After that I [a true next step: took a course, practised on data]. Over [time], that curiosity grew into a decision."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "A colleague showed me a pivot table that summarised 500 rows in seconds. I rebuilt my own leave report with it that evening. I was hooked and started learning more the next week."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: the trigger. Task: what I tried. Action: what I did next. Result: how curiosity became a plan.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use the true origin even if small: "I saw a [news chart, a friend's job, a course ad] and wanted to see if I could do it. I tried and enjoyed it." Never invent a dramatic moment.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What did you try first?* Name the real step. 2) *What was hardest early on?* Mention one real hurdle, such as SQL joins.
**Common mistakes:** Making up a "lightbulb moment"; long story; mentioning only pay.

### Q13 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In your own words, what does a data analyst do all day?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a reality-check question for switchers (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand the real job, including the boring parts.
**Say this in the interview:** "Most of the day is getting data, checking it, answering a manager's question, and explaining it. Much less is fancy modelling than people think."

**Explain simply:**
- **Understand the question:** talk to the person asking and agree what decision the numbers will support.
- **Get and clean data:** pull it with SQL or Excel, check for duplicates, blanks and wrong values. This often takes the largest share of time.
- **Analyse:** group, compare, calculate rates, look for patterns.
- **Present:** a chart, a dashboard or a short note in plain words.
- **Maintain:** keep regular reports running and fix them when the data changes.
**Plan / steps:** A sample day (an illustration, not a fixed rule):
1. Check overnight reports and refreshes (about 30 minutes).
2. Clarify a new request with a manager.
3. Pull and clean data for it.
4. Analyse and build a chart.
5. Write two lines of "what it means" and send.
6. Note assumptions and anything to follow up.
**Worked example or script:** "A manager asks why returns went up. I ask which products and which dates. I pull the return data, check there are no duplicate entries, and compare returns by product. Say it shows one product has 5 of 20 returns, 25 percent. I explain that clearly and suggest checking that product's quality."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What share is cleaning?* It varies by team; I expect it to be significant. 2) *Which tools?* Name the ones in the job post you have used.
**Common mistakes:** Describing only dashboards; saying "I would build AI models"; ignoring the stakeholder conversations.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What attracted you to this particular job description?

**What the interviewer is checking:** That you read the job post and matched it to your skills and goals.
**Say this in the interview:** "Three lines in the post stood out: [line 1], [line 2] and [line 3]. Each matches something I have practised or want to learn."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "It looked like a good fit and I meet most of the criteria."

**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "The post mentions [a real task, e.g. weekly MIS reports], which I have done in [a true context]. ### Q15 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What do you expect to learn from us in your first year?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a growth-mindset probe (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you are realistic, eager to learn and know what you do not yet know.
**Say this in the interview:** "In year one I expect to learn your data, your business and how your team works, and to get sharper on [tools]. I want to be useful quickly and reliable over time."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I expect to become a senior analyst and learn machine learning in a year."
**Why it is weak:** Unrealistic, and skips the basics of the business.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "First, your business and data: where it comes from and what the numbers mean. Second, how the team works: how requests arrive, how reports are checked. Third, skills: I want to go deeper on [SQL, a BI tool, statistics] from experienced colleagues. By the end of the year I would like to own a couple of reports end to end and make them more reliable."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "In the first three months I would learn the data and the existing reports. By six months I would take one report over fully. By twelve months I would suggest one improvement, such as automating a manual step."
Say this only if it is true for you. Pick learning goals you really hold.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: learn the business and craft. Reason: why this order. Example: a 3, 6, 12 month view. Link back: contribution to the team.
**If you have no direct experience:** This is future-looking, so none is needed. Keep it modest and specific.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What will you do if you struggle?* Ask early and show my attempt. 2) *What do you need from your manager?* Clear priorities and regular feedback.
**Common mistakes:** Overpromising; only technical goals; sounding passive.

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What do you like least about data work, or expect to find hardest?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "weakness" style questions appear in prep-site analyst lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you are realistic about the job, honest, and able to name a real difficulty without it being a deal-breaker.
**Say this in the interview:** Name one real, ordinary difficulty (messy data is the safest true one), say what you do about it, and show you still like the work.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Nothing really, I love everything about data." Or: "I hate cleaning data, it is boring."
**Why it is weak:** The first sounds untrue or naive; the second names the core of the job as a dislike.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "The part I expect to be hardest is [cleaning and checking messy data / waiting for data owners / explaining numbers to people who want a quick yes or no]. It takes patience, and it is easy to rush. What I do is [make a short checklist: check blanks, duplicates and totals before I trust anything].
