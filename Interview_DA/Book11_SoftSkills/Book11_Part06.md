### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you found an error in a report or file after you had already shared it. What did you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "error already shared with stakeholders" appears on prep question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you tell people quickly, fix it, and stop it happening again, instead of hiding it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I told the people who had the file the same day, said what was wrong and what the right number was, and then added a check so it could not happen again."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I quietly fixed it and sent the new file. I did not want to make a big deal of it."
**Why it is weak:** Silent fixes leave people using the old number and show you hide mistakes.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I had sent [report or file] to [who]. On [day] I noticed [what was wrong, for example a filter missed some rows]. First I checked how big the problem was: [how many rows or what size of difference]. Then I told [who] straight away: what was wrong, what the correct figure is, and that the corrected file was attached. I also asked them to delete the old one. After that I added [a check, for example a total I compare with the source] so it would be caught before sending. The correction took [time]. [Say 'nobody acted on the wrong number' only if true.]"
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A monthly headcount file went to three managers. Next morning I saw 6 of 240 rows (2.5%) were left out because of a filter on a date column. I emailed all three within the hour: "The headcount in yesterday's file is 6 too low. Corrected file attached; please use only this one." Then I added a step that compares my total with the HRMS (the HR software system) total before I send.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: report already sent, error found later. Task: limit damage and fix it. Action: size the error, tell recipients at once with the right number, replace the file, add a check. Result: no wrong decision made, and a check now exists (state it only if true).
**If you have no direct experience:** Say: "I do not have an exact case of a shared file. The closest is [an error I caught in my own work before sending]. For a shared one I would check the size, tell everyone who has it the same day, send the corrected version, and add a check." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use this no-experience version.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Did you tell your manager first?* Yes, a short heads-up first, then the wider message. 2) *What if no one had noticed?* I would still tell them; they might decide on it later.
**Common mistakes:** Hiding it; blaming the data source; no fix for next time; inventing a heroic result.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you had to learn something new quickly to finish a task.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); learning-agility prompts are common on question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you learn in a sensible order, use the right help, and still deliver.
**Say this in the interview:** "I worked out the smallest amount I needed to learn, found a trusted source, practised on a small sample, and checked my result before I used it."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am a fast learner. I just watched some videos and figured it out."
**Why it is weak:** No detail, no proof, no checking.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I needed to [task] by [deadline], and I had never used [tool or skill]. I first listed what I really needed, which was only [two or three things]. I learned those from [a colleague, official help, a short course], and practised on [small sample]. Then I checked my result against [a known answer]. I finished by [date]. Afterwards I wrote [a short note] so the next person could do it faster."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** I had to match 300 attendance rows to payroll and had only used basic Excel. I learned lookups (a formula that pulls matching values from another list) in one afternoon, tried them on 20 rows where I knew the answer, found 2 mismatches caused by extra spaces, fixed them, then ran all 300. I saved a two-line note on the steps.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: new task, unfamiliar tool. Task: finish by the deadline. Action: narrow what to learn, learn, test small, verify. Result: delivered on time, with a note left behind (only if true).
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a course or personal project: "I taught myself [SQL basics] for [a practice project] and checked my answers against known results." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version: describe exactly how you would learn a tool for a task, step by step.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How did you know you had learned enough?* The test sample matched a known answer. 2) *What if no one could help?* Official help and a small practice file.
**Common mistakes:** "I am a quick learner" with no example; claiming expert level after a day; skipping the checking step.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What does ownership mean to you at work, and can you give an example?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); Glassdoor snippets say managerial rounds probe ownership (anonymous, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you see your work through to the end, including the unglamorous parts.
**Say this in the interview:** "Ownership means the result is my job until it is truly done, not only my part of it. If something goes wrong, I say so and fix it."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I always do my work on time and never need reminders."
**Why it is weak:** It is about discipline, not about owning an outcome or a problem.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "To me ownership is three things: I own the result, not only my task; I speak up early when something is at risk; and I follow up after delivery. For example, [situation] was my responsibility. I [action that went past the minimum]. When [problem] came up I [told people and fixed it]. The outcome was [result]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** I prepared the monthly leave report. Beyond sending it, I checked with two managers whether it answered their question. One needed it by department, so I added that column and made it the standard version.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: ownership is about outcomes. Reason: three habits. Example: one real case. Link back: "In this role I would own the numbers I publish."
**If you have no direct experience:** "A time I felt real ownership was [a task, even outside work]. Here is what it showed me about how I would work as an analyst." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, give the definition and how you would apply it to a report you own.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Where is the line with other people's work?* I flag it to the owner, not take it over. 2) *Is ownership blaming yourself?* No, it is fixing and learning.
**Common mistakes:** Only defining it; saying "we" throughout; choosing a story where you simply obeyed instructions.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you solved a problem that was not clearly yours to solve.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Initiative with good manners: did you help without stepping on others?
**Say this in the interview:** "I noticed a problem nobody owned, checked with the right person that I could help, solved it, and handed it back."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Nobody else was doing it, so I just took it over and fixed it my way."
**Why it is weak:** It ignores the owner and the risk of changing things without asking.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I noticed [problem] in [area], which belonged to [team or person]. I asked [owner] if it was okay if I looked into it. I [what you did] in [time], without touching their data or process. I shared [findings or fix] with them and they decided [outcome]. It saved [rough time or effort]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** The onboarding checklist kept missing laptop requests. IT owned laptops. I asked the IT contact if I could add a reminder step on the HR side. I did, and told IT about it. Over the next month no new joiner waited for a laptop (only if true).
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: unowned problem. Task: help without overstepping. Action: ask, solve in scope, hand back. Result: problem fixed, owner informed.
**If you have no direct experience:** "I have not had a perfect case. Here is how I would handle it: confirm who owns it, offer help, stay inside my access, and report back." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the owner said no?* I would respect it and share what I saw. 2) *Did it affect your own work?* Say honestly how you managed the time.
**Common mistakes:** Taking over; not telling the owner; making yourself the hero.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you missed a deadline. What happened and what did you change afterwards?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); missed-deadline prompts appear on prep banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, early warning, and a real change.
**Say this in the interview:** "I missed it, I told the person as soon as I saw the risk, and I now do [specific change] so it does not repeat."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I never miss deadlines." Or: "It was late because another team did not send their input."
**Why it is weak:** One sounds untrue; the other blames others and shows no lesson.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I committed to [task] by [date]. I underestimated [the real cause, such as data cleaning]. I realised on [day] and told [person] straight away, with a new date. I delivered on [date], which was [X] late. Since then I [change, for example add buffer, break tasks into smaller pieces, confirm inputs earlier]. My last [few deliveries] have been on time."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** I promised a 5-day attrition summary and delivered 2 days late (40% over my estimate) because exit data had many blanks. I told my manager on day 3 with a new date. Now I check data quality on day 1 before I give a date.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: promise made. Task: deliver. Action: flag risk, give new date, deliver. Result: late but honest; new habit in place.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a smaller miss (study, project). Never say "I have never missed one." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use a smaller real miss or answer as "how I would handle it: warn early, offer options, deliver partial first."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who was affected?* Be specific and honest. 2) *How do you estimate now?* Add buffer, check data first.
**Common mistakes:** Blame; no lesson; claiming perfection; picking a trivial example.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a difficult problem you solved at work and how you approached it.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "describe a data problem that challenged you" appears in DataCamp and Coursera guides (snippet only).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Your thinking order: understand, break down, test, check.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will walk through what the problem was, how I broke it into smaller parts, what I tried, and how I knew the answer was right."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "It was a very hard problem but I worked hard and solved it."
**Why it is weak:** No method visible.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "The problem was [what, and why it mattered]. I started by [asking or checking basics]. Then I split it into [parts]. I tested [most likely cause first] and found [what]. I confirmed the fix by [check]. The result was [outcome, only true numbers]. What I learned was [one thing]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Monthly headcount differed by 8 from Finance (1,196 vs 1,204, about 0.66%). I compared the two lists by employee ID, found 8 joiners entered after my cut-off date, agreed one cut-off with Finance, and both numbers matched next month.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: mismatch. Task: find the cause. Action: compare by ID, find pattern, agree rule. Result: numbers matched.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a project or study problem, labelled as such. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version: explain a problem-solving method you would use.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the first idea failed?* Say what you tried next. 2) *What would you do differently?* One honest improvement.
**Common mistakes:** Jargon; skipping the checking; picking a problem with no obstacle.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you approach a problem when you do not know the answer?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a calm, repeatable method and know when to ask.
**Say this in the interview:** "I make sure I understand the question, break it into parts, find what I can check, try the simplest idea first, and ask for help when I am stuck for too long."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I would Google it or ask someone."
**Why it is weak:** Shows no thinking of your own.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "First I restate the problem to be sure I understand it. Then I split it into smaller parts and see which part I can answer. I check whether similar work exists, such as an old report or a colleague's notes. I try the simplest explanation first. If I am still stuck after about [time], I ask, and I bring what I tried so the person can help quickly."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A leave balance looked odd. I restated the question: "Which employees differ from the HRMS?" I checked one employee by hand, found the issue was half-day leaves counted as full days, then checked all rows for the same rule.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: method. Reason: steps reduce panic and wasted time. Example: one short case. Link back: how this helps in analytics.
**If you have no direct experience:** The method itself is the answer; add a small real example only if you have one. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, give the method and say you would practise it on small tasks.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long before you ask?* A set limit; 30 to 60 minutes depending on urgency is my suggestion, not a rule. 2) *What do you bring when asking?* What I tried and what I think.
**Common mistakes:** Panic; silently struggling; asking with no effort shown.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You have never worked in analytics. How will you learn the tools and processes of this team in your first 30 days?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); career-switch roles often get this kind of question (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A realistic, specific plan, not "I will learn fast".
**Say this in the interview:** "Week one I listen and map how work flows; weeks two to three I take small tasks under review; by day 30 I own one small report."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am a fast learner, so I will pick everything up quickly."
**Why it is weak:** No plan.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Week 1: meet the team, ask for the list of reports, tools, and data sources, and read existing documentation. Week 2: shadow a report from request to delivery and practise in a safe copy. Week 3: take a small task, with review. Week 4: own one small recurring report, and write down what I learned. I would ask my manager to check progress at day 15 and day 30. My HR background already helps with [people data, confidentiality, how HR asks questions], so I can focus on the tools."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** This is a plan, not a story, so there is nothing to invent. If you have already started learning, add: "I have already practised [a tool] on [a real practice project]." Say this only if it is true for you.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Week 1 learn, Week 2 shadow, Week 3 do with review, Week 4 own and document, link back to HR strengths.
**If you have no direct experience:** This question assumes none, so be honest and show the plan. Do not claim tools you have not used.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the tool is new to you?* Official training, a test copy, ask a buddy. 2) *How will I know you are on track?* Agreed check-ins and one finished small task.
**Common mistakes:** Over-promising ("I will be fully productive in a week"); no mention of asking for feedback; hiding the switch.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you were given a task with unclear instructions. How did you proceed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ambiguity prompts appear on TryExponent and PracHub (snippet only, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you clarify and make assumptions visible, not guess in silence.
**Say this in the interview:** "I asked a few short questions, wrote my assumptions down, and showed an early draft before building the whole thing."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I did what I thought they wanted and sent it."
**Why it is weak:** Risks rework and shows no communication.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I was asked to [vague task]. I asked [two or three questions: purpose, scope, deadline]. Where the answers were still unclear, I wrote down my assumptions, such as [example], and shared them. I sent a quick draft to check direction. [Person] corrected [one thing] early, so I did not waste time. I delivered [result]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** My manager said "get me attrition numbers." I asked: which period, and for what meeting? She said for the quarterly review. I assumed voluntary exits only, wrote that on the first page, and she confirmed. It saved me redoing a full-year file.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: vague ask. Task: deliver the right thing. Action: clarify, state assumptions, draft early. Result: no rework (only if true).
**If you have no direct experience:** Say how you would do it, with the same steps. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the manager is unavailable?* Write assumptions and proceed with a label. 2) *Did you ever guess wrong?* Be honest.
**Common mistakes:** Asking too many questions at once; not stating assumptions; waiting too long for clarity.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you took initiative without being asked.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you notice and act sensibly, and keep others informed.
**Say this in the interview:** "I noticed [problem], told my manager what I planned to do, did a small version first, and shared the result."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I always do more than asked." (no example)
**Why it is weak:** No proof.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I noticed that [repeated problem or gap]. Nobody had asked me to look at it, but it was taking [time or causing errors]. I told [manager] my idea, and built [small fix] in my own time or after my main work. I showed it to [users], collected feedback, and [outcome]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Every month I rebuilt the same joiners list by hand (about 2 hours). I made a simple template with fixed formulas, tested it for one month against the manual version, and it matched.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: gap noticed. Task: improve it unasked. Action: propose, build small, test. Result: time saved or errors reduced (only true figures).
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a non-work example (volunteering, study). Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, say what you would look for in your first month.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Did you ask permission?* Yes, I informed my manager. 2) *Did it work?* Be honest.
**Common mistakes:** Acting without telling anyone; ignoring your main work; overclaiming results.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you worked under a tight deadline with several tasks competing for your time.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** How you prioritise and communicate when time is short.
**Say this in the interview:** "I listed everything with its deadline and effort, did the most urgent and important first, and told people early what would be late."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I worked late and did everything."
**Why it is weak:** Hard work alone is not a plan, and it hides the problem.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I had [number] tasks due [when]: [list]. I estimated each at about [hours] and saw that I had [available] hours. I did [most urgent] first, asked [person] if [lower priority task] could move, and told [person] clearly what would arrive late. I delivered [what and when]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** I had three tasks needing 3, 2 and 1.5 hours (6.5 hours in total) with only 5 hours free. I did the manager's 3-hour report first, shared the 1.5-hour clean-up with a colleague, and asked the requester of the 2-hour task for the next morning.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: more work than time. Task: deliver what matters. Action: list, estimate, order, negotiate. Result: key work on time, others re-planned.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a study or personal example, or describe your method. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you decide priority?* Impact and deadline. 2) *What if everything is urgent?* Ask the manager to rank.
**Common mistakes:** Silent overwork; no negotiation; no mention of what was dropped.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Your report total does not match the number Finance has. What do you do, and who do you tell, and when?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); report-vs-Finance mismatch is a scenario on prep sites such as Hyring (snippet only, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Calm investigation, early communication, and no blame.
**Say this in the interview:** "First I check my own work, then compare with Finance row by row, and I tell my manager early with what I know so far."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Finance is probably wrong; I would ask them to check."
**Why it is weak:** Assumes the other side is wrong before checking.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Step 1: I confirm I am comparing like with like: the same period, definition and cut-off date. Step 2: I check the size of the gap and compare by ID or category to find where it comes from. Step 3: I tell my manager within the day: 'Gap is [size], I am checking [likely cause], update by [time].' Step 4: I talk to Finance, asking how they define it. Step 5: I agree one definition in writing and fix the report."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** An exercise: my headcount is 1,196 and Finance has 1,204; the gap is 8, about 0.66%. Comparing by employee ID shows 8 joiners dated after my cut-off. I tell my manager, then agree the cut-off with Finance.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: check, locate, tell, agree. Reason: mismatches are usually definitions. Example: the 8-person gap. Link back: trust in numbers.
**If you have no direct experience:** This is a situational question; the steps are the answer. If you add a real case, say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, give the steps.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When do you tell the manager?* As soon as I know it is real, not after solving it. 2) *What if I was wrong?* Fix it, tell everyone who used it.
**Common mistakes:** Blaming Finance; waiting until sure; not agreeing a single definition afterwards.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time a process you owned went wrong. Who was affected and how did you fix it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Ownership under pressure: impact, fix, prevention.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will say who was affected, how fast I told them, what I did to fix it, and what I changed so it would not repeat."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "There was a small issue but it was not really my fault."
**Why it is weak:** Dodges ownership.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I owned [process]. It went wrong because [honest cause]. [Group] was affected: [number or description]. I informed [person] on the same day, [fixed the immediate issue], and told those affected what had happened. Afterwards I changed [specific step] so it could not happen the same way."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A payroll input file I prepared had a wrong allowance code for 18 of 360 employees (5%). I told my manager and payroll that day, payroll corrected the next run, and I added a step that checks codes against the master list before sending.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: own process failed. Task: contain and fix. Action: tell, correct, prevent. Result: affected people sorted out, check added (only if true).
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a smaller case, or describe how you would respond. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How did you apologise?* Plainly, briefly. 2) *What check did you add?* A concrete one.
**Common mistakes:** Shifting blame; vague fix; no prevention.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you make sure the numbers in your work are correct before you send them?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A real checking habit, not "I am careful".
**Say this in the interview:** "I have a short checklist: reconcile the total to the source (check that my total equals the original record's total), check counts and blanks, test a few rows by hand, and ask whether the number makes sense."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I double-check everything carefully."
**Why it is weak:** No method.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Before I send anything I do four checks. One: does my total match the source or a previously published number? Two: do counts, blanks, and duplicates look right? Three: I pick [a few] rows and verify them by hand. Four: I ask whether the result makes sense, for example a sudden jump. If I can, I ask a colleague to look over anything important. I note my definitions on the file."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Before sending a leave report: total employees match HRMS count (480), no blank department, five random employees verified by hand, and a sentence on the page stating the cut-off date.
**STAR breakdown:** Point: a repeatable checklist. Reason: catches the common errors. Example: the leave report checks. Link back: trusted numbers.
**If you have no direct experience:** Present the checklist as your method and say where you have practised it. Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, say you follow this checklist on practice projects.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you have no time?* Do the total reconciliation and a few spot checks at minimum. 2) *Have these checks ever caught something?* Only say yes if true.
**Common mistakes:** Vague carefulness; checking only formulas not logic; no definitions written.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What would you do if you realised on Friday evening that Thursday's report you sent to the leadership had a wrong figure?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Speed, honesty, and judgement about who to tell and how.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not wait until Monday. I would confirm the error, tell my manager tonight, and send a short correction to the leadership with the right figure."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I would wait until Monday and fix it quietly."
**Why it is weak:** Leaders may act on the wrong number over the weekend.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "First I confirm the error and work out the correct figure and its size. Then I message my manager right away, with what is wrong, what it affects, and my proposed correction. With their agreement, or if they cannot be reached and the risk is real, I send a short note to the same recipients: 'The figure in Thursday's report was [wrong]. The correct figure is [right]. **What the interviewer is checking:** A calm, clear problem-solving process, not a clever answer. (Detailed analysis methods stay in Book 8; this is the behavioural version.)
**Say this in the interview:** "I clarify the real question, check what data I have, break it into parts, test small, and tell the person early what I find and what I need."

**Explain simply:**
- A new problem is not an emergency; it is a few smaller questions.
- First understand, then plan, then do, then check, and keep the person informed.
- Say what you do not know instead of pretending.

**Plan / steps:**
1. Clarify: what is the problem, who needs the answer, by when, and what decision depends on it?
2. Check what is available: data, past reports, who owns the source.
3. Break it into smaller questions and pick the first one to test.
4. Try a quick version on a small sample.
5. Check the result against common sense and a known number.
6. Share early (a draft or a status), ask for feedback, then finish and write what you did.

**Worked example or script:** "Say a manager asks why overtime cost rose (invented). I would ask: which months, which sites, what is the decision? Then I would check the payroll and attendance files, split overtime by site and month, test one site first, check the total against payroll, and show a draft: 'Most of the rise is in one site; I need the shift roster to confirm why.'"
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below: present the steps as your method, not as past events.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- What if you are not sure where to start? "I start with the data owner and the smallest testable question."
- When do you tell the requester? "Early, with a short update on what I have found and what I need."

**Common mistakes:** Jumping into the data without asking the real question; going silent until the deadline; no check of results; presenting guesses as findings.

### Q78 ⭐ 🔴 What would you do if you were asked to own a dashboard in a tool you have never used, with a two-week deadline?

- Agree the scope and a review date early.

2. Days 1 to 2: learn the basics (official tutorials, a colleague who knows the tool).
