### Q29 ⭐⭐ 🔴 The interviewer asks a question, you answer, and they say, "Wrong," without giving a reason. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "stay calm when challenged" is a common theme in stress-interview guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you stay calm, think again honestly, and neither collapse nor argue.
**Say this in the interview:** "Pause, re-check your reasoning out loud, ask what they have in mind, and either correct yourself or hold your answer politely with a reason."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Oh, sorry, then the answer is the opposite," said at once. Or: "No, I am right," said sharply.
**Why it is weak:** The first shows you have no belief in your own reasoning; the second shows you cannot take a challenge.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Let me re-check that. I said [your answer] because [your reasoning in one line]. Is there something in the question I have missed, for example [a condition, a data detail]? If you are thinking of [other answer], I can see that works when [condition]. If I have made a mistake, I would like to find it, so let me walk through it once more." Then re-trace slowly. If you find the error, say "You are right, I missed [step]; the correct answer is [x]." If you still believe it, say "I still get [x] for this reason; I may be missing context, so happy to be corrected."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Asked "How many rows will a left join return?" you say "same as the left table." The interviewer says "Wrong." You say: "If the right table has duplicate keys, a left join can return more rows than the left table. Is that the case you mean?" The interviewer says yes. You reply: "Then I should have said that first; thanks." (Invented example, for illustration only.)
**STAR breakdown:** Point (pause and re-check) then Reason (it shows reasoning, not ego) then Example (re-trace out loud) then Link back to the role (analysts get challenged by stakeholders on numbers).
**If you have no direct experience:** Say honestly: "I have not been in this exact moment, but this is how I would handle it," and give the steps. If you have a true example of a manager questioning your numbers and you checking them, use it. Say this only if it is true for you.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *So are you sure or not?* Give a level of confidence in plain words ("fairly sure, about 80 percent") and say how you would verify. 2) *What if they will not explain?* Say, "Could you give me a hint? I would like to learn the right answer."
**Common mistakes:** Changing the answer instantly to please; arguing; going silent; getting visibly upset.

### Q30 ⭐⭐ 🔴 You freeze in a live technical round and the interviewer says time is running out. How do you salvage the round?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); blanking and recovery advice appears in recruiter and career guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can still show partial work, prioritise under pressure, and communicate clearly.
**Say this in the interview:** "Say what I have so far, give the simplest version that works, and explain how I would finish with more time."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** Staying silent, or saying "I cannot do this, sorry."
**Why it is weak:** Silence gives the interviewer nothing to score; giving up ends the round.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Let me take ten seconds and write down what I know. The goal is [restate it]. I will start with a simple version: [first small step, for example, count rows, or filter one month]. That already gives [partial result]. With more time I would [next step], and I would check it by [a quick test]. Is it better if I finish this part or explain the full approach?" Asking this hands the interviewer a choice and shows you can prioritise.
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** On a "rank employees by salary within each department" task, you freeze on the window function. You say: "I will first sort by department and salary to see the pattern; then I would number the rows within each department, which is what a ranking function does, and I would check the first row of each group." You may not have written the exact syntax, but the logic is visible. (Invented example, for illustration only.)
**STAR breakdown:** Point (show partial work) then Reason (interviewers score thinking, not only the final answer) then Example (the simple version first) then Link back to the role (analysts often deliver an 80 percent answer on deadline and say what is left).
**If you have no direct experience:** Say: "I have not frozen in a live round yet, so I practise this: timed mock questions where I speak while solving." Say this only if it is true for you; otherwise describe your practice plan.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would you do with 10 more minutes?* Name the next two steps and one check. 2) *What if you cannot start at all?* Restate the problem, ask one clarifying question, write the input and the expected output.
**Common mistakes:** Going silent; apologising repeatedly; trying the hardest version first; ignoring the clock.

### Q31 ⭐ 🟢 What do you do with your hands, eyes and posture in a video interview, and how do you look at the camera and the screen?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); virtual interview tips appear in India and data-career blogs (unverified, snippet level).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you come across as attentive, comfortable and professional on video.
**Say this in the interview:** "This is rarely asked aloud; it is a how-to for you. Sit upright, keep the camera at eye level, look at the lens when speaking, and keep your hands calm."
**Explain simply:**
- Eyes: when you speak, look at the camera lens now and then, not only at the face on screen. When you listen, looking at the screen is fine.
- Posture: sit upright, shoulders relaxed, a hand's width of space above your head in the frame.
- Hands: keep them visible or resting on the desk; small natural gestures are fine, avoid tapping or touching your face.
- Notes: a small sticky note near the lens with 3 keywords is better than a script you read.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Raise the laptop on books so the camera is at eye level.
2. Put a small sticker beside the lens as a reminder.
3. Do a one-minute recorded practice and watch it with the sound off.
4. Smile when you greet, and nod slightly while listening.
5. Breathe out slowly before you start.
**Worked example or script:** If you realise you were looking at your own face, do not comment on it; just look at the lens at the next sentence. If you look down at notes, say "let me check my notes for the exact figure" and then look up again.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you wear glasses and see a glare?* Move the light source to the side and test beforehand. 2) *What if you move a lot?* Sit on a stable chair and plant your feet.
**Common mistakes:** Staring at your own video; reading a script; slouching; typing during questions; fidgeting with a pen.

### Q32 ⭐ 🟢 How do you prepare the night before and the morning of an interview to reduce nerves?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); nerve-management advice comes from university and media career guides (unverified, snippet level).
**What the interviewer is checking:** This is a self-preparation question, not usually asked aloud; it helps you arrive calm, and may appear as "how do you handle pressure".
**Say this in the interview:** "If asked, I say I prepare in a fixed routine: practice, logistics ready, sleep, and a short calm routine before I start."
**Explain simply:**
- Nerves are normal; a routine turns uncertainty into small checklist tasks.
- The night before is for logistics and light review, not new topics.
- The morning is for settling the body and mind.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Night before: re-read the job description; review your 60 to 90 second introduction and 3 stories; do not learn new tools.
2. Lay out clothes, ID, charger, notebook, water; test link, camera and microphone (see Q14).
3. Write 3 to 5 questions to ask the interviewer.
4. Sleep at a normal time; avoid a late-night cram.
5. Morning: light meal, water, a short walk or stretch, slow breathing (in for four counts, out for six).
6. Read your one-page notes once; then put them away.
7. Join 5 to 10 minutes early; say your first line aloud once.
**Worked example or script:** If asked "How do you handle pressure?", say: "Before big days I use a simple routine: I prepare the night before, sleep, and take a short walk. During the interview if I feel stuck I pause and breathe." Use this only if it is true for you; otherwise say the routine you plan to practise.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you cannot sleep?* Rest anyway; one short night rarely ruins an interview; keep the morning routine. 2) *Coffee?* Keep your normal amount; extra can raise jitters.
**Common mistakes:** Last-minute cramming; new clothes or a new route; skipping food; checking the link at the last minute.

### Q34 ⭐ 🟢 After an interview where you blanked on two questions, how do you reflect and what do you do before the next interview?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); post-interview reflection is common advice in career guides (unverified, snippet level).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Mostly self-improvement for you; in an interview it may be asked as "tell me about a time you failed".
**Say this in the interview:** "I note what happened, find the gap, practise that topic, and rehearse out loud before the next one."
**Explain simply:**
- Blanking is common; one bad answer is not the whole interview.
- Write the questions within an hour while you remember them.
- Separate the cause: knowledge gap, nerves, or unclear question.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Within an hour: write the two questions and what you said.
2. Look up the correct answers; write them in your own words.
3. Label the cause: gap (study it), nerves (practise a calm routine), or unclear question (practise clarifying).
4. Do 3 mock questions out loud on the same topic this week.
5. Add a "pause line" to your toolkit: "Let me think for a moment."
6. Keep a short log of questions after each interview.
**Worked example or script:** Illustrative example, replace with your own true story: "I blanked on a question about handling duplicates in a table. After the interview I looked it up, wrote a three-line answer, and practised it. In the next interview a similar question came and I answered it." Say this only if it is true for you; use the plan above if it is not.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Should you email the interviewer?* A short thank-you is fine (see Q37). 2) *How do you avoid blanking again?* Practise speaking answers aloud, not only reading them.
**Common mistakes:** Replaying it for days; blaming only nerves; not writing the questions down; skipping the study step.

### Q37 ⭐ 🟡 You sent a thank-you email after the interview. What should it say, and should you mention a question you answered badly?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); thank-you notes are standard career-guide advice (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Not usually asked aloud; the email itself shows professionalism and interest.
**Say this in the interview:** "Keep it short and warm: thank them, mention one specific thing from the conversation, restate interest, and fix a weak answer only if it is a small, clear correction."
**Explain simply:**
- Send within 24 hours, three to five lines.
- Mention one real topic you discussed, so it does not look copied.
- Correcting an earlier answer is fine if short and factual; do not apologise at length.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Subject: "Thank you, [role] interview on [date]".
2. Line 1: thanks for their time.
3. Line 2: one specific point you enjoyed, for example "the discussion on how the team tracks attrition".
4. Line 3 (optional): a brief correction.
5. Line 4: restate interest and fit.
6. Close politely; check names and spelling.
**Worked example or script:**
"Dear [Name], thank you for your time today. I enjoyed our discussion on [topic]. One small addition on the question about [topic]: after thinking again, [one-sentence correct answer]. I remain very interested in the role and in contributing with my HR background and analytics skills. Regards, [your name]."
The correction is one sentence, calm, with no long apology.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you hear nothing?* Follow up politely after about a week (a common guideline, unverified; follow the timeline they gave). 2) *Should every panellist get an email?* One email to the main contact, addressing the panel, is usually enough.
**Common mistakes:** A long apology; copying a template with the wrong name; correcting several answers; sending nothing at all.
