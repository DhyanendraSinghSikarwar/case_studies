### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is a group discussion round, and what are the evaluators actually scoring?
**Asked at:** commonly asked as a concept question. GD reports for analytics roles are mostly campus or early-career (candidate reports compiled in research/interview_processes.md Section 2.5, anecdotal, unverified); no report found for lateral hires.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know what the round is and that it is about how you behave in a group, not who "wins".
**Say this in the interview:** "A GD is a short, observed conversation where a group discusses a topic. Observers watch how clearly we think, how we listen, and how we work with others."
**Explain simply:**
- A group of about 5 to 12 people gets a topic or a case and 10 to 20 minutes. Observers watch silently and score each person.
- Criteria named in coaching guides (from memory, unverified, firms differ): clear communication, structured thinking, knowledge or reasoning, listening and teamwork, and leadership or initiative.
- It is not a debate to win. Calm, short, relevant points that build on others usually score better than loud ones.
- Honest note: GDs are mostly a campus and fresher filter. Lateral hires seldom get one, so ask the recruiter.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Learn the five criteria above.
2. Practise one point at a time: claim, reason, example.
3. Practise listening: repeat the last speaker's point in one line before adding yours.
**Worked example or script:** "I would like to build on Priya's point about cost. Cost matters, and the second angle is employee productivity." This one line shows listening, structure and teamwork together.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who scores better, the first or the loudest speaker?* Neither by default; quality and fit matter more. 2) *Is there a right answer?* Usually no; the process is judged.
**Common mistakes:** Treating it as a debate to win; talking only to impress; ignoring quiet members.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 The panel gives your group of 8 a topic and 15 minutes. How do you decide when and how to enter the discussion?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can start contributing early without grabbing the floor.
**Say this in the interview:** "I take about a minute to jot my points, listen to the first speakers, and enter at the first natural pause with a short, useful point that links to what was said."
**Explain simply:**
- Enter early enough to be seen (in a 15-minute GD, ideally within the first 3 to 4 minutes) but not by cutting someone off.
- Good entry moments: a pause, a point you can build on, a point you can clarify, or a topic that has drifted.
- Enter with a link: "Building on that..." is safer than a fresh monologue.
**Plan / steps:**
1. First minute: write 3 points (for, against, example).
2. Listen for the first two speakers' angles and note gaps.
3. Wait for a pause or a hand gesture; say a short opener.
4. Make one point in about 30 seconds.
5. Return every few minutes with a second, third point.
**Worked example or script:** "Sorry to add here, building on Ravi's point about reach: a gap I see is the cost side, and I would like to add one example." (Pause rule: wait about two seconds of silence, do not talk over anyone.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if nobody pauses?* Use a small hand raise and a polite "May I add something?". 2) *What if all my points were taken?* Agree briefly and add a new angle or example.
**Common mistakes:** Waiting so long you never speak; shouting over others; entering with a point unrelated to the flow.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you open a group discussion, and why do many guides warn against opening with a firm stand?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). The warning against a firm opening stand appears in coaching guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can start with structure and still stay open to the group.
**Say this in the interview:** "I open by defining the topic and listing the angles we could cover, then invite others. I avoid a hard 'I am fully for this' stand because it can lock me in and sound closed."
**Explain simply:**
- A first speaker gets noticed, but a weak or extreme opening is noticed more.
- A firm stand early invites attack, and if it is challenged you may look defensive or have to flip (see Q23).
- A framing opening shows structure: meaning of the topic, two or three angles, and a suggestion for how to proceed.
- Open only if you are ready. A good second or third entry beats a poor first one.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Define the key word in one line.
2. Name two or three angles (for example people, cost, risk).
3. Give a light lean at most ("on balance I lean towards..., but let us see").
4. Invite others in.
**Worked example or script:** Topic: "Work from home". "Let me suggest we first agree what we mean: working from home most days. We could look at three angles: productivity, team culture and cost. I would like to hear everyone's view on each."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is opening mandatory?* No. 2) *What if someone else frames it first?* Say you agree with the structure and add a missing angle.
**Common mistakes:** Opening with a loud absolute view; a long definition; memorised statistics as an opener.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Two candidates keep interrupting each other and the discussion is turning into a shouting match. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked as a situational GD question (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Calm, teamwork and the ability to steer without taking sides.
**Say this in the interview:** "I step in calmly, acknowledge both views in one line, suggest we hear one person at a time, and bring the group back to the topic."
**Explain simply:**
- Do not join the shouting, and do not scold anyone.
- Name what is common between the two views; this lowers the heat.
- Offer a simple process: one speaks, the other responds, then the rest of us add.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Wait for a half second of breath, raise a hand, use a calm voice.
2. Summarise both sides in one sentence each.
3. Propose an order and a focus question.
4. Invite a third person to add a new angle.
**Worked example or script:** "Let me pause us for a moment. Both Amit and Sara agree the cost matters; they differ on timing. Maybe Amit finishes his point, then Sara responds, and then the rest of us share how we see timing."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they ignore you?* Repeat once calmly, then continue with your own point; do not escalate. 2) *Does this count as leadership?* It can, if done politely and briefly.
**Common mistakes:** Shouting back; taking a side; lecturing the group on manners.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you conclude or summarise a group discussion, and why should the summary reflect the group and not only your view?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Guides advise summarising what the group covered (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can listen, structure and represent others fairly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I give a 20 to 30 second wrap-up: the main points on each side, where we agree, and one closing line. I do not add a brand new argument."
**Explain simply:**
- A summary that reflects the group shows listening and fairness. A summary of only your own view looks like self-promotion.
- Use others' names for credit.
- Conclude only if time is near the end or the group is stuck; if the panel says "time up", keep it very short.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Point 1 raised for; point 2 raised against.
2. Where we agree.
3. Open item or balanced recommendation.
4. Stop.
**Worked example or script:** "To summarise: Neha and Karan highlighted productivity; Ravi and Meera raised the culture risk. We seem to agree a hybrid pattern could work, with clear team days. That could be our recommendation."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if I am not the last speaker?* Add a brief summary if there is time, or none. 2) *Can I give my view?* Yes, in one line, labelled as yours.
**Common mistakes:** Re-arguing your own point; missing the quiet member's input; summarising too long.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Is it better to speak first, speak most, or speak well? How many times should you speak in a 15-minute GD?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). No source gives an official speaking count.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand quality beats quantity.
**Say this in the interview:** "Speaking well matters most. I aim for two to four short, relevant contributions, with at least one that adds a new angle or a summary."
**Explain simply:**
- Speaking first helps only if the point is strong.
- Speaking most can look dominant and reduce listening.
- The number is a judgement, not a rule: in 15 minutes with 8 people, about 2 to 4 contributions of 20 to 40 seconds is a reasonable target (my estimate, unverified).
**Plan / steps:**
1. First speak by minute 3 to 4.
2. Second, a link or example in the middle.
3. Third, a clarification or a summary near the end.
**Worked example or script (arithmetic):** 15 minutes = 900 seconds. If 8 people spoke equally, each would get about 112 seconds, roughly three to four 30-second turns. So 3 turns of 30 seconds is a fair share.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if I only get one chance?* See Q33. 2) *Is silence fatal?* Long silence can hurt; see Q10.
**Common mistakes:** Counting turns instead of value; repeating the same point; talking over the shortest person.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Will AI take away more jobs than it creates? Show how you would structure your points in a GD on this topic.
**Asked at:** commonly asked as a GD topic (the most repeated topic family in 2025-26 placement-blog lists, unverified); no company-specific report found.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Balanced, structured thinking on a topic where nobody knows the answer.
**Say this in the interview:** "Nobody can say for sure. I would look at which tasks change, which jobs are created, who is affected most, and what we can do about it."
**Explain simply:**
- Separate tasks from jobs: AI changes tasks inside jobs; some jobs shrink, some grow, some are new.
- Time matters: short-term disruption versus long-term adjustment.
- Who is affected: routine, repetitive work first; reskilling decides outcomes.
- Avoid quoting exact forecasts unless you are sure of the source; say "various studies suggest, I do not remember the exact figure".
**Plan / steps:**
1. Frame: "It is both; the real question is who is affected and how fast."
2. For: routine tasks automated (data entry, basic reporting).
3. Against: new roles (data, AI oversight) and productivity gains.
4. Way forward: reskilling, company and policy support.
**Worked example or script:** "In an HR team, AI may automate attendance reports and resume screening first. But someone must define good data, check bias in screening, and talk to people. So the work changes more than it disappears, if people are reskilled in time."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Give a number.* Say you do not recall an exact figure and prefer to speak of direction. 2) *What about your own career?* One line on moving to analytics, no personal story.
**Common mistakes:** All doom or all hype; fake statistics; reading out memorised points.

### Q8 ⭐⭐ 🟢 You have never taken part in a GD. How would you prepare in two weeks?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can build a practice plan and learn quickly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would learn the format, practise in small groups, record myself, and work on a few topic families."
**Explain simply:**
- Skills to train: structuring, listening, short speaking, and calm tone.
- You need people: friends, a study group or a mock group (online practice sites exist, from memory, unverified).
**Plan / steps:**
1. Days 1 to 2: read what GDs score (Q1) and watch a few sample GD videos.
2. Days 3 to 5: solo practice, speak 1 minute on a topic (claim, reason, example); record and listen.
3. Days 6 to 9: mock GD with 3 to 5 people, 10 minutes, one gives feedback.
4. Days 10 to 12: practise entering, disagreeing and summarising (Q2, Q5, Q12).
5. Days 13 to 14: two full mocks including one case topic and one online mock.
**Worked example or script:** Topic list for rotation: AI and jobs, work from home, privacy, gig economy, degree relevance, monitoring employees.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *No group available?* Practise alone with a timer, then join an online mock. 2) *Should I memorise points?* No, memorise structures.
**Common mistakes:** Only reading, never speaking; practising only monologues; no feedback.

### Q9 ⭐⭐ 🟢 One participant dominates and nobody else gets a chance to speak. How do you bring yourself, and the quiet members, into the discussion without being rude?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Tact and leadership without aggression.
**Say this in the interview:** "I acknowledge the dominant person's point, then politely widen the floor by asking the quiet members for their view."
**Explain simply:**
- Do not attack or interrupt rudely; appreciate first.
- Invite by name: quiet people often have good points.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Thank the speaker for the point.
2. Pivot: "Let us hear others too."
3. Ask a quiet person a simple, easy question.
4. Then add your own point.
**Worked example or script:** "Thank you, Rahul, that is a clear point on cost. Before we move on, I would like to hear from Anita and Sam, who have not spoken yet. Anita, how do you see the people side?"
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if he keeps talking?* Try once more, then speak in the next pause. 2) *Is naming people risky?* Not if it is warm and the question is easy.
**Common mistakes:** Saying "you are talking too much"; ignoring the problem; pushing a quiet person with a hard question.

### Q10 ⭐⭐ 🟢 You are the quietest person in the group and 10 minutes have passed without you speaking. What do you do now?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you recover and contribute usefully.
**Say this in the interview:** "I speak now with a short, useful point: a new angle, an example, or a summary. Late is better than never."
**Explain simply:**
- Do not apologise at length or repeat what was said.
- Best late points: a new angle, a clarification, or a wrap-up of what has been covered so far.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Pick the best of your notes that no one has said.
2. Raise a hand, wait for the pause.
3. Speak for about 30 seconds.
4. If time remains, speak once more.
**Worked example or script:** "May I add a point that we have not covered? Everyone discussed productivity; one angle is how managers measure the work of remote teams, which affects fairness in appraisals."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if no gap comes?* Use a hand signal and a polite "May I?". 2) *Will late entry hurt?* It is better than silence.
**Common mistakes:** Staying silent hoping to be noticed; "sorry, I was quiet" speeches; repeating earlier points.

### Q11 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Someone states a wrong statistic confidently. How do you correct it politely in a GD?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Courtesy plus honesty, and whether you can handle facts under uncertainty.
**Say this in the interview:** "I say what I remember, admit if I am not sure, and focus on the point rather than the person."
**Explain simply:**
- You may not be sure either. Do not claim certainty you lack.
- Correct the idea, not the person; offer a way to move on.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Soft opening ("Just to check that figure...").
2. State what you recall and how sure you are.
3. Show why it matters or say the argument still holds either way.
4. Move on.
**Worked example or script:** "Just to check that figure, Vikram. I remember it being lower, but I am not certain of the exact number. Either way, I think the direction of your point holds, so let us look at what it means for hiring."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if I am sure?* Say so briefly and kindly; give the source if you truly know it. 2) *What if they argue?* Do not insist; move to the main point.
**Common mistakes:** "You are wrong"; quoting a number you also invented; spending the discussion on the number.
