### Q76 ⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you changed your mind after hearing new information.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); flexibility and "open to feedback" prompts appear in prep-site behavioural banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you follow evidence rather than ego, and whether you can say "I was wrong" calmly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I had one view, someone showed me a fact I had missed, I checked it, and I changed my plan. I will tell you what the fact was and what I did next."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I never really change my mind unless I am sure I am wrong. Usually I am right, so it does not come up much."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds stubborn, and it gives the interviewer nothing to believe.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In [situation] I believed [your first view], because [your reason]. Then [person or data] showed me [new fact]. I did not accept it straight away; I checked it by [how you checked]. It turned out [what you found], so I changed [decision or plan]. The result was [outcome]. What I took from it is to [habit, for example check the source before I commit]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I thought the high exits in one department were about pay, because that was the common view. A colleague pointed out that most leavers had the same manager. I pulled the exit list, and 7 of the 10 leavers did report to one person. So I dropped the pay theory and suggested a manager-level review instead. I now look at the split by manager before I name a cause."
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: a view I held. Task: reach the right decision. Action: tested the new fact, then changed course openly. Result: a better outcome plus a habit.
**If you have no direct experience:** Use a small real moment (a study group, a course project, a household decision) and keep it honest. Or say: "When I get new information I do three things: check where it came from, test it on a small sample, and then update my plan and tell the people affected."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Was it hard to admit?* "A little, but a wrong plan costs more than a bruised ego." 2) *Do you ever hold your ground?* "Yes, when the evidence does not support the change; I ask for the data first."
**Common mistakes:** Choosing a trivial example; making the other person look foolish; skipping the "how I checked" step; pretending you were never wrong.

### Q77 ⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you were praised for your work. What exactly did you do to earn it?

### Q81 ⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you worked with a person who was not pulling their weight.

### Q83 ⭐ 🟡 The interviewer says "That sounds like a team result, what did you personally do?" How do you answer without sounding boastful?

### Q90 ⭐ 🔴 How would you decide which of your stories to use when the question does not match any of them exactly?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "forcing a story that does not fit" is a common STAR mistake in guidance articles (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you listen to the real question and adapt, rather than reciting a memorised story.
**Say this in the interview:** "I find what the question is really testing, pick the story that shows that, and bend only the emphasis, never the facts."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I have five stories ready, so I use the closest one whatever the question is."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds scripted and may not answer the question.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "First I pause and ask myself what quality is being tested, like ownership or teamwork. Then I look through my story list for the one that shows it most clearly. If it fits only partly, I say so: 'The closest example I have is...'. I keep the facts the same and change what I stress. If nothing fits, I say so honestly and describe how I would handle it. Then I ask whether that helps."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Suppose the question is 'Tell me about a time you led without authority' and my best story is about a payroll deadline. I could say: 'I never held a lead title, but here is a time I got 5 team leads to submit data on time.' The facts stay the same; the focus becomes persuasion."
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**STAR breakdown (here: structure):** Point: find the quality tested. Reason: stories are tools, not scripts. Example: map one story to several themes. Link back: keep it true.
**If you have no direct example:** Prepare 5 or 6 true stories on a table with columns for theme, "I" actions and result. Practise each in two shapes: short and long. If no story fits, answer with "how I would handle it" and say so.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you blank?* "Ask for a moment to think; it is fine." 2) *Can one story serve many questions?* Yes, if you change the angle honestly.
**Common mistakes:** Reciting a story that does not answer the question; changing facts to fit; refusing to answer; using the same story repeatedly in one interview.
