### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What do you do when you do not understand what a business stakeholder is trying to tell you in a meeting?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); stakeholder-handling questions appear in prep-site banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you will ask when unsure, or nod and build the wrong thing.
**Say this in the interview:** "I say it politely and early: let me repeat it back in my words, and you tell me if I have it right. Then I confirm in writing after the meeting."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I nod and later try to work it out myself, or I Google it."
**Why it is weak:** Silent guessing is how analysts deliver a report nobody wanted.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "If I do not follow, I do not pretend. I say, 'Let me check I understood. You want [the decision or number] so that you can [the action], is that right?' If it is still unclear I ask for one example, like 'Can you show me one case where this happened?' After the meeting I send a short note: 'My understanding is [x]. Please correct me by [day].' This costs a minute and saves days of rework."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A plant head says, "I need the people numbers by shift." Not sure whether that meant headcount or absence, the analyst asks, "Do you mean how many people are on the roll per shift, or how many were absent last week?" The answer was absence. The follow-up note read: "Absence count by shift, last four weeks, sent each Monday."
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: unclear request in a meeting. Task: get the right ask. Action: repeat back, ask for an example, confirm in writing. Result: [the report that was actually needed, no rework].
**If you have no direct experience:** Say: "I have not been in an analyst meeting yet, but in HR I [true example of clarifying a request, e.g. a policy question from a manager]. This is the same habit I would use." Say this only if it is true for you.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you feel embarrassed to ask?* A repeat-back sounds careful, not weak. 2) *What if the stakeholder is impatient?* Ask one short question, then send the rest by message.
**Common mistakes:** Asking "what do you mean?" in a way that sounds like blame; never confirming in writing; building first and asking later.

### Q34 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you listen in a meeting, and how does listening make your analysis better?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); listening and stakeholder skills sit under communication in analyst prep guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you listen for the real problem behind the request, not only the words.
**Say this in the interview:** "I listen for the decision they must make, the word they keep repeating, and what worries them. That tells me what to measure."
**Explain simply:**
- Listen for the decision, not only the data request ("I need attrition" may really mean "should I raise pay in this team?").
- Notice repeated words and worries; they point to the metric that matters.
- Take short notes of numbers, dates and names, and ask one clarifying question at the end.
- Do not plan your reply while they speak; pause one second before answering.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Before: write what you think they want. 2. During: note decision, deadline, definition of key terms. 3. End: repeat back in two lines. 4. After: send the note, then check the analysis against it.
**Worked example or script:** HR head says, "Exits are hurting us in the sales team." Listening for the worry, you ask, "Is the concern the number of exits, or who is leaving, such as experienced people?" She says experienced people. Now you split exits by tenure instead of giving one total.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Give an example of listening changing your work.* Use a true one, or the example above labelled as how you would do it. 2) *Do you take notes?* Yes, by hand or laptop, short.
**Common mistakes:** Taking the first sentence as the whole request; interrupting; nodding without noting the deadline.

### Q35 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What analogy or everyday example would you use to explain a dashboard, a database or a KPI to an HR manager?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "explain to a non-technical person" is a common test (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can use plain pictures from the listener's own world.
**Say this in the interview:** "I use things HR already knows: the car dashboard, the employee master file, and the monthly headcount number."

- **Database:** like the office filing cupboard, but every drawer is linked. The employee file, the attendance register and the payroll sheet all use the same employee ID, so they match.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Where does the analogy break?* Say it honestly, in one line. 2) *Another analogy for a join?* Matching two registers by employee ID.
**Common mistakes:** Stacking three analogies; picking a technical analogy (such as "like an API"); never saying the limit.

### Q36 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you explain a correlation to a manager without letting them hear it as proof of cause?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); correlation vs causation is a standard analyst theme in question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you protect decisions from a false conclusion.
**Say this in the interview:** "Two things moving together is a clue, not a reason. I show the pattern, then name another possible explanation."
**Explain simply:**
- Correlation means two numbers rise and fall together.
- Cause means one makes the other happen; data alone does not prove that.
- A third factor may drive both (ice cream sales and drowning both rise in summer).
**Plan / steps:** 1) State the pattern in plain words. 2) Say "this does not prove cause". 3) Give one other explanation. 4) Suggest a small check (compare teams, or a pilot).
**Worked example or script:** Invented numbers: overtime hours and exits across six teams (overtime 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16 hours a week; exits 8, 9, 11, 12, 15, 16 per cent a year). The correlation is about 0.99 (computed by script). Say: "Teams with more overtime also have more exits. That does not prove overtime makes people leave. Those teams may also have heavier targets or weaker managers. Before we cut overtime, I would compare people in the same team who work different overtime." Note: six teams is also very few points.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you prove cause?* A controlled trial or pilot, when possible. 2) *Is a high correlation useless?* No, it is a lead to investigate.
**Common mistakes:** Saying "overtime leads to exits"; quoting 0.99 to a non-technical person without saying what it means; skipping the sample-size caveat.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you decide how much detail to give in a presentation of findings, and what do you keep in the appendix?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "how do you present to management" appears in prep banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can choose what the audience needs and keep proof ready.
**Say this in the interview:** "The main talk answers their decision. Everything that proves it, but they will not ask about unless doubtful, goes in the appendix."
**Explain simply:**
- Ask: what decision will they make, and how much time do they have?
- Main body: answer first, two or three supporting points, one recommendation.
- Appendix: method, data sources, definitions, full tables, extra cuts, data-quality notes.
- Rule of thumb: if removing it changes the decision, keep it in the body.
**Plan / steps:** 1) Write the decision in one line. 2) List everything you found. 3) Mark each item "changes the decision" or "supports". 4) Keep only the first group in the talk. 5) Number the appendix so you can jump to it.
**Worked example or script:** For a 10-minute talk to an HR head: slide 1 answer, slides 2-3 evidence, slide 4 recommendation; appendix: how attrition was defined, data cleaning notes, the full department table. If asked, "Detail is in appendix 2, shall I open it?"
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they want everything?* Offer a follow-up session. 2) *Do you hide bad news in the appendix?* No: anything that changes the decision stays in the body.
**Common mistakes:** Putting every chart in the body; moving an awkward finding to the appendix; no appendix, so no proof when challenged.

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You do not know the answer to a question about your own analysis during a meeting. What do you say?
