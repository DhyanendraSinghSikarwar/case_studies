### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about yourself.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); self-introduction is reported as an opening question in some Glassdoor India reviews (for example Accenture, Capgemini; snippet level, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can give a short, clear, relevant summary and link it to this job, not read out your life story.
**Say this in the interview:** "Present, past, future in about 90 seconds: what I do now, how I got here, and why this analyst role is the next step."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I was born in [city], did my MBA, then joined a company in HR, then another company, and now I want a change. I am hard working and a team player."
**Why it is weak:** It is a list of facts with no link to the job and no reason for being here.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I am an HR professional with [X years] in [areas, for example payroll, hiring, attendance]. A big part of my work was [reports or data I handled], and I noticed I enjoyed finding the story in the numbers more than anything else. So over the last [time] I have been learning [Excel, SQL, a BI tool] and built [one small project]. I am now looking for a data analyst role where I can use my people-data knowledge and my new skills. That is why this role at [company] interests me."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I work in HR operations, mostly payroll and attendance for about 240 employees. Every month I reconciled the attendance sheet with payroll, and I found I liked spotting patterns, like which teams had the most missing punches. I started learning Excel pivots and basic SQL, and I built a small attendance report. Now I want to do this full time as an analyst, ideally on people or operations data."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Not a story question. Structure: Present (role now), Past (one or two relevant steps), Future (the analyst goal), Link back to this company. About 90 seconds, roughly 200 words at normal speaking speed.
**If you have no direct experience:** Skip the project line and say what is true: "I have been learning through [course name] and practising on sample HR data." Honest learning is better than a made-up achievement.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can you go deeper on one point?* Pick the data-related part and give one concrete example. 2) *Why are you leaving HR?* Answer with what you are moving towards, not what you dislike.
**Common mistakes:** Starting from school; reading it like a memorised speech; going past two minutes; leaving out why you are in this room.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Please introduce yourself in one minute.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can be brief and prioritise: the three things that matter for this role.
**Say this in the interview:** "One minute is about 130 to 150 spoken words. I choose three points and drop the rest."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I will start from my school days..." and then two minutes pass and the interviewer interrupts.
**Why it is weak:** No sense of priority; the best part (the analytics link) never arrives.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I am [name], an HR professional with [X years] in [area]. I handled [one data-related thing: reports, trackers, payroll checks], and that is where I found I enjoy analysis. I have since learned [tools] and built [project or practice]. I am looking to move into an analyst role where I can [what you want to do], and [company] is a good fit because [one reason]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I am an HR executive with four years in hiring and onboarding. I kept the weekly hiring tracker, and I noticed offers were dropping out at the same stage, so I started learning Excel and SQL to look at it properly. I am looking for a junior analyst role on people or operations data."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Who I am (one line), My data link (one example), What I have learned (tools), What I want (this role). Practise with a timer.
**If you have no direct experience:** Replace the data example with a true task, such as "I prepared monthly headcount numbers" and name the tool you used, even if only Excel.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Tell me more about the tracker.* Be ready with the numbers and what changed. 2) *Which tool are you best at?* Name one and show it (see Q31).
**Common mistakes:** Cramming everything in at fast speed; no ending; sounding rehearsed. Practise until it is a shape in your head, not a script.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Walk me through your resume.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); resume walk-through is a standard opener in career-change guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your career has a logic, and whether you can tell it in order without wandering.
**Say this in the interview:** "I walk through it newest to oldest, or oldest to newest, but I spend most time on the part that matters for this role."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** Reading every line of the resume in the same flat tone, spending equal time on each job.
**Why it is weak:** The interviewer already has the paper; they want the story and the judgement behind it.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I studied [degree], then joined [company] as [role] where I [one-line summary]. After [time] I moved to [company/role], where I took on [more responsibility, especially data work]. In that role [one achievement or lesson]. Around then I started learning analytics through [course/practice] and I am now applying for analyst roles. If you like, I can go deeper into any of these steps."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I did my MBA in HR, then joined a services company as an HR executive handling onboarding. After two years I moved to a larger team where I also prepared the monthly attendance and attrition numbers in Excel. That was the part I liked best, so I added SQL and a dashboard tool on my own. Now I want a role where analysis is the main job."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Education (one line), Job 1 (what and why moved), Job 2 (data link), Switch decision, Today. Give each step one sentence, and name the reason for each move.
**If you have no direct experience:** If a step is a gap or a short stint, say the honest reason in one calm sentence and move on (see Q45).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why did you leave that job?* Give a forward-looking reason. 2) *What was your best achievement there?* Pick one with a number if you truly have it.
**Common mistakes:** Reading the paper aloud; criticising past employers; skipping the reasons for each move.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You have an HR background and you are applying for a data analyst role. Why?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "why are you changing career paths" is listed as a standard question by course and career sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether the switch is thought through, positive and backed by action, not an escape from HR.
**Say this in the interview:** "I did not choose analytics by accident. I discovered I like the data side of HR, I tested it by learning, and now I want to do it full time."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "HR is boring and analytics pays more, so I want to switch."
**Why it is weak:** It is about escape and money, and shows no evidence of real interest.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In my HR work I kept ending up with questions like [real question you faced]. Answering them meant working with data, and I enjoyed that more than anything else. So I started learning [tools] and tried it on [practice dataset or small project]. HR gives me an understanding of people data that many analysts have to learn from scratch. I want to use that together with analytics skills in a role like this one."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Our managers kept asking why people left in the first six months. I pulled the exit list into Excel and saw that most of them were from one hiring batch. I enjoyed digging into that so much that I began learning SQL and pivots. Now I want to do that kind of work all the time."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Point (why analytics), Reason (the moment you noticed), Example (what you did about it), Link back to the role.
**If you have no direct experience:** "I have always worked with HR reports, and learning analytics showed me I enjoy the work. I started with [course] and practice data." Do not claim a big discovery you did not have.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Will you miss HR?* Honest and calm: you value it and will use it. 2) *Why not HR analytics only?* It is a fine fit; say you are open to people analytics roles.
**Common mistakes:** Running down HR; talking only about salary; vague passion with no proof.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What did you do in your last HR role, and what part of it involved data?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can describe your real work and spot the data in it, without inflating it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I describe my main duties in two lines, then pick the two or three tasks that involved numbers, and say what I did with them."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I handled all HR work and also did a lot of data analysis." (no detail)
**Why it is weak:** Vague claims are easy to test and sound exaggerated.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In my last role at [company] I was responsible for [main duties]. The data part was [tasks, for example monthly attendance report, hiring tracker, payroll check]. For example, with [task] I used [Excel/other tool] to [what you did], and the result was [honest outcome]. I want to build on exactly this kind of work."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I handled onboarding and attendance for 240 employees. Each month I checked the attendance sheet before payroll. In one month 18 of the 240 records had missing punches, which is 7.5%, so I listed them by team and sent each manager the names to fix before payroll closed."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: the duty and its data. Task: what you owned. Action: tool and steps. Result: what changed (only a number you truly have).
**If you have no direct experience:** If your role had little data work, say so and pick the closest task, such as counting joiners and leavers in a sheet. Add what you learned outside work.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which tool did you use?* Name only what you used. 2) *How did you check your numbers?* Mention comparing totals against another source.
**Common mistakes:** Claiming "analysis" for tasks that were typing data in; no example; using "we" so the interviewer cannot tell your part.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What are your strengths, and which of them help in an analyst role?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); strengths and career goals appear in HR-round reports (Glassdoor India snippets, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Self-awareness and relevance: strengths backed by evidence and useful in analytics.
**Say this in the interview:** "Two or three strengths, each with a one-line proof, and each tied to something an analyst does."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am hard working, honest and a quick learner."
**Why it is weak:** Everyone says these words, and nothing proves them.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "My first strength is [for example attention to detail]. For example, [true proof]. That helps in analytics because small errors change results. My second is [communication with non-technical people]; in HR I [true proof], which helps when explaining findings to managers. My third is [learning quickly]; I picked up [tool] in [time] by [how]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Attention to detail: before each payroll I compared the attendance count with the payroll count and caught a mismatch of six rows in a 48-row file, which is 12.5%. Communication: I explained leave rules to staff in plain words, so I am used to explaining without jargon."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure for each strength: Point (the strength), Reason (why analysts need it), Example (one true proof), Link to the role.
**If you have no direct example:** Choose strengths you can prove with a class project, a course exercise or a daily habit, and say that plainly.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which one is strongest?* Pick one and expand. 2) *Which is your weakness?* See Q16.
**Common mistakes:** Listing five strengths with no proof; choosing strengths unrelated to the job.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about yourself in a way that connects your HR experience to this analyst job.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "connect past, pivot, future" is the usual advice in career-change guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can build a bridge between two fields instead of presenting two separate lives.
**Say this in the interview:** "I tell it as one line of thought: the HR work gave me people data, the data gave me analysis skills, and the analysis is what I want to do."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I worked in HR for five years. Now I want to do analytics. They are different but I will manage."
**Why it is weak:** It calls the two fields different and leaves the interviewer to find the link.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In HR I spent [X years] close to people data: [two true examples]. That taught me what the numbers mean for real decisions like [hiring, retention, pay]. I then built [tools] so I could analyse that data properly. In this role I could bring both: I understand the business context behind people data, and I have the skills to analyse it."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "In HR I tracked hiring and exits. I know why a 30-day dropout is different from a 3-year exit, which a pure data person may not. I then learned SQL and Excel to measure those things better. For a people-analytics or operations role, I can ask the right questions and then find the answer."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Point (HR gives context), Reason (people data needs judgement), Example (one true case), Link back to the role.
**If you have no direct experience:** "I do not yet have analyst work experience, but I have done [course/project] and I understand the business side of people data." Say what is true.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is this role related to HR data?* Adjust the bridge to the job at hand. 2) *Would you stay in HR analytics?* Be open if true.
**Common mistakes:** Presenting HR as "wasted years"; making the bridge too long; claiming domain expertise you have not shown.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Your resume says HR, not analytics. Walk me through how you decided to switch and what you have done about it.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether the switch is a considered decision with real steps, not a sudden whim.
**Say this in the interview:** "I tell it in three steps: what I noticed, how I tested it, and what I have built since."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I saw that analytics is in demand, so I did a course and now I am applying."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds like a trend-chase and gives no real action or result.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "It started when [true trigger, for example a report I was asked to build]. I realised I enjoyed [specific thing]. To test it, I [first step: online course, practice on a public dataset]. Over [time] I learned [tools] and built [project]. That convinced me this is the work I want to do, so I am now applying to analyst roles."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "My manager asked why joiners left within 90 days. I built a quick Excel sheet and enjoyed the digging. Over the next four months I studied for about an hour on weekdays, practised SQL on a sample employee table, and made a small attrition dashboard. That is when I decided to apply for analyst roles."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: the trigger. Task: test if I enjoy it. Action: learning and a project. Result: the decision plus what exists now (a project, a certificate, a habit).
**If you have no direct experience:** If there was no single trigger, say so: "It built slowly: the more reports I made, the more I wanted to learn." Then list your real steps.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long did the decision take?* Give your true timeline. 2) *Show me what you built.* Have it ready to share.
**Common mistakes:** Giving a dramatic story that is not true; naming courses but not what you can do now.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What have you done so far to prepare for a data analyst role?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); showing self-directed learning is advised for HR-to-analytics switchers (blog level, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your preparation is real, regular and visible, not just a certificate.
**Say this in the interview:** "I name the skills, the practice and one thing I made. Evidence first, courses second."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I did an online course in data analytics and got a certificate."
**Why it is weak:** A certificate alone shows attendance, not ability.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "For [time] I have been studying [skills] through [resources]. I practise by [habit, for example 1 hour a day on real-style data]. I built [project] where I [what you did] and found [what you learned or found]. I am now working on [next skill] to strengthen [gap]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "For about four months I studied Excel pivots and SQL joins. I practised on a made-up employee table of 300 rows, built an attendance dashboard, and found that Mondays had the most absences. Now I am learning to explain results in one slide."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Skills (list), Practice (habit), Proof (one project), Next step (what you are learning now).
**If you have no direct experience:** If you have only just started, say so: "I started [time] ago; so far I have learned [skills] and I am building [first project]." A small true start beats a big false one.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which project are you proudest of?* See Q24. 2) *What is next?* Name a real next step.
**Common mistakes:** Listing many tools you only watched videos on; no project; no plan.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What from your HR experience will make you a better analyst than someone from a pure IT background?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand your real edge and state it without putting others down.
**Say this in the interview:** "I know the business behind people data, and I am used to explaining things to non-technical people. I will not claim I am better at technical skills."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "IT people only know tools. I understand people, so I am better."
**Why it is weak:** It is arrogant, and it ignores the real technical gap.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "A pure IT analyst may be stronger on tools, and I am working on that. What I add is [domain understanding: for example I know how attrition, payroll or hiring really work], and I have experience [explaining to managers or employees in plain language]. That helps me ask the right question first, and present results people understand."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "If a manager says 'attrition is up', I know to ask which tenure band and which hiring batch, because I have seen that exits in the first 90 days have different causes from exits after three years. That question saves hours of wrong analysis."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Point (my edge), Reason (why it matters in analysis), Example (one true case), Honest note (where I am still learning).
**If you have no direct experience:** Speak about what you have seen as a user of HR data, such as reports that confused managers, and how you would make them clearer.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What about your technical gap?* Say what you are closing and how. 2) *Give an example of the domain advantage.* Use the one above, in your own words.
**Common mistakes:** Comparing yourself badly with IT people; claiming domain skill in areas you have not worked in.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You have never worked as an analyst. Why should we trust that you can do this job?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you are honest about the gap and can show evidence you will close it quickly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I agree I have not held the title. Here is the evidence that I can do the work, and here is how I would prove it in the first months."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am a fast learner, so I will manage. Please give me a chance."
**Why it is weak:** It asks for trust without giving any proof.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "You are right, I have not had the title. But I have done analyst-type work: [true example of data work in HR], and I have built [project] to practise [skills]. I am also used to [reliable habits: checking numbers, meeting deadlines]. I would suggest a small task or test so you can see how I work, and I will tell you early if I am stuck."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I have not been an analyst by title. If you give me a sample task, I can show my method."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Agree (honest), Evidence (work and project), Reliability (habits), Offer (a test or trial task).
**If you have no direct experience:** Rely on learning evidence: course work, practice projects, and your discipline. Say you will start small and ask questions early.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you struggle in the first month?* Say how you will ask for help and check understanding. 2) *Would you take a test?* Yes, calmly.
**Common mistakes:** Getting defensive; promising too much; skipping the offer to be tested.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 Your introduction sounds like a list of jobs. Tell me about yourself again in 60 seconds, but this time as a story that ends with why you are here.

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is your biggest weakness, as a switcher into analytics?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, self-awareness, and whether you are already fixing it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I name a real, fixable weakness, say what I am doing about it, and show some progress."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "My weakness is that I work too hard" or "I have no weaknesses."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds evasive and the interviewer learns nothing.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "My biggest gap is [real gap, for example no experience with large datasets or advanced SQL]. I know it matters because [reason]. To close it, I am [specific action and routine] and I have already [small progress]. I also ask [for review/feedback] so I catch my mistakes early."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "My gap is that I have not worked on large, messy company databases. I have practised on sample tables of a few hundred rows, so I am now practising joins on bigger public data for about an hour every weekday, and I check my results against totals I know."
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**STAR breakdown:** Structure: Point (the weakness), Reason (why it matters), Action (what I do about it), Progress (small proof).
**If you have no direct experience:** The honest weakness is the lack of hands-on analyst experience; say what you do to reduce the risk, such as practice and asking for review.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How will you know when it is fixed?* Give a measurable test, such as finishing a project on larger data. 2) *Any other weakness?* Have a second, smaller one ready.
**Common mistakes:** Fake weaknesses; naming a weakness that rules you out of the job; no plan to fix it.
