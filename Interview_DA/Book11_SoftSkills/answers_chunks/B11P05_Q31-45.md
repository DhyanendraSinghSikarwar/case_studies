### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you keep stakeholders in other cities or time zones updated and aligned?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); distributed teams and GCC-style setups are common in Indian job posts, but no interview report is tied to this wording (unverified).

**Say this in the interview:** "I keep one written source of truth, send short regular updates, and agree on working hours and response times at the start."

**Explain simply:**
- Written beats verbal: people in another time zone cannot "overhear" what happened.
- Fixed rhythm: the same update at the same time, so nobody has to chase you.
- Agree the rules early: overlap hours, how fast you reply, which channel is for what.
- Make decisions and next steps obvious, so a person waking up the next day knows what to do.

**Plan / steps:**
1. At the start, ask each person: preferred channel, working hours, and the one thing they need from me.
2. Find the overlap window and keep live calls inside it.
3. Send a short written update (weekly or after each milestone): done, in progress, blocked, needed from you, by when.
4. After every call, send a three-line recap: decisions, owners, dates. Ask them to reply only if something is wrong.
5. Keep one shared tracker or folder; do not hide the status in private chats.
6. Rotate awkward call timings if the team is fair about it, and say thank you for late-evening calls.

**Worked example or script:** "Hi team, update for [week]. Done: [item]. In progress: [item], due [date, with time zone]. Blocked: waiting on [person] for [item]. **Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if someone does not reply for a day?* A polite nudge in the same thread, then a short call in the overlap window (see Q3). 2) *Which tools?* Whatever the company uses (email, chat, a shared tracker); I adapt to them.
**Common mistakes:** Updates only on calls; forgetting time zones; long messages with no clear ask; changing deadlines without telling anyone.

### Q34 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you handle receiving tasks on chat from three different people on the same day?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prioritising competing asks is a standard managerial-round theme (unverified prep sites).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can prioritise calmly, avoid saying yes to everything, and keep people informed.
**Say this in the interview:** "I write all three down, check urgency and impact, tell each person a realistic time, and ask my manager if two clash."

**Explain simply:**
- Do not reply "yes" to everything on instinct; first understand each ask.
- Rank by deadline, impact and who is waiting.
- Tell everyone a clear time, so nobody is left guessing.
- If priorities truly clash, the manager decides, not you.

**Plan / steps:**
1. Collect all the requests in one list (my own tracker, not scattered chats).
2. For each, ask: what is it for, when is it needed, how big is it?
3. Rank them: urgent and important first; a quick five-minute task can be done straight away.
4. Reply to each person: "Got it. I can send this by [time]." For a later one: "I have two urgent items today; I can do yours by [day]. Is that fine?"
5. If two people both say "urgent", send both to the manager or to a common thread and ask for the priority.
6. Keep a note of what was agreed, and update people if timing changes.

**Worked example or script:** Suppose I have 6 free hours today. Request A (payroll check) 2 hours, request B (attendance count) 1 hour, request C (onboarding list) 3 hours. Together that is 6 hours, with no buffer at all. I say to C's owner: "I can do your list tomorrow by 11 am. If it must be today, could you check priority with [manager]?" Today then holds 3 hours of work (A and B) against 6 free hours, leaving a 3-hour buffer for surprises, and everyone is informed.
Chat script: "Thanks, I have two urgent items right now. Could I send this by tomorrow noon? If today is a must, please check with [manager] so we pick the right order."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if all three are from senior people?* Same steps; the manager decides the order. 2) *What if you cannot finish one?* Tell the person early, with a new date and why.
**Common mistakes:** Saying yes to all and missing every deadline; going silent; deciding priorities against the manager's view; doing the loudest request first.
