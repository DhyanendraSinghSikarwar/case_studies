### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you taught someone a tool or a concept in a way they understood. How did you do it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** can you teach, not just do; do you start from the learner's level and check they got it.
**Say this in the interview:** Name the person and the thing, say how you started from what they already knew, and end with proof they could do it alone.
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I showed my colleague how to use Excel. She understood it quickly. I am a good teacher."
**Why it is weak:** No method, no detail, no evidence the other person really learned.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "[Name or role] needed to learn [tool or concept] for [real task]. I first asked what they already used, which was [familiar thing]. I explained it using that, with one small real example from their own work: [example]. Then I let them do it while I watched, and I only stepped in when stuck. At the end I asked them to repeat it on a new case without me. [Result: they did it alone / saved time / stopped asking for help]. What I learned is to start from their world, not mine."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** An attendance clerk kept counting late-comers by hand from a 300-row sheet. I explained a filter as "the same as pulling out only the red-pen entries from the register". We filtered one team together, then she did the other three teams herself. Her weekly count dropped from about 40 minutes to about 10 (invented numbers).
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: [who] struggled with [tool]. Task: [help them do it alone]. Action: [start from known thing, one real example, hands-on, check]. Result: [they did it alone; what changed].
**If you have no direct experience:** Say so, then use a real case from a course, a study group or helping a family member. Or describe how you would teach: ask what they know, use their own data, let them drive, ask them to repeat it back. Say it is a plan, not a past event.
**Likely follow-ups:** "What if they still did not understand?" Try a different example and slow down; the fault is the explanation, not the learner. "How did you know they understood?" They did it on a new case without help.
**Common mistakes:** Talking about the tool's features instead of the learner; saying "I explained and they got it" with no check; making yourself the hero.

### Q50 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you had to persuade someone using data. What did you say and what happened?

### Q53 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You are asked in a client call, "Can you explain how you got this number?" How do you do it live, without jargon?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** can you explain your method calmly, in steps, and show the number is traceable.
**Explain simply:**
- Give the number's definition in one sentence.
- Walk through three steps in everyday words: where the data came from, what you counted or removed, how you calculated.
- Offer to show the detail afterwards.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Pause; say "Good question, let me walk you through it."
2. Source: "I started from [file or system] as of [date]."
3. Rules: "I counted [what], and left out [what], because [reason]."
4. Calculation: say it as a sentence, "[A] divided by [B]."
5. Check: "I cross-checked it against [another source]."
6. Ask: "Is that the definition you had in mind?"
**Worked example or script:** (invented numbers) "The 8.7% is leavers divided by average headcount for the quarter. I took the exit list from the HR system: 27 people who left between April and June. **Likely follow-ups:** "What if you cannot explain a step?" Say you will confirm and reply today; never bluff. "What if the client disagrees with the definition?" Agree on the definition, then recalculate.
**Common mistakes:** Naming functions or formulas ("I did a vlookup then a groupby"); skipping what was excluded; sounding defensive.

### Q54 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you explain the limits of a small sample, such as a survey with 40 responses out of 400 employees, to a non-technical manager?
40 / 400 = 10% response rate. Say what you can and cannot claim.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** can you be honest about weak evidence without being useless.
**Explain simply:**
- Only 1 in 10 people answered; we do not know how the other 9 feel.
- People who reply are often the ones with strong views, so the group may not be typical.
- We can say what these 40 told us; we cannot say it is what the 400 think.
- We can still use it as a pointer to check further.
**Plan / steps:**
1. State the response rate: 40 of 400 is 10%.
2. Say who is missing (check by department, location, grade).
3. Say what you can claim: "Among the 40 who replied, X said Y."
4. Say what you cannot: "This may not represent everyone."
5. Suggest a next step: reminders, a short follow-up, focus groups.
**Worked example or script:** (invented) "Forty of our 400 people replied, so one in ten. Of those 40, 28 (70%) said workload is too high. I would call that a warning sign, not a headline for the whole company. As a rough guide only, with 40 answers the result could easily be 15 points or so either side, and that assumes the replies are random, which they may not be. Let me check whether the 40 come from every department, then send one more reminder."
(The 15-point figure is a rough textbook rule for a random sample, computed by script as about 15.5 points before any adjustment; treat as awareness, confirm with a statistician.)
**Likely follow-ups:** "Can we act on it anyway?" Yes for low-cost steps, no for big spending. "What response rate is enough?" There is no single magic number (from memory, unverified); depends on who answered and the decision.
**Common mistakes:** Saying "40% of employees think..." from a 10% response; using statistical words like "margin of error" without plain explanation; refusing to say anything useful.

2. Split the change into the 2 or 3 pieces that explain it (overtime, new joiners, arrears, bonus).

### Q59 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A stakeholder asks for "the story behind the numbers". What does that mean to you and how do you deliver it?
