### Q65 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you give feedback on a colleague's report or chart without hurting their feelings?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a teamwork and review-culture question in prep-site behavioural banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can be honest and kind at the same time, and whether you criticise the work and not the person.
**Say this in the interview:** "I start with what works, then I point to one or two specific things and say what the reader will experience. I ask before I tell, and I offer a fix, not just a problem."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I tell them straight what is wrong, because being direct saves time." Or: "I never give feedback, I just fix it myself."
**Why it is weak:** The first sounds harsh and unaware of people; the second means you hide problems and never help anyone grow.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "First I say what is good, for example [one real strength of the work]. Then I ask a question, like 'who is going to read this?', so they can see the gap themselves. Then I give one specific suggestion: [what I would change and why it helps the reader]. I keep it to two or three points, I do it in private, and I say 'I found it hard to read' rather than 'you made it confusing'. Afterwards I thank them for being open to it."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A colleague built an attendance chart with 12 colours. I said: "The monthly trend at the top is clear and useful. When I looked at the chart, I could not tell which lines mattered. Could we show only the three plants with the highest absence and grey out the rest? I can sit with you for ten minutes." They reduced it to four colours and said it was easier to present.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: [colleague's report or chart that was hard to read]. Task: [help them improve it before it reached the manager]. Action: [named a strength, asked a question, gave one specific fix, did it privately]. Result: [what changed, and how the reader or manager reacted].
**If you have no direct experience:** Say so, then describe how you handled feedback you received (a mentor or a team lead reviewing your work) and what you liked about the way it was given. Then say how you would do it: strength first, one or two specifics, a question, a fix, in private. Only say it is a plan, not something you did.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they get defensive?* I slow down, ask what they were trying to show, and agree on the goal first; the fix then feels like a shared answer. 2) *What if it is a senior person's work?* Same steps, framed as a question: "would the reader see X if we did Y?"
**Common mistakes:** Giving ten comments at once; praising in a fake, empty way; criticising in public or by email to everyone; saying "you always" or "you never".

### Q66 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you decide what to leave out of a presentation of findings, and how do you justify it if someone asks?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "focus, do not dump data" appears in data-storytelling guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you can choose, not just show everything, and that leaving something out does not mean hiding it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I keep only what helps the audience make the decision in front of them. Everything else goes into a backup page, and I tell people it is there if they want it."

**Explain simply:**
- Start from the decision: "What does this person need to decide or do?" Anything that does not change that goes out of the main story.
- Keep the main result, the reason behind it, the limits that could change the decision, and one action.
- Never leave out something that would change the conclusion (for example a big data gap). That is not simplifying, that is hiding.
- Move interesting but secondary material to an appendix or backup slide and say so.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Write the decision or question in one line at the top.
2. List everything you found. Mark each item "changes the decision / explains it / just interesting".
3. Keep the first two kinds in the main story, and put "just interesting" in the appendix.
4. Check: if this was missing, would a reasonable listener decide differently? If yes, put it back.
5. Prepare one line for when someone asks: "I kept this to what affects the decision; the full detail is in the appendix, and I can walk you through it."
**Worked example or script:** An analysis has 14 charts. For an HR head deciding where to focus retention efforts, I keep 3: attrition by department, attrition by length of service, and the main exit reasons. The other 11 go in the appendix. If asked "why did you not show the age breakdown?" I say: "It did not change where we should act, so I put it on backup slide 9. Shall I open it?"

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the audience wants all of it?* I give it in the appendix, which keeps the main talk short and still open. 2) *How do you avoid cherry-picking?* I state the rule I used and that the omitted items are available.
**Common mistakes:** Showing everything to look thorough; hiding a caveat that matters; having no appendix, so any question is a surprise.

### Q67 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A stakeholder asks you to simplify a finding so much that it becomes slightly wrong. How do you keep it simple without making it wrong?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a version of the "honest simplification" question appears in stakeholder-pushback guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you protect accuracy under pressure while still serving a busy person.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will make it shorter, never wrong. I look for a simpler sentence that is still true, usually by dropping detail, not by changing the meaning."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I would give them what they want, they are the manager." Or: "I would refuse and insist on the full technical version."
**Why it is weak:** The first puts a wrong number into a decision; the second ignores the person's real need for a short answer.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I ask what they need the sentence for, so I know how short it has to be. Then I try a version that drops detail but stays true, for example [your simpler true sentence]. If the shortest version would be wrong, I explain the one thing it would miss in a few words: 'if we say X, people will read it as Y, which the data does not show.' I offer the simple line plus a small note, so they get speed and I stay accurate."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A manager wanted "staff are unhappy with workload" from a survey of 400 where 40 replied, and 18 of those 40 (45% of replies) said workload was too high. I said: "Shorter version: nearly half of the people who replied, 18 of 40, said workload is too high. I would not say 'staff are unhappy', because only 10% replied, so we cannot speak for everyone." He used my line in his note.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: [stakeholder wanted a one-line version]. Task: [keep it short and correct]. Action: [asked what it was for, wrote a true short version, named what the shortcut would get wrong]. Result: [what they used, and any decision made].
**If you have no direct experience:** Say "I have not faced this with a senior person, but here is how I would handle it", and then give the steps above. A class or project where you had to cut a report to one page also works if it is true.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they insist on the wrong version?* I explain the risk in business terms (a wrong number could mislead a decision) and escalate politely if it matters; I do not put my name to something false. 2) *Give an example of a simple but true sentence.* "Most of the rise comes from two departments."
**Common mistakes:** Rounding away the key point; saying "everyone" when it was only some; arguing about statistics when a simple true sentence was available.

### Q68 ⭐⭐ 🔴 You have found that one location accounts for most of the overtime cost, and its manager is in the meeting. How do you present it so it informs and does not blame?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); an HR-analytics situational question (judgement-based, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Tact, fairness, and whether you separate a finding from a verdict.
**Say this in the interview:** "I describe the pattern, not the person. I say what the data shows, say I do not yet know why, and ask the location's manager to help explain it, because they know things the data does not."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Location C is wasting money on overtime and its manager needs to fix it." Or: hiding the finding to avoid conflict.
**Why it is weak:** The first blames before the cause is known; the second withholds a finding the company needs.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Before the meeting I would show [location manager] the numbers privately so there are no surprises. In the meeting I would say: 'Overtime cost is concentrated: [location] accounts for [share] of the cost with [share] of the staff. I do not know the reason yet. It could be [reason such as vacancies, a seasonal peak, or shift pattern]. [Manager], what are you seeing on the ground?' Then I would suggest a next step we agree together, such as checking vacancies and shift coverage."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Invented numbers: overtime cost Rs 8.0 lakh in a month; Location C accounts for Rs 5.2 lakh, which is 65% of the cost, with 60 of 300 staff, which is 20% of headcount. I say: "65% of overtime cost comes from a site with 20% of our people. I think it deserves a look. Rahul, I know you had 6 open vacancies and a big order in March. Would those explain it?" Rahul adds that two machine operators left; the group agrees to fill the vacancies first.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: [overtime cost concentrated in one site, manager present]. Task: [share the finding without blame]. Action: [met the manager first, stated the share and headcount side by side, asked for context, proposed a joint next step]. Result: [what was agreed and what was learned].
**If you have no direct experience:** Say it is how you would do it. If you have shared a sensitive HR number with a person affected (a team's leave or attrition figure), describe how you showed them first and asked for their view; keep it true.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the manager gets angry?* I stay calm, repeat that I am asking, not accusing, and offer to review the data together after the meeting. 2) *What if the cause is poor management?* I report the facts and let the business leaders decide, not me.
**Common mistakes:** Using "your location" or "your team's problem"; guessing a reason in front of everyone; surprising the manager in the room; showing the share without the headcount share.

### Q69 ⭐⭐ 🔴 A director asks you to present a result you have not fully checked, in ten minutes. What do you say and what do you not say?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a pressure and integrity question (judgement, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you will not present shaky numbers as fact, and that you stay helpful, not obstructive.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would present what I have, clearly labelled as preliminary, say what is checked and what is not, and say when I will confirm it. I would not say 'this is final' or hide the open points."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Sure, here is the final result." Or: "I cannot present it, it is not ready."
**Why it is weak:** One risks a wrong decision and your credibility; the other leaves a busy director with nothing.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I would say: 'This is a first read, not final. What I am confident about is [the checked part]. What I have not verified yet is [the unchecked part]. If this turns out to be wrong, the effect would be [direction or size]. I will confirm by [time] and send you a note.' Then I give the number with that label. In the ten minutes before, I would quickly check the one or two things most likely to be wrong, such as totals against the source."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Attrition by department. I have checked the company total against the HR records, but not yet whether contract staff are included. I say: "Provisional: attrition is about 12% for the quarter. I checked the total against the HR master. I have not yet confirmed whether contract staff are included; that could move it by a point or two. I will confirm by 4 pm today."
**STAR breakdown:** Point: present with a label. Reason: honesty protects the decision and your name. Example: what is checked, what is not, the likely effect, a time for confirmation. Link back to the role: analysts are trusted because they flag risk early.
**If you have no direct experience:** Say "I have not had to do this in a job yet, but it is how I would do it", and mention a college or practice-project case where you reported something as provisional. Never claim a past case that did not happen.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the director wants one clean number?* I give a number with a range or label and say why, so it is not quoted later as final. 2) *What do you do after?* Send the confirmed version quickly, and say if anything changed.
**Common mistakes:** Giving a number with no label; apologising for ten minutes; over-explaining uncertainty; not following up with the confirmed result.

### Q70 ⭐⭐ 🔴 You must explain a regression, a forecast or an ML score you built to a business user who will rely on it. How do you explain how much to trust it?
**Asked at:** one anonymous report (unverified) describes an Amazon question about explaining a modelling decision to a non-technical sales rep; otherwise commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can say what the model does, how well it has done, where it fails, and how to use it, without maths words.
**Say this in the interview:** "I say what it predicts, show how it did on past data in plain numbers, say where it is weak, and say how to use it, as a guide for a decision and not a guarantee."

**Explain simply:**
- What it is: "a tool that uses past patterns to estimate the future," not a certainty.
- How good it is: compare its guesses with what actually happened, in everyday units (people, rupees).
- Where it is weak: situations it has not seen, such as a new policy or a one-off event.
- How to use it: as one input, with a human check, and a rule for when to override it.
- How we will know it is going wrong: review it regularly against real results.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Say in one line what it predicts and for what decision.
2. Show a backtest: forecast versus actual for months already known.
3. Give the typical error in people or rupees, not a statistic name.
4. Name two situations where it will be unreliable.
5. Agree how it will be used and when you will check it again.
**Worked example or script:** Invented forecast of monthly leavers, checked against six past months: actual 18, 20, 22, 19, 25, 27; forecast 20, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24. The misses were 2, 1, 1, 3, 2, 3, which average 2.0 leavers a month, about 9% of the typical 21.8 (rounded). Script: "On the last six months, my forecast was off by about 2 people a month on average, and never by more than 3. So you can plan hiring using it. It will not see a sudden event, such as a plant closing, so please tell me about big changes and I will re-check it each month against what really happened."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the user asks for the accuracy as one number?* "About 9% off on average on past months", and I explain that it is a past record, not a promise. 2) *What if it is wrong once?* One miss is expected; I look at whether misses grow over time.
**Common mistakes:** Saying "it is 91% accurate" without saying what that means; hiding the limits; using terms like R-squared with this audience; presenting a forecast as a promise.

### Q71 ⭐ 🟢 What is the most complicated thing you have explained to another person, and how did you know they got it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a variation on "tell me about explaining something complex" in behavioural guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you can explain something real and, more important, that you check understanding instead of assuming it.
**Say this in the interview:** "It was [topic]. I used [an everyday example], and I knew they understood because [they explained it back or used it correctly]."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I explained a very complex model and they said they understood." (no detail, no check)
**Why it is weak:** No story, and "they said they understood" is not evidence.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "The most complicated thing I explained was [topic, in an HR or study setting]. The person was [role] and was worried about [what]. I started from what they already knew, used [everyday example], and kept to [one or two main points]. To check, I asked them to tell me in their own words [what I had said], and [what they said or did that showed it worked]. Afterwards [result, for example they used it in their own work]."

She said "3%" and then corrected last month's figure, which she had calculated using the wrong headcount.

**If you have no direct experience:** Use a true example from studies, a family member, a training session in HR, or a tool you taught a colleague. If you have none, say how you would check understanding: ask them to explain it back or try a small example.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you check without sounding patronising?* "Just so I explained it clearly, how would you tell your team?" 2) *What if they did not get it?* I try a different example and a smaller piece.
**Common mistakes:** Picking something that is not really complex; using "they nodded" as proof; inventing a dramatic story.

### Q73 ⭐ 🟢 How do you use body language, pace and pauses when presenting numbers?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); related to Accenture-style and Cognizant-style communication assessments mentioned in research/interview_processes.md (formats unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know that how you say a number affects whether it lands.
**Say this in the interview:** "I slow down for the key number, pause after it, look at the person, and say it once clearly. I keep my hands open and steady so the number, not my movement, gets attention."

**Explain simply:**
- Pace: slow on numbers and key words, normal on the rest.
- Pause: stop for about two seconds after the important number, so it sinks in.
- Eyes and posture: look at the listener (in a video call, at the camera) and sit upright.
- Hands: calm, simple movements; point to the part of the chart you mean.
- Say numbers in round, spoken form ("about one in eight") and then the exact figure once.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Mark the one or two numbers that matter in your notes with a "pause" sign.
2. Practise out loud twice, with a phone recording.
3. Watch the recording on mute (body) and then with sound only (pace).
4. Cut filler such as "basically" and "you know" and replace it with a pause.
5. Do a final read-through with a friend, asking: "what number do you remember?"
**Worked example or script:** "Attrition last quarter was... [pause] twelve percent. [pause] That is two percentage points higher than the quarter before. [pause] Most of it comes from two teams." Short sentences, one number each, a pause between.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you get nervous?* I breathe out before I start, slow my first sentence, and pause rather than rush. 2) *What about a phone call?* More voice emphasis, since there is no body.
**Common mistakes:** Rushing through every number; reading from a slide with your back to the audience; no pause after the key number; many numbers in one sentence.

### Q74 ⭐ 🟢 How would you explain what a data analyst does to your parents or a friend outside the industry?

### Q76 ⭐ 🟡 Why might a short answer be better than a complete answer when a manager asks about a number?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a "judgement and brevity" question (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you answer the question that was asked, then offer more.
**Say this in the interview:** "A manager asking about a number usually needs it to act or decide. I give the number and one line of meaning, then ask whether they want the detail. A short answer respects their time and shows I understand the question."

**Explain simply:**
- Short answer: the number, what it means, and whether it is good or bad.
- Then offer: "I can walk you through how I got it if that helps."
- Complete answers are not wrong, but they bury the point and use the manager's time.
- Exceptions: when the number is risky or likely to be misread, add the one caveat that matters.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Answer the exact question in one sentence.
2. Add the "so what" in one more sentence.
3. Offer detail, and stop talking.
4. If a caveat could change the decision, add it in one line.
**Worked example or script:** Manager: "What is absenteeism this month?" Short answer: "4.5%, up from 3.0% last month, so 1.5 percentage points higher. Mostly the night shift. I can break it down by team if useful." (4.5 minus 3.0 is 1.5, checked by script.)

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When is a longer answer better?* When the topic is complex or the decision is big, and the person asks for it. 2) *What if they want more?* They will ask; the short answer opens the door.
**Common mistakes:** Starting with the method; skipping the meaning; stopping so short that the caveat is missed.

### Q77 ⭐ 🟡 How do you use questions to check understanding while you speak, without sounding patronising?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); checking understanding is a recurring theme in explaining-to-non-technical guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you check understanding in a respectful, natural way.
**Say this in the interview:** "I put the responsibility on me, not them: 'Did I explain that clearly?' or 'Which part would you like me to say again?' I also ask them to apply it: 'What would you do with this?'"

**Explain simply:**
- Avoid "Do you understand?" because it feels like a test; people say yes to be polite.
- Use "me" language: "Let me know if I went too fast."
- Ask for a use, not a definition: "How would this change your plan for next month?"
- Ask at natural pauses, after the main point, not after every sentence.
- Watch faces and tone as well as words.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Plan two check points in your talk, after the main finding and after the recommendation.
2. Use a "me" phrase at the first one.
3. Use an "apply it" question at the second.
4. If you see doubt, re-explain with a different example, not the same words louder.
**Worked example or script:** "So that is the key point: most of the rise is in two teams. Before I go on, did I explain that clearly, or would a quick example help? ... Good. Given this, which team would you speak to first?"

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if no one answers?* I name a likely question: "Some people ask whether this includes contract staff; it does not." 2) *What about in writing?* I add "tell me if anything is unclear" with an exact contact point.
**Common mistakes:** "Does that make sense?" every minute; "Do you understand?"; asking a question and then not waiting.

### Q78 ⭐ 🟡 Do you use AI tools to draft summaries or emails about findings? How do you make sure the message is still accurate and your own?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); an emerging question in 2025-26 prep material (unverified, no company report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honest use of AI, protection of confidential data, and ownership of accuracy.
**Say this in the interview:** "If I use it, I use it for wording only, never for the numbers, and never with confidential employee data unless the company has approved the tool. I check every figure against my own analysis and rewrite in my own voice."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Yes, I paste the data into an AI tool and send what it writes." Or: "No, never; I do not trust it."
**Why it is weak:** The first risks wrong numbers and a data leak; the second sounds unrealistic for a modern workplace.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "[Say honestly whether you use it.] If I use it, it is to tidy my own draft or suggest a clearer sentence. I do not paste personal employee data or anything confidential unless the company has an approved tool and policy. ### Q80 ⭐ 🟡 What would you say in a meeting if two senior people disagree about what your numbers mean?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a stakeholder-conflict situational question (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can stay neutral, clarify what the data does and does not say, and not take sides.
**Say this in the interview:** "I stay neutral. I restate what the data says, check whether they are using the same definition, and say what it cannot tell us. Then I suggest what would settle it, such as an extra check."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I would agree with the more senior person." Or: "I would say the numbers are clear and one of them is wrong."
**Why it is weak:** The first drops the facts; the second is blunt, picks a fight and may be wrong.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I would first thank both and say I want to be sure we are looking at the same thing. I would restate what the number measures, and ask each how they define it. Often the gap is a definition. Then I say what the data supports, what it does not, and what extra check I could do, with a date. I keep it to the facts and do not say who is right."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** The finance head says attrition is 12%; the HR head says 8%. I say: "Both can be right. 36 people left in the year with an average headcount of 300, which is 12% for all exits. 24 of those were voluntary, which is 8%. So one number counts everyone who left, and the other counts only resignations. Which one would be more useful for this decision? I can put both on one page." (Invented numbers, computed by script.)
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: [two seniors read the numbers differently]. Task: [keep the discussion fact-based]. Action: [restated the measure, asked for each definition, showed both versions, suggested a check]. Result: [what they agreed, and what was sent after].
**If you have no direct experience:** Say it is your plan. If you have seen two managers disagree about an HR figure (headcount, attrition), describe truthfully how definitions were the cause.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if it is not a definition issue?* I show the data, say what it cannot prove, and propose a check. 2) *What if one of them pressures you to change the number?* I explain that the number stays what the data says, and that I can show other cuts.
**Common mistakes:** Taking the senior person's side; being drawn into the argument; not checking definitions; not writing up the agreed meaning afterwards.
