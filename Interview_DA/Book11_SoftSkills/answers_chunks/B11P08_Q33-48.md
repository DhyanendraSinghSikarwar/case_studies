### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You have two offers: one pays more, the other has a better learning path. How will you decide, and what will you tell us?

**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Invented figures for an exercise: Offer A is Rs 6.0 LPA with mostly reporting-support work; Offer B is Rs 5.2 LPA with a dashboard role and a senior analyst as mentor. **Common mistakes:** Inventing an offer to create pressure; saying money is the only factor; bad-mouthing the other company.

### Q47 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Role-play: you accepted our offer, but a better one arrives before your joining date. What do you do, and what would you tell us?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); post-acceptance dropouts are discussed in HR trade press (SHRM India, People Matters, snippet level, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Your integrity and whether you honour commitments.
**Say this in the interview:** "I gave my word, so I would honour it unless something truly major changed. If I had a serious reason to reconsider, I would tell you early and honestly, not disappear."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I would take the better one and just not turn up." Or: "I would use the new offer to get you to raise yours."
**Why it is weak:** Silent dropout harms the company and your own reputation; using it as leverage after acceptance breaks trust.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I take my acceptance seriously, so my first answer is that I would join as promised. If a much better offer arrived, I would think hard about whether it is that different in what matters, which is learning and fit, not only pay. If I decided to change, I would call [the HR contact] myself, early, explain honestly, and apologise. I would not avoid the call. I know that has a cost, so I would not accept an offer I was unsure about in the first place."

### Q48 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Role-play: three days before joining, your current employer asks you to stay for a month for a handover. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); last-minute extensions are a known joining risk in HR trade press (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can handle two commitments honestly and not simply drop one.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not decide on the spot. I would check what I owe my current employer in writing, and then speak to you the same day about my options."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Sure, I will stay a month," without telling the new company. Or: "No, I do not care, I am leaving."
**Why it is weak:** The first breaks your promise to the new company without warning; the second may cause a dispute or a poor relieving.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Three days before joining, I would check my notice terms and relieving letter status first. If I have already served my notice, I would politely say I am bound to join on [date], and offer a short handover by phone or two evenings, to the extent that I can. If I owe more under the agreement, I would call you at once, explain, and ask whether a short delay is possible. I would not agree to anything with either side until I have it in writing."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Invented: joining date 15 December 2026. A 30-day extension would move the start to 14 January 2027 (computed by script). The candidate offers instead: "I can document everything this week and be available by phone for two weeks after, outside work hours." They confirm what they can with the new HR before replying to the old manager.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: a late ask from the current employer. Task: protect both commitments. Action: check terms, offer a limited handover, tell the new company the same day. Result: [a written agreement; a joining date kept or moved with consent].
