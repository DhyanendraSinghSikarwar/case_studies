### Q31 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You had a stable HR career. What is the risk you are taking with this move, and how have you reduced it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); career-switch question banks list "will you change again" style probes (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have thought about the downside honestly and acted to reduce it, instead of acting on excitement alone.
**Say this in the interview:** "The risk is real: I give up seniority and some certainty. I reduced it by testing the work first, building skills in the evenings, and choosing a role where my HR knowledge still counts."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "There is no risk. I am very confident, and analytics is the future."
**Why it is weak:** It sounds naive; a calm answer that names the risk sounds more mature.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Honestly, there are two risks. One, I start lower than where I am in HR, and my pay or title may not match for a while. Two, I could find I enjoy the idea more than the daily work. To reduce the first, I [saved a buffer / discussed it with my family / accepted the entry-level scope]. To reduce the second, I did [a real project / a course with graded work / helped with a report at my job] before deciding, and I enjoyed [the specific part]. This is a considered move, not a jump."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Before I applied, I took our monthly attendance sheet, which had about 400 rows, and built a pivot and a simple chart showing late marks by team. I enjoyed the cleaning and finding that one team had most of the late marks, more than I expected. That told me the work suits me, so the risk felt smaller."
**STAR breakdown:** Point: name the risk plainly. Reason: why you accepted it. Example: one step you took to test the move (true). Link back: why the role is a safe place to learn.
**If you have no direct experience:** Say so: "I have not done analytics at work. My test was [course project / public dataset analysis], and I plan to learn fast in the first six months." Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version above.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if it does not work out?* "I would review at six months with my manager and still have skills useful in HR analytics." 2) *Did you talk to analysts?* Answer only if true.
**Common mistakes:** Claiming zero risk; naming a risk with no step to reduce it; inventing a "side job" you never had.

### Q35 ⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a data-driven decision or story that impressed you.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); appears in analyst question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you follow data stories and can explain the link from data to decision in plain words.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will pick one story I can explain end to end: what the question was, what the data showed, what changed."
**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I read that big companies use data to make decisions. It is very impressive."
**Why it is weak:** Vague, no specific story, no sign you understood it.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "One that stayed with me was [story you really know: from your work, a course, a news article, a case study]. **Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "Imagine a team that thought attrition was a pay issue. **Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would you have done differently?* Name one limit, for example sample size. 2) *Could you do that here?* Link to the role's data.
**Common mistakes:** Naming a company story you cannot explain; mixing up facts; no decision in the story.

### Q36 ⭐ 🟢 What questions do you have for us about the role and the team?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the closing question in most interview rounds.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you are curious, prepared and thinking about success in the job.

**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Choose questions from what you actually read about the company. For example: "I saw the job mentions [tool from the JD]. How much of the day is that?" Never reference facts you have not read.

**If you have no direct experience:** Not applicable; any candidate can ask these. Use the JD to personalise, and keep salary for the HR round. Say this only if it is true for you.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Anything else?* One more question about team culture. 2) *Why that question?* "Because I want to know how to succeed here."
**Common mistakes:** Asking about pay or leave first; asking what the company does (shows no research); asking too many questions.
