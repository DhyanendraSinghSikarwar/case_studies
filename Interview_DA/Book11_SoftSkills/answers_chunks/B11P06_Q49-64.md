### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🔴 What would you do if you found a data problem that would embarrass your manager if raised publicly?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ethics and judgement prompts appear in question banks, and managerial rounds are said to probe ownership (anonymous Glassdoor snippets, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you protect the truth and the manager at the same time: you tell them early, privately, with a fix, and you do not hide the problem.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not hide it and I would not raise it in a meeting. I would tell my manager privately and early, with the facts and a proposed fix, so they can decide how to communicate it."

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I would keep quiet so my manager does not look bad," or "I would point it out in the next team meeting so everyone knows."
**Why it is weak:** the first hides a wrong number that others will use; the second turns a data issue into a public blame moment.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "First I would confirm the problem is real: I would re-check my own steps and size it, for example [how many records, how much impact]. Then I would ask my manager for a few minutes in private and say, 'I found [issue]. It affects [report or decision]. Here is what I know, what I do not know yet, and a fix I can have ready by [time].' That way they are never surprised, and they can choose how to tell others. If the number has already gone to leadership, I would suggest a short correction note instead of waiting. My manager's reputation is best protected by being first to fix it, not by silence."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Imagine I find that 30 exited employees are still counted as active in the headcount the manager showed last week (small invented number). I would check it twice, work out that headcount is overstated by 30, then message my manager: "Can I show you something before Friday's review? Here is the issue, the corrected count, and a one-line note you could send." The manager decides the wording; I prepare the facts.
**Structure that fits:** Point (tell privately and early), Reason (silence and public blame both hurt), Example (the steps), Link back to the role (analysts protect trust in numbers).
**If you have no direct experience:** say "I have not faced this exact case, but this is how I would handle it," and walk through the steps above. Say this only if it is true for you; if you do have a real example, use the template with your own details.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if your manager says to ignore it?* I would explain the risk calmly in writing, ask what they want to do, and if a wrong number would affect people or decisions I would raise it with the next level or the data owner through the proper channel. 2) *Who else do you tell?* Only those who used the number, and only through or with my manager's agreement.
**Common mistakes:** staying silent; raising it in public; arriving with a problem and no proposed fix; guessing the impact instead of measuring it.
