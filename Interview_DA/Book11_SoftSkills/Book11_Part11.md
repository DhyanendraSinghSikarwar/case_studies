### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you dived deep into data or a process to find the real cause of a problem. (Dive Deep)
**Asked at:** Dive Deep prompts like this appear in prep-site and anonymous-review samples for Amazon business analyst and BIE loops (unverified, no Amazon-confirmed India question bank). Principle name from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you look past the surface number, check the detail yourself, and prove the cause with evidence.
**Say this in the interview:** one problem, the numbers you pulled, the cause you proved, the fix, and a measured result, all in "I".

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "There were errors in our payroll, so we checked everything again and fixed it."
**Why it is weak:** no method, no evidence, no number, and "we" hides your part.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "In [month/year], [problem, with its size, for example X of Y records wrong]. I was responsible for [your task]. Instead of fixing each case, I [pulled the raw data / compared two sources]. I grouped the errors by [shift, site, code, date] and found that [number or share] came from [one cause]. I confirmed it by [test or sample check]. Then I [fix] and added [check so it does not return]. After that, errors went from [before] to [after] over [period]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** 38 of 600 payslips (6.3%) had wrong overtime. I exported the raw attendance, grouped the 38 by shift code, and saw 33 were night shift. The night-shift code was mapped to the wrong overtime rule. I corrected the mapping, re-ran a sample of 50 payslips by hand, and added a monthly check. Next month only 3 of 600 (0.5%) had errors.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: 38 of 600 payslips wrong. Task: find why and stop it. Action: I grouped by shift code, traced the mapping, tested a sample, added a check. Result: 6.3% down to 0.5%.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a course or personal dataset where you traced an odd number to its cause, and say it was a course project. Or describe your steps: check the source, split the number by groups, test your guess, then fix.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "How did you know that was the real cause?" I tested it on a sample and the errors matched.
- "What would you do differently?" Add the check earlier.

**Common mistakes:** describing the fix but not the digging; no number; saying "we"; claiming a cause you never tested.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you went above and beyond for a customer, internal or external. (Customer Obsession)
**Asked at:** Customer Obsession-style prompts recur in prep-site samples for Amazon loops (unverified; no frequency data). Principle name from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you start from the user's need and keep going beyond the minimum, with proof of effect.
**Say this in the interview:** name the customer (an employee or manager counts), what they needed, the extra step you chose, and what improved for them.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I always try to keep employees happy and I answer all their queries."
**Why it is weak:** a claim, not a story; no extra effort shown and no result.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "My customer was [who]. They were struggling with [problem]. Answering each request was my job, but I noticed [pattern, for example the same question repeated]. I decided to [extra action nobody asked for], and I [how you checked it helped them]. As a result, [measurable change for them] and [what happened to your own workload or their satisfaction]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Employees sent about 40 leave-balance queries a week. I noticed most asked the same three things. I wrote a one-page guide with screenshots and shared the leave balance sheet monthly. Queries dropped to 14 a week (a 65% cut), and employees stopped waiting for replies.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: 40 repeat queries a week. Task: serve employees, which was my job. Action: I spotted the pattern and built a guide plus a monthly sheet. Result: 40 to 14 a week.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a college club, a family business, or a volunteer role where you served a real person, and name what you did beyond the minimum. Or explain how you would find a user's real need: ask, observe, then fix the cause.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "Who was the customer?" Name them specifically.
- "How did you know it helped?" Point to the 40 to 14 count.

**Common mistakes:** a story about being nice, with no result; confusing "customer" with "boss"; the extra effort was actually part of the job.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you took ownership of something that was not strictly your job. (Ownership)
**Asked at:** Ownership prompts recur in prep-site and review samples for Amazon loops (unverified; no frequency data). Principle name from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you act on problems nobody assigned you and see them through, without blaming others.
**Say this in the interview:** say why nobody else owned it, what you took on, and how you saw it finished.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "It was not my department, but I told my manager about it and she took care of it."
**Why it is weak:** reporting is not owning; you did nothing yourself.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I noticed [gap] that belonged to [nobody / another team]. It was not part of my role, but it was hurting [who]. I asked [owner] and agreed I would [what you took on]. I [steps], kept [people] updated, and finished it by [date]. The result was [measurable change], and I handed over [document / process] so it would not depend on me."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Exit interview notes sat in separate files with no summary. Nobody owned them. I compiled 45 exits from three months into one sheet and found 18 (40%) mentioned a long commute. I shared it with my manager and the transport team. A shuttle pilot started for that route.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: exit notes scattered, no owner. Task: none assigned; I chose to take it. Action: I compiled 45 exits and analysed reasons. Result: 40% cited commute; a pilot started.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a college or volunteer task that nobody was assigned. Or say how you would act: check it is safe, tell the owner, offer to do the work, and finish it.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "Did you tell your manager first?" Yes, I agreed the scope before starting.
- "Did you drop your own work?" No, I did it after core tasks.

**Common mistakes:** picking something that was your job; stopping at "I escalated it"; no result.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you made a decision quickly with incomplete information. (Bias for Action)
**Asked at:** Bias for Action appears in prep-site samples and a mentorcruise guide (unverified). Principle name from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you move without waiting for perfect data, and manage the risk of being wrong.
**Say this in the interview:** state what you knew, what you did not, why waiting was costly, the step you took, and how you limited the risk.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I waited until I had all the data, because I do not like to make mistakes."
**Why it is weak:** it shows the opposite of speed, and gives no example of a decision.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "[Decision] was needed by [deadline], but I only had [what you had] and was missing [what was missing]. Waiting would have meant [cost]. I made a call: [decision], based on [reasoning]. I told [people] the assumptions, and set [a check or reversal step]. Result: [outcome, with a number if true], and when the full data arrived [how close you were]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** A manager needed a headcount estimate in two hours. Three of four site reports were in. I used the three and the last month's figure for the fourth, and flagged it as an estimate. My estimate was 112; the final count was 118, a 5.1% gap. The manager made the decision on time.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: 2-hour deadline, one source missing. Task: give a headcount estimate. Action: I used three sources plus last month's figure and stated the assumption. Result: 112 against 118 actual (5.1% gap), decision on time.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a college or personal decision made with limited information. Or explain your rule: if a decision is reversible, move fast with stated assumptions; if not, check more.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "What if you were wrong?" I had a way to correct it.
- "How did you decide it was safe?" It was reversible and low cost.

**Common mistakes:** describing a reckless guess; no risk control; not mentioning what happened next.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you delivered a result despite a serious obstacle or tight deadline. (Deliver Results)
**Asked at:** Deliver Results appears in prep-site samples for Amazon loops (unverified). Principle name from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you hit outcomes under pressure by planning and removing blocks, not by luck.
**Say this in the interview:** the goal and deadline, the obstacle, the steps you chose, and the result against the target.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "We had a tight deadline, so we all worked late and got it done."
**Why it is weak:** effort only; no obstacle, no method, no number.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I had to deliver [output] by [deadline] when [obstacle]. I broke the work into [parts], decided to [what you cut or automated], and [removed the block by doing X]. I checked [quality step]. I delivered [on time / early], and [measurable result, for example hours saved or errors]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** 420 attendance records had to be reconciled in two days. By hand at about 12 records an hour it would take 35 hours. I wrote a lookup that matched records to the biometric export, which cleared about 105 an hour on my machine, so the matching took about 4 hours, and I checked 40 flagged cases by hand. I finished a day early with zero payroll disputes.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: 420 records, 2 days. Task: reconcile them. Action: I built a lookup and hand-checked flagged cases. Result: 35 hours of manual work cut to about 4; finished a day early.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a college deadline or personal project, labelled as such. Or describe your steps: list the must-haves, cut the rest, remove the biggest block, and check quality before delivery.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "What did you cut?" Name what and why.
- "What would you do with more time?" A cleaner process.

**Common mistakes:** "we worked late" as the whole story; no target to compare with; overstated numbers.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager or a senior colleague and how it ended. (Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit)
**Asked at:** disagree-and-commit prompts appear in prep-site samples (unverified). Principle names from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you say a respectful, evidence-based no, and then move on professionally.
**Say this in the interview:** show you disagreed with data and respect, listened, and either changed the decision or committed fully.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I never disagree with my manager. I always follow instructions." Or: "My manager was wrong, so I did it my way."
**Why it is weak:** the first shows no backbone, the second shows no respect or commitment.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "My manager wanted [decision]. I disagreed because [data or reason]. I asked for [time] and showed [evidence] privately. [She listened and we changed X / she kept her decision for reason Y]. I [committed and did it fully / supported the new approach]. The result was [outcome with a number]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** My manager wanted to report average tenure of leavers. I showed that three very long-tenure exits pulled the average up, so the typical leaver was gone sooner. I showed the median alongside the average. She agreed to report both, and the retention discussion focused on the first-year drop.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: tenure report using averages. Task: give an accurate picture. Action: I showed the data privately and suggested the median. Result: both were reported; the discussion moved to year one.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a disagreement with a teammate or mentor in college or volunteering. Or explain your approach: raise it privately, bring evidence, listen, then commit once decided.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "What if the manager had said no?" I would have committed and delivered.
- "Have you ever been proven wrong?" Have a short honest example.

**Common mistakes:** a story where you simply "won"; attacking the person; saying you never disagree.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Tell me about a time you had to earn the trust of a team or stakeholder who did not know you. (Earn Trust)
**Asked at:** earn-trust prompts appear in prep-site samples (unverified). Principle name from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you build trust through small kept promises, openness and honesty, not charm.
**Say this in the interview:** who did not know you, what you did to prove reliability, and how the relationship changed.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am friendly, so people trust me quickly."
**Why it is weak:** a trait, not evidence.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "When I joined [team/site], [stakeholder] did not know me and [sign of low trust]. I [met them / asked what they needed]. I made one small promise, [promise], and [kept it by date]. When I made a mistake with [thing], I told them first. Over [period], [result: faster replies, more requests, a measurable change]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** At a new site with 60 employees, supervisors sent HR queries to the old contact. I spent a week asking what they needed and promised replies within one working day, then kept it. When I gave a wrong policy answer once, I corrected it the same day. Average reply time fell from 3 days to 1, and supervisors began contacting me directly.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: new site, supervisors did not know me. Task: become their trusted contact. Action: I asked, promised one-day replies, owned an error. Result: replies 3 days to 1.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a new class group, internship or volunteer team. Or explain how you would do it: small promises, kept, with honest updates.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "How long did it take?" Give an honest period.
- "Did you ever break that trust?" Share the corrected mistake.

**Common mistakes:** "I just worked hard"; hiding the mistake in the story; no result.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Amazon asks for a STAR answer. What do Situation, Task, Action and Result each contain, and why do interviewers keep saying "focus on I, not we"?
**Asked at:** commonly asked; Amazon's own interview tips (snippet only, not opened) describe STAR and "I not we" (unverified; check Amazon's pages).
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can tell a clear story and own your part.
**Say this in the interview:** "I keep Situation and Task short, spend most time on what I did, and end with a number. I say 'I' because they are hiring me, not my team."

**Explain simply:**
- Situation: the setting and the problem in one or two lines.
- Task: your own responsibility or goal.
- Action: the steps YOU took, in order, with reasons. About 60% of the time (my rule of thumb, not an Amazon rule).
- Result: the outcome in numbers, plus what you learned.
- "I not we": the interviewer cannot judge you from your team's work. Using "we" hides what you did, so they will ask "what did YOU do?".

**Plan / steps:**
1. Write each story as six lines: one for S, one for T, three for A, one for R (my suggestion, not an Amazon rule).
2. Underline every "we" and replace it with "I" where it was truly you.
3. Add one number to the Result (time, count, percentage, cost).
4. Practise aloud for about two minutes.
5. Credit others briefly ("my manager approved it") but keep your actions clear.

**Worked example or script:** Weak: "We fixed the errors." Better: "I compared the two files, found 33 duplicate rows, and removed them. Errors fell from 38 to 3." (Illustrative only; use your own true facts.)

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "Can I mention my team?" Yes, briefly, and say what part was yours.
- "How long should it be?" About two minutes.

**Common mistakes:** "we" throughout; long Situation; no Result; making up a number.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Interviewers will ask a follow-up such as "What exactly did YOU do?" or "What was the number?". How do you prepare your story to survive three or four layers of probing?
**Asked at:** commonly asked; reviews say interviewers dig into stories (anonymous candidate reviews, snippet only, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether the story is true and you really know it in detail.
**Say this in the interview:** "I prepare each story with the facts behind it, so any follow-up has a real answer."

**Explain simply:**
- Probing checks whether you did the work or watched it.
- Every claim needs a detail behind it: numbers, dates, names of tools, why you chose it.
- A true story survives; a made-up one breaks at the second follow-up.

**Plan / steps:**
1. For each story, write a fact sheet: dates, team size, tools, your exact tasks, the baseline number, the final number, who agreed.
2. List five follow-ups: "Why that approach?", "What was the alternative?", "What was the number?", "What went wrong?", "What would you do differently?".
3. Write a one-line answer to each.
4. Practise with a friend who keeps asking "why?" four times.
5. If you do not know a number, say "I do not have the exact figure; my estimate is X, based on Y".

**Worked example or script:** Interviewer: "What was the number?" You: "Errors went from 38 of 600 payslips to 3 of 600, about 6.3% to 0.5%." Interviewer: "How did you measure it?" You: "I counted queries raised in the next payroll cycle." (Illustrative only; use your own true numbers.)

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "What if I forget a detail?" Say so honestly; do not invent it.
- "Can I prepare a script?" Prepare facts, not a script.

**Common mistakes:** memorising a script; changing numbers; forgetting your own role; overclaiming.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You have never worked in an analytics job. Which kinds of experience (HR work, college, a course project, volunteering) can you use for Amazon Leadership Principle stories, and which should you avoid?
**Asked at:** commonly asked of career switchers; Amazon's interview tips mention career-changers and early-career examples (snippet only, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can draw real evidence from any honest setting.
**Say this in the interview:** "I use real HR work first, because the skills are the same; I label course or volunteer examples clearly."

**Explain simply:**
- Good: HR work that used data (payroll checks, attendance, attrition, onboarding, hiring trackers), because it shows judgement and ownership.
- Good: a course or personal project, clearly labelled.
- Good: volunteering or college events with a measurable result.
- Avoid: stories that are not yours, exaggerated ones, and old personal stories with no result.
- Avoid: confidential details or real names from your employer.

**Plan / steps:**
1. List 8 situations from the last 2 to 3 years.
2. Tag each with the principle it fits.
3. Check each has a number and your own action.
4. Remove anything you cannot defend under probing.
5. Keep two backups from outside work.

**Worked example or script:** "This was a course project, not a job: I cleaned a dataset of 500 rows and found duplicates that changed the average by 4%. I'm sharing it because it shows how I check the detail." (Illustrative only.)

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "Is a course project weaker?" It is fine if honest and specific.
- "Can I use a personal story?" Yes if it shows the principle with a result.

**Common mistakes:** hiding that it was a course; using someone else's story; skipping HR work as "not analytical".

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time a customer or internal user asked for one thing but you realised they needed something different. (Customer Obsession)

Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you worked with little or no data to solve a problem. (Bias for Action; Are Right, A Lot)
**Asked at:** wording modelled on prompts in prep-site samples (unverified). Principle names from memory, unverified, check Amazon's own page.
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can reason, find proxies and still be careful about being right.
**Say this in the interview:** say what was missing, which proxies you used, how you stated the uncertainty, and how you checked later.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "There was no data, so I used my gut and it worked."
**Why it is weak:** no method; luck is not judgement.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "We needed [decision] but there was no [data]. I looked for [proxies: related data, experts, small sample]. I [collected a small sample / asked three people], built an estimate of [value] with a range, and stated the assumptions. We [acted], tracked [metric], and [outcome, how close the estimate was]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** Planning hiring for a new city office with no history. I used three proxies: another office's attrition (12%), a recruiter's call-back rate, and 10 phone checks. I recommended 20 offers for 15 seats, with a range. Six months later, 14 of 15 seats were filled.
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: new city office, no history. Task: size the hiring. Action: I used three proxies and stated assumptions. Result: 14 of 15 seats filled.
Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.
**If you have no direct experience:** use a course exercise where you estimated something. Or describe the steps: define the decision, find proxies, estimate a range, test small, update.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- "How confident were you?" I gave a range.
- "What did you check afterwards?" The actual outcome.

**Common mistakes:** "gut feeling"; no range; no follow-up check.

### Q36 ⭐ 🔴 The interviewer says, "That sounds like what your team did. What was your own contribution, and what would have happened without you?" How do you respond without sounding defensive?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Your real individual contribution and honesty, with no inflated claims and no defensiveness.
**Say this in the interview:** "Fair question. Here is exactly what I did, and here is what the team did. Without my part, [honest effect]."

**Common mistakes:** Getting defensive; saying "we" for everything; exaggerating to sound important.
