# Book 11 — Soft Skills, HR Round, GD & Career-Switch Story | Part 10: Skill-Improvement Playbooks 🟢🟡🔴

**Target:** 40 questions | **Actual:** 43 questions
**Scope:** skill-development playbooks for communication, storytelling, business acumen, structured thinking, presentation, written emails and confidence, as interview or coaching questions: self-assessment, daily practice, 30-day plans, free resources, mini-projects, self-check tests and how to show the skill.

### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How would you rate your communication skills out of 10, and what evidence supports that score?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honest self-awareness: a believable number backed by proof, not a boast or false modesty.
**Say this in the interview:** Give a number in the 6 to 8 range only if your evidence supports it, name two pieces of proof, and name one thing you are still improving.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "10 out of 10, I am an excellent communicator." Or: "Maybe 5, I am not sure."
**Why it is weak:** A 10 sounds unaware and a 5 with no plan sounds unprepared; neither has evidence.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I would say [7] out of 10. The evidence: in [my HR role] I [explained a report or policy change to a group of about X people], and [a named person or result showed it landed, for example fewer follow-up questions]. I also [wrote a weekly summary / ran a training] that people actually used. Where I am not a 9 yet is [one gap, for example keeping answers short when I am nervous]. For that I currently [one specific action, for example recording a two-minute answer each day]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I would say 7 out of 10. In an HR operations role I wrote a one-page monthly attendance summary for 12 team leads, and after I cut it from three pages to one, the number of clarification emails dropped from about 10 a month to about 3 (invented numbers). My gap is that under pressure I add too much detail, so I practise two-minute recordings."
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: where you communicated. Task: what the audience needed. Action: what you changed or did. Result: a sign it worked. Then add the honest gap.
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**If you have no direct experience:** Use evidence from anywhere true: a class presentation, a mock interview a friend rated, a written summary of a project. Say "I rate myself [6] because I have [mock-interview feedback from X]; here is how I plan to reach 8."

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Why not higher?* Name one real gap and your current fix.
- *Who gave you that feedback?* Give a real person or situation; never invent one.

**Common mistakes:** Giving 9 or 10; giving no evidence; naming a gap with no action.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is your biggest weakness in communication, and what are you doing about it right now?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Self-awareness and whether you actively improve.
**Say this in the interview:** Pick a real, fixable weakness (not a disguised strength), say how it showed up, and describe the practice you are doing this month.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am a perfectionist and I work too hard." Or: "I have no weakness."
**Why it is weak:** It dodges the question and sounds rehearsed.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "My biggest weakness is [for example giving too much detail before the main point]. I noticed it when [a true moment, for example a manager said 'what is the bottom line?']. Right now I am working on it by [specific action: stating the answer in one sentence first, then one reason], and I [record or practise X times a week]. It is better than it was: [one small true sign of progress]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I tend to start with background. After a manager once asked me for the bottom line in a payroll meeting, I began writing my answer in two sentences first. For three weeks I have recorded a one-minute update each morning and checked whether the first sentence is the answer."
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: when it showed up. Task: what was needed. Action: your practice. Result: small visible improvement.
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**If you have no direct experience:** Choose a weakness you can truthfully see in mock answers, for example "my answers run long", and describe how you timed and shortened them.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *How do you know it is improving?* Timing, a checklist score, or feedback from one person.
- *Does it affect your work?* Be honest: it affected [X] mildly, and here is the fix.

**Common mistakes:** Fake weaknesses; a weakness that is critical to the role (for example "I avoid talking to stakeholders") with no plan.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Which soft skills matter most for a data analyst, and how have you been developing them?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Our internal JD sample (n = 40, low to medium confidence) mentions communication in about 73% of postings and stakeholder management in about 44%.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand what the job needs beyond tools, and whether you act on it.
**Say this in the interview:** Name three skills (communication, structured thinking, business understanding), and for each say one true thing you do to build it.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "Communication, teamwork, leadership, time management, and everything else." (a list with nothing behind it)
**Why it is weak:** No priorities and no proof of effort.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "For an analyst I would put three first. One, communication: [what I do, for example I explain one chart aloud daily]. Two, structured thinking: [for example I break problems into parts before opening data]. Three, business understanding: [for example I note what decision each number supports]. In my HR work I already [one true example], and I am building the rest through [a project, a course, practice]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "In HR reporting I learned that managers ignore tables, so I practise saying 'what, so what, now what' about each chart. I also take a public HR dataset and write one recommendation per chart."
**STAR breakdown:** Point: the three skills. Reason: why the role needs them. Example: one true action each. Link back to the role.
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**If you have no direct experience:** Say what you are doing now, honestly: "I am practising weekly with [a dataset or mock questions]."

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Which is your weakest?* Name one honestly, with your plan.
- *Why not technical skills first?* They matter; the question was soft skills, and both are needed.

**Common mistakes:** Long lists; claiming leadership you have not shown; no examples.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You come from HR and have never worked as an analyst. How have you built your data storytelling skill?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Storytelling questions appear in data-storytelling question banks (unverified prep sites).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn numbers into a point a business person acts on, and whether you practised outside a job.
**Say this in the interview:** Be open about the switch, then show real practice: a project, a rewritten report, spoken walkthroughs.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I have not done it professionally, but I am a fast learner."
**Why it is weak:** No evidence of any actual practice.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "My HR work already involved explaining numbers to non-technical people: [one true example, for example monthly headcount to managers]. To build the analyst side I [took a public HR dataset / did a project] and wrote [a one-page memo] with the finding first, one chart, and a recommendation. I rehearse it aloud and [get feedback from X]. So I bring a business audience instinct and a growing practice habit."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I used a public attrition dataset, found that employees under one year of tenure left at roughly three times the rate of others (illustrative), and presented it in a 90-second walkthrough ending with one action: strengthen the first-90-day check-ins."
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: HR background, no analyst role. Task: learn storytelling. Action: project, memo, rehearsal. Result: a portfolio piece and a clearer spoken story.
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**If you have no direct experience:** Say plainly: "I have not told data stories at work, so I built one from a public dataset; here it is." Then give it.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Show me one.* Have a 90-second story ready.
- *What did you learn?* One honest lesson, for example cutting charts from six to two.

**Common mistakes:** Hiding the switch; claiming analyst experience you do not have; describing courses instead of output.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you structure a spoken data story so a busy manager remembers the main point?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Answer-first thinking and the ability to be brief.
**Explain simply:**
- Lead with the headline, because a busy listener may stop listening after 20 seconds.
- Give one or two pieces of evidence, not ten.
- End with the action you recommend and what you need.
- Repeat the headline in one short line at the end.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Write the point in one sentence: "X is happening, so we should do Y."
2. Pick the single strongest number or comparison as evidence.
3. Add one "why" (cause or context).
4. State the decision or action and who owns it.
5. Close by repeating the headline.
6. Cut anything the listener could not act on.
**Worked example or script:** "Early leavers are our biggest attrition problem: 32 percent of people with under a year of tenure left, versus about 11 percent of others (exercise numbers). My recommendation: add a structured 30-60-90 day check-in for new joiners. Can we pilot it with two teams? In short, new joiners are leaving, and early check-ins are the first fix to test."

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What if they ask a deep question mid-way?* Answer briefly, then return to the storyline.
- *What if you have two findings?* Lead with the one that drives a decision.

**Common mistakes:** Starting with method; reading every number; ending with "so that is it" and no action.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you show in an interview, and not just claim, that you are a strong communicator?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your answers demonstrate the skill.
**Explain simply:**
- Your answers are the evidence: structure, brevity, clarity.
- Give the answer first, then support it.
- Check understanding and listen to the question fully.
- Use a short example instead of saying "I am a good communicator".
**Plan / steps:**
1. Pause two seconds before answering; it shows control.
2. Give the answer in one sentence, then one reason and one example.
3. Keep most answers under about 90 seconds.
4. Use plain words; explain any term the listener may not know.
5. If unsure, ask a clarifying question instead of guessing.
6. Close with a link to the role, then stop talking.
**Worked example or script:** Asked "Tell me about a project": "In short, I built a small attendance dashboard for [X purpose] that [result]. The problem was [one line]; I did [two lines]. What I would do differently is [one line]." That structure is itself proof you communicate well.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Can you explain that more simply?* Do it: this is your chance to show the skill.
- *Can you summarise in one line?* Always have a one-line version.

**Common mistakes:** Saying "I am a great communicator" then rambling; speeding up when nervous; not stopping after the answer.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Design a 30-day plan to improve your communication for analyst interviews if you can give one hour on weekdays and three hours on weekends. What do you do each week?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your plan is realistic, specific and measurable.
**Explain simply:**
- Time budget: about 5 weekday hours plus 6 weekend hours is roughly 11 hours a week, about 44 over four weeks (exercise assumption of 4 full weeks).
- Practice aloud beats reading about communication.
- Record, review, repeat.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Week 1, baseline: record a 2-minute "tell me about yourself" and a 2-minute project explanation; score them on a checklist (answer first, under time, plain words, clear end). Weekdays: 20 min recording and review, 40 min reading on structure. Weekend: write your 90-second project story.
2. Week 2, structure: practise answer-first with 5 common questions daily (10 min each day). Weekend: do one mock interview with a friend and get written feedback.
3. Week 3, data stories: take a public HR dataset, write a one-page memo and say it aloud in 90 seconds. Weekend: build a 5-slide version.
4. Week 4, pressure: do impromptu drills and two full mocks. Re-record the week 1 answers and compare scores.
5. Each Sunday: write what improved and what is the single next fix.
**Worked example or script:** Scorecard: out of 10 points (answer first, time, clarity, examples, ending), week 1 might be 4, week 4 target 7 (invented targets; your own scores matter, not these).

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What if you miss days?* Do a shorter 15-minute version rather than skipping.
- *How will you know it worked?* Compare week 1 and week 4 recordings.

**Common mistakes:** Plans that are all reading; no recording; no review day.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You have no analyst job experience. How do you build real confidence before interviews, and not just pretend to be confident?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your confidence comes from preparation and evidence.
**Say this in the interview:** Confidence comes from reps: projects completed, answers rehearsed aloud, mocks done, and being honest about gaps.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I am very confident, I can do anything."
**Why it is weak:** It is a claim with no support, and it sounds like pretending.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "I do not have analyst job experience, so I built evidence. I completed [a project] using [tools], I can explain every step, and I have done [number] mock interviews. I know my gaps, for example [one gap], and I am closing them with [plan]. That is where my confidence comes from: preparation I can show, not acting."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "I did an attrition analysis on a public dataset, practised the story 10 times aloud, and did 3 mocks with friends. Because I know exactly what I can and cannot explain, I stopped fearing surprise questions."
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: no experience. Task: build real confidence. Action: projects, rehearsal, mocks, gap list. Result: calmer, more honest answers.
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**If you have no direct experience:** Start now: finish one small project, do your first mock, and list what you can and cannot explain. Say "I am building this" rather than claiming more.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What if you do not know the answer?* Say what you do know, then how you would find out.
- *What if you panic?* Pause, breathe, ask for a moment.

**Common mistakes:** Faking certainty; avoiding mocks; practising only silently.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What does a good two-minute spoken explanation of an insight sound like, and how do you practise it every day?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clear, structured, short explanation of a finding.
**Explain simply:**
- Two minutes is about 250 to 300 spoken words at a calm pace (rough rule of thumb, not measured).
- Order: headline, evidence, meaning, action.
- Plain words; no tool talk.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Pick one chart or finding a day (from a dataset or news).
2. Write the headline in one sentence.
3. Set a 2-minute timer and speak it aloud, recording.
4. Listen once: was the headline first? Any filler? Over time?
5. Re-record once, fixing one thing only.
6. Log the date, time taken and one fix.
**Worked example or script:** "Early attrition is the issue. Of 150 people with under one year of tenure, 48 left (32 percent), compared with 36 of 330 others (about 11 percent). That is nearly three times higher, which points at onboarding rather than pay. I would test a structured first-90-day check-in with two teams. That is the one thing I would start with." (exercise numbers)

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What if you run over?* Cut the second piece of evidence first.
- *How do you handle questions?* Short answer, then point back to the headline.

**Common mistakes:** Opening with background; reading numbers out; no action.

### Q10 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you honestly assess your own storytelling and presentation skills, and what scoring checklist do you use?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can measure yourself objectively.
**Explain simply:**
- Self-assessment needs a recording and a checklist, not a feeling.
- Score each item 0, 1 or 2 so progress is visible.
- Ask one other person to score the same recording.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Record a 3-minute talk.
2. Score: headline first; one clear message; one piece of evidence; plain words; pace and pauses; eye line or camera look; ending with action; finished on time.
3. Eight items at up to 2 points is a maximum of 16; note your total.
4. Ask a friend to score blind; compare differences.
5. Pick the lowest-scoring item as your one focus.
6. Re-score after a week.
**Worked example or script:** Your scores: 1,1,2,1,0,1,0,2 = 8 of 16 (invented). Focus: "ending with action" and "pace and pauses". Next week target: 11 of 16.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Is the checklist reliable?* It is a practice tool, not a measure of how an interviewer will score you.
- *What if the friend scores lower?* Trust the gap and examine it.

**Common mistakes:** Scoring from memory; fixing five things at once; not re-scoring.

### Q12 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What 15-minute daily habit would you set to improve how you communicate, and how will you keep it going for 30 days?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Realistic habit design and follow-through.
**Explain simply:**
- Small, same-time, tied to an existing habit.
- Visible tracking keeps you going.
- A missed day gets a 5-minute version, never zero.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Minutes 0-3: pick a question or chart.
2. Minutes 3-8: speak the answer aloud and record it.
3. Minutes 8-12: listen, note one thing to fix.
4. Minutes 12-15: re-record the fixed version.
5. Keep a simple calendar and tick each day.
6. Fixed anchor: right after morning tea, say.
7. Weekly reward and a Sunday review.
**Worked example or script:** 15 minutes a day over 30 days is 450 minutes, or 7.5 hours (computed). "If I miss a day, I do the 5-minute version so the streak stays alive."

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What if it gets boring?* Change the question set weekly.
- *How do you know it works?* Compare day 1 and day 30 recordings.

**Common mistakes:** Making it 60 minutes; skipping the review; no tracking.

### Q13 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is business acumen, and how would you show it as a junior analyst with no analyst experience?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Business-case prompts in prep articles test clarifying questions and linking metrics to goals (unverified prep sites).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand what the business cares about and link data to decisions.
**Explain simply:**
- Business acumen is understanding how the business makes money, spends it and decides.
- For an analyst it means asking "what decision will this support?"
- You can show it from HR: you know cost, headcount, attrition and hiring drivers.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Learn the basics of the company or industry you are applying to.
2. For each metric, ask: who uses it, what decision, what threshold.
3. In answers, ask a clarifying question about the goal first.
4. Tie your project to a cost or time impact.
5. Read news daily (10 min) and note one business lesson.
**Worked example or script:** "If asked to look at attrition, I would first ask what the business wants to protect: critical roles, cost, or a specific team. Then I would size the cost, for example [X] exits at [Y] replacement cost, before suggesting any fix."

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *How would you show it in a project?* Frame the project around a decision.
- *Example of a business metric?* Cost per hire, time to hire, attrition rate.

**Common mistakes:** Treating it as MBA knowledge; skipping the "so what"; naming metrics with no decision.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you practise structured thinking in everyday life, and not only in interviews?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether structure is a habit, not a performance.
**Explain simply:**
- Structure means: say the point, group the reasons, order them.
- Everyday decisions are free practice.
- Grouping and ordering are the two core muscles.
**Plan / steps:**
1. When you decide something (a purchase, a trip), write the answer plus three reasons.
2. Group reasons: cost, time, risk.
3. When you read news, summarise it in one sentence.
4. When you explain something to family, answer first.
5. Draw a tiny issue tree for one daily problem each week.
6. Notice when you ramble and restart with the point.
**Worked example or script:** "Should I take the bus or auto? My answer: bus. Reasons: cheaper by about [X], similar time, and no traffic stress. Risk: crowding, so I leave 10 minutes earlier." That is structure in 15 seconds.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Does it get natural?* With repetition it tends to; keep reps small.
- *Example in HR work?* Structure a policy question into rules, impact and exceptions.

**Common mistakes:** Practising only in mocks; building trees with overlapping branches; over-structuring simple chats.

### Q15 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you structure a professional email so that a busy stakeholder reads it and replies?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clear writing and consideration for the reader.
**Explain simply:**
- Bottom line up front: the ask or point in line one.
- Short subject line saying what you need and by when.
- One email, one main ask.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Subject: "Decision needed by Fri: [topic]".
2. Line 1: what you need, and by when.
3. Two or three lines of context or numbers.
4. Clear next step: who does what.
5. Attachment named and mentioned.
6. Read once as the receiver; cut anything unneeded.
**Worked example or script:**
"Subject: Please confirm by Friday: headcount figures for the Q3 report
Hi Meera, could you confirm that the attached headcount (480) matches your records by Friday? One thing to check: 12 contractors are excluded. If correct, I will finalise the report on Monday. Thanks, [Name]"

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *How long should it be?* Short enough to read on a phone.
- *Follow-up if no reply?* One polite nudge with the same ask.

**Common mistakes:** Vague subject; hiding the ask at the end; stock phrases like "do the needful".

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟢 As a career switcher you may feel like an impostor next to people with years of analytics experience. How do you manage that feeling?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Resilience and honest self-perception.
**Say this in the interview:** Acknowledge the feeling, say what you do about it, and point to your own strengths.

**Weak answer (what not to say):** "I never feel that way, I am as good as anyone."
**Why it is weak:** Not believable and shows low self-awareness.
**Strong answer (template, speak it in your own words):** "Yes, sometimes, especially around [technical areas]. I manage it by keeping a list of what I have actually done: [projects, skills]. I also remind myself that my HR background gives me [domain knowledge]. When I am behind, I treat it as a learning list, not a verdict: this month I am working on [skill]."
**Illustrative example, replace with your own true story:** "When I first compared myself with analysts who knew advanced SQL, I felt behind. I wrote down what I could already do and what I needed next, and spent 30 minutes a day on the next item. My HR knowledge helped me spot that a headcount figure looked wrong."
**STAR breakdown:** Situation: comparison with experienced people. Task: manage the doubt. Action: evidence list and learning plan. Result: steadier focus.
**Say this only if it is true for you. If you have no direct example, use the no-experience version below.**
**If you have no direct experience:** Say how you would handle it: keep an evidence list, set small goals, ask for feedback, and use HR knowledge as a real advantage.

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Does it affect your work?* It makes me prepare, not freeze.
- *What helps most?* A written list of real wins.

**Common mistakes:** Denying it; over-sharing anxiety; apologising for the background.

### Q17 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Here is a finding: employees with under one year of tenure left at 32 percent a year, against 11 percent for everyone else. Give me the 60-second spoken version for the HR head, with the headline, one piece of evidence and one recommended action.
**Inline data:** 480 employees total, 150 with under one year of tenure; 48 of the 150 left; 36 of the remaining 330 left. Check the rates before you speak.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Rate checking and a crisp answer-first message.
**Explain simply:**
- Check rates first: 48/150 = 32.0 percent; 36/330 = 10.9 percent, which rounds to 11 percent. The question's numbers check out.
- Overall: 84/480 = 17.5 percent.
- Early leavers are 48 of 84 exits, about 57 percent, from 31 percent of staff (150/480).
- 32 is about 2.9 times 10.9; that is about 21 percentage points higher.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Headline: new joiners leave much more.
2. Evidence: 32 percent versus 11 percent.
3. Action: test a structured first-90-day programme.
4. Note the caveat: one period only, no cause shown yet.
**Worked example or script:** "Our biggest attrition risk is the first year. Of 150 people with under a year of tenure, 48 left, which is 32 percent; for the other 330, 36 left, about 11 percent. That is nearly three times higher, and these new joiners make up 57 percent of all exits from 31 percent of staff. I recommend a structured 30-60-90 day check-in pilot, and exit-interview coding for first-year leavers, to confirm the cause. Can we start with two teams?" (about 90 words, about 40 seconds)

**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Is this causal?* No; it shows where to look, so I would check exit reasons and team-wise splits.
- *Sample size?* 150 is workable but I would check by team before acting.

**Common mistakes:** Quoting 11 and 32 without checking; claiming a cause; no action.

### Q18 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Rewrite this email so it is clear and professional.
**Inline data:** "Hi, as discussed kindly find attached the file. Please revert at the earliest. Some numbers may look off, do the needful."
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clear ask, deadline, honesty about data quality.
**Explain simply:**
- Problems: no named file, no deadline ("at the earliest"), "revert" and "do the needful" are vague, and "numbers may look off" gives no specifics.
- Fix: say what the file is, what you need, by when, and exactly which numbers to check.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Write a specific subject.
2. State the ask and deadline.
3. Name the doubtful numbers.
4. Offer a next step.
**Worked example or script:**
"Subject: Please review attrition file by Thursday 5 pm
Hi [Name], attached is the [monthly attrition file] we discussed. Could you review it and reply by Thursday 5 pm? Two figures look unusual: [the March exits count] and [the Team A headcount]. Please confirm or correct them. If I do not hear back, I will keep the current values and flag them in the report. Thanks, [Name]"
(Placeholders are for you to fill; the original did not give them.)

**Likely follow-ups:**

### Q41 ⭐ 🔴 Take the problem "overtime cost rose by 20 percent this quarter". Draw an issue tree out loud with at most three branches and say which branch you would check first and why.
**Inline data:** overtime cost = overtime hours x average hourly rate; if hours rose 12 percent and rate rose 7 percent, cost rose about 20 percent (1.12 x 1.07 = 1.198).
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); issue trees and MECE are standard in case-style prep (consulting prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Structured thinking: non-overlapping branches and a reasoned first step.
**Say this in the interview:** "Cost is hours times rate, so I split it into three branches: hours, rate, and mix of who works the overtime. I check hours first."
**Explain simply:**
- MECE: branches do not overlap and together cover the problem.
- The formula gives the tree: cost = hours x rate.
- Check: 1.12 x 1.07 = 1.1984, so about 19.8 percent, which rounds to the stated 20 percent.
**Plan / steps:**
1. Restate: "Overtime cost is up about 20 percent."
2. Branch 1, hours: more overtime hours (by team, shift, reason).
3. Branch 2, rate: higher hourly rate (pay revision, more senior people).
4. Branch 3, mix: who does the overtime (higher-paid roles more).
5. Choose the first: hours, because it is usually the larger lever and the easiest to verify, and the data shows hours rose 12 percent.
6. Say what you would do next: split hours by team and reason.
**Worked example or script (invented numbers):** Last quarter: 1,000 hours at Rs 200 = Rs 2,00,000. This quarter: 1,120 hours at Rs 214 = Rs 2,39,680, up 19.84 percent. Note: mix is partly inside the average rate, so say that honestly.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not start with rate?* It rose less and has a fixed policy cause. 2) *What if branches overlap?* Redraw them; overlap leads to double counting.
**Common mistakes:** Four to six overlapping branches; no reason for the first branch; starting with data pulls.
