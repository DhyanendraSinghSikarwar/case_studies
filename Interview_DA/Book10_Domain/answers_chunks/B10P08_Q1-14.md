### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You are joining a team in an industry you have never worked in, such as banking or healthcare. How do you learn the business in your first two weeks?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); similar "learn a new domain fast" questions appear in public question banks (Dataford, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a calm, structured method instead of panic or "I will just google it".
**Say this in the interview:** "In week one I learn the words and the money flow: who the customers are, what is sold, how the company earns. In week two I learn the data: which reports exist, which tables feed them, and which three numbers the team watches. I ask a lot of questions and write everything in a glossary. I do not change any report until I understand why it exists."
**Explain simply:**
- **Week 1, words and map:** read the company's public pages and any onboarding documents; start a glossary (a list of terms in plain words); sketch one page: customers, products, how money comes in, where data is created (see Q5).
- **Week 2, data and people:** list the existing reports and dashboards (an inventory); find out which table feeds each; sit with 2 or 3 colleagues who use those reports and ask what decision each report supports.
- **Rule:** listen before changing anything. A strange-looking number often has a business reason.
- **HR parallel:** like learning a new company's org chart, job families and pay cycle before you analyse its headcount.
- **Suggested split:** about 40% reading, 40% talking to people, 20% looking at data.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What is your first deliverable?* A one-page business map plus a glossary, shared with the team to be corrected. 2) *What if no one has time?* Learn from existing reports and tickets, and book short 15-minute slots with a list of questions ready.
**Common mistakes:** Diving into tables before knowing the business; trying to learn everything at once; criticising old reports early.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You come from HR. How are you building knowledge of the industry you are applying to, and what from your HR background carries over?
**Asked at:** commonly asked of career switchers (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty about the gap, evidence that you are already closing it, and whether you see your past work as an asset.
**Say this in the interview:** (Template: adapt with what you have truly done; do not claim experience you lack.) "I am learning [the domain] in a planned way: I read [real source you used], keep a glossary, and I am practising on a public dataset. From HR I bring comfort with sensitive data, working with many stakeholders, and thinking in headcount, attrition and cost, which are people metrics every industry needs."
**Explain simply:**
- **What carries over:** handling confidential data carefully; explaining numbers to non-technical managers; metrics like attrition, absenteeism, cost per hire, which have close cousins elsewhere (customer churn is attrition for customers).
- **Process knowledge:** you know how a business runs its people processes end to end (hire, pay, exit), the same way you will learn its customer or order processes.
- **What you are building:** vocabulary, 5 to 8 core metrics, one dataset project (see Q14 and Q17 for plans). Name only what you have really done so far.
- **Be specific:** one real sentence beats ten claims, for example "I computed churn on a public bank dataset and wrote down three questions I would ask the business."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not stay in HR analytics?* Answer truthfully with your own reasons; HR analytics is also a valid domain (Q13). 2) *What is your biggest gap?* Name one and say how you are closing it.
**Common mistakes:** Hiding the HR background; listing courses without any output; saying "I learn fast" with no example.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What goes into a personal domain glossary in the first week, and how do you keep it accurate?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "glossary first" is standard advice in analyst onboarding guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you capture meaning precisely and check it with the business, not with guesses.
**Say this in the interview:** "Each entry has the term, a plain-words meaning, an example, how it appears in data (table or column), who confirmed it and when. I keep it accurate by confirming each term with someone who uses it daily and noting where definitions differ between teams."
**Explain simply:**
- **Columns to keep:** term, short meaning, example, related metric, where in data, source, confirmed by, date, confidence (sure, unsure).
- **First-week terms:** the 20 to 30 words you hear most, in meetings and report headers, plus abbreviations.
- **Accuracy habits:** mark each entry "unconfirmed" until a colleague agrees; record differing definitions side by side (for example, two teams may count "active customer" differently); review weekly and delete or fix errors.
- **HR parallel:** like a policy glossary where "employee", "contractor" and "full-time" must mean one thing in payroll reports.
- **Tip:** write terms in your own words first, then compare with the official wording.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if two experts disagree?* Record both, ask which definition the report uses, and label metrics with it. 2) *Where do you store it?* Any shared sheet or notes page you can search.
**Common mistakes:** Copying definitions from the internet without checking company usage; no examples; never updating it.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 When you enter a new domain, how do you decide what to learn first and what to postpone?
**Asked at:** commonly asked; the wording "how did you decide what to learn first" appears in public question banks (Dataford, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Prioritisation: do you tie learning to the job to be done rather than to curiosity.
**Say this in the interview:** "I learn first what my next deliverables need: the vocabulary of the questions I will be asked, the metrics on the team's dashboard and the tables behind them. I postpone rare edge cases, deep regulation detail and other departments' processes until a task needs them."
**Explain simply:**
- **Learn now:** the main customer, the main product, the money flow, the top 5 metrics, the 3 to 5 core tables.
- **Learn soon:** how data is produced (where errors enter), exceptions such as returns or reversals.
- **Postpone:** legal fine print, rare products, history of the industry, tools you are not asked to use.
- **Method:** list what you do not know, score each item by "how soon will my work need it" and "how costly is a mistake". Study high-high items first.
- **Check:** ask your manager "what are the three questions I will be asked in month one?" and let that steer you.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you pick wrong?* Review weekly and re-rank. 2) *Do you ever learn something not needed?* Yes, briefly, when it explains a surprising number.
**Common mistakes:** Starting with textbooks end to end; ignoring the manager's priorities; postponing everything that feels hard.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you map a new business: its customers, products, main processes, money flow and the places where its data is created?
**Asked at:** commonly asked; entity and top-level process mapping is recommended for entering a domain in BA community material (Modern Analyst, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you can turn a business into a simple picture connecting people, products, money and data.
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw one page with five boxes: who the customers are, what they buy, the main process from first contact to money received, where money comes in and goes out, and at each step which system records data. That picture tells me which tables to look for."
**Explain simply:**
- **Customers:** who pays and who uses (they can differ, as with a company buying health cover for staff).
- **Products:** list the 3 to 5 main ones and how each earns.
- **Process:** a simple chain, for example lead, onboarding, use, billing, support, exit.
- **Money flow:** revenue sources, big costs, timing (paid upfront or later).
- **Data creation points:** each step is a form, app, machine or person that writes a record; note the system name and the timestamp it stores.
- **Worked sketch (template, adapt to your target domain):** for an online shop: customer, order, payment, warehouse, delivery, return; data at each step as an order record, a payment record and a delivery status.
- **HR parallel:** hire to retire: requisition, offer, joining, payroll, exit, each with its system.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you check the map?* Show it to an insider and fix it. 2) *Which part do you map deepest?* The one your reports cover.
**Common mistakes:** A map with no data points; copying an org chart instead of a process; making it too detailed.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Tell me about a time you had to learn an unfamiliar subject quickly and then use it in your work.
**Asked at:** commonly asked behavioural question (MockQuestions and Dataford list similar prompts, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A real, specific story showing method, speed and a result.
**Say this in the interview:** (Spoken template: fill every bracket with your own true experience; never invent one.) "In [year/role], I had to [task] about [unfamiliar subject]. I first [listed what I did not know], then [talked to one expert, read one source], and within [time] I could [produce a result]. The outcome was [real result]. What I would repeat is [one habit]."
**Explain simply:**
- **Structure (STAR):** Situation (one line), Task, Action (your method, 3 steps), Result (a number or a clear outcome if you really have one).
- **HR ideas you may truly have lived:** learning a new payroll or statutory rule for a compliance task, learning a new HRIS (human resources information system) module, picking up Excel pivot tables to produce a report.
- **Keep it short:** about 90 seconds.
- **Show learning method:** glossary, expert conversation, practice, then a check with someone.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would you do differently?* Name one real improvement. 2) *How did you know you had understood?* Someone confirmed the output or you got it right on a test case.
**Common mistakes:** A vague story; claiming results you cannot support; a story with no learning method.

### Q7 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Which free resources would you use to learn a new business domain from zero?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know reliable sources and use them with a method.
**Say this in the interview:** "I start with the company's and industry's own public material: annual reports, regulator and industry-body websites, glossaries; then free course videos and beginner articles; then datasets to practise. I cross-check any definition with a second source."
**Explain simply:** (All items from memory, unverified, check the link before relying on them.)
- **Company material:** annual report, investor presentation, earnings call transcript, product pages, careers page (see Q18).
- **Industry glossaries and explainers:** Investopedia for finance terms; Khan Academy finance and economics videos; the websites of regulators and industry bodies for official terms (for India, central bank and insurance regulator sites; confirm current pages).
- **Courses:** free-to-audit courses on Coursera or edX in finance, healthcare data or supply chain basics (audit availability changes).
- **Data practice:** Kaggle datasets (see Q8) and public data portals.
- **Community:** industry subreddits, LinkedIn posts, YouTube walkthroughs; treat as unverified opinion.
- **Self-test:** after each source, write 5 terms in your glossary and 1 question for an expert.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you trust a source?* Prefer official or primary ones; confirm with a second. 2) *How long per week?* See Q14 for a plan.
**Common mistakes:** Collecting links without reading; trusting one blog; no notes.

### Q8 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Which free public datasets would you use to practise analysis in domains such as banking, e-commerce, healthcare and supply chain?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have actually practised on domain-shaped data and know each dataset's limits.
**Say this in the interview:** "For banking I would use the UCI Bank Marketing data; for e-commerce the Olist Brazilian orders data; for healthcare a synthetic dataset such as Synthea or CMS synthetic claims, never real patient data; for supply chain the DataCo Smart Supply Chain data."
**Explain simply:** (From memory, unverified, check the link, licence and column list before use.)
- **Banking:** UCI Bank Marketing (phone campaign of a Portuguese bank, customer outcome yes or no); Kaggle also hosts credit-risk datasets (names vary).
- **E-commerce:** Olist Brazilian E-Commerce (orders, items, payments, reviews, sellers, several linked tables, good for joins); UCI Online Retail (invoice-level sales).
- **Healthcare:** Synthea (synthetic patient records generator); CMS DE-SynPUF (synthetic Medicare claims). Real clinical sets such as MIMIC need credentialed access and training; do not assume they are open.
- **Supply chain:** DataCo Smart Supply Chain on Kaggle (orders, shipping, delay); M5 Walmart forecasting data from the Kaggle competition for demand.
- **HR:** IBM HR Analytics attrition sample on Kaggle (a fictional, synthetic set).
- **Portals:** Kaggle, UCI Machine Learning Repository, data.gov and data.gov.in.
- **Tip:** read the data description first; synthetic data teaches structure, not real-world rates.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can you quote findings as real industry facts?* No, many are old, sampled or synthetic. 2) *Which first?* The one closest to your target job.
**Common mistakes:** Using any dataset without reading its documentation; treating synthetic data results as real; ignoring licences.

### Q9 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Which people would you meet in your first month to learn a new domain, and what would you ask each of them?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you learn from several viewpoints and respect people's time.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would meet my manager, a senior analyst, a business user of our reports, an operations person who sees the real process, and the data engineer or owner of the main system. I ask each a few focused questions and share back what I understood."
**Explain simply:**
- **Manager:** what are the three priorities, and what does success look like in 90 days?
- **Senior analyst:** which tables and reports matter most; which numbers are often wrong and why?
- **Business user (sales, risk, ops):** what decision do you take with this report; what is missing?
- **Operations or front-line staff:** walk me through a normal day and what goes wrong.
- **Data engineer or system owner:** where is this data created; how often refreshed; known issues?
- **Compliance or privacy contact:** what data should I handle carefully?
- **HR parallel:** like a stay interview: ask open questions, listen, write it down.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long is each chat?* 20 to 30 minutes, with questions sent ahead. 2) *Whom first?* The manager, then whoever owns the data.
**Common mistakes:** Meeting only senior people; asking things already written in documents; not following up with thanks and a summary.

### Q10 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How much domain knowledge does a junior data analyst really need compared with tool skills?
**Asked at:** commonly asked; job-posting samples in this project's research list domain knowledge as good-to-have for most junior roles (research/market_skills_2026.md, small internal sample).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have realistic priorities: tools get you hired, domain makes you useful.
**Say this in the interview:** "For a junior, tools come first: SQL, Excel and clear reporting are tested directly. Domain knowledge needs to be enough to read a number correctly and ask smart questions: key terms, main metrics and the process. It deepens on the job."
**Explain simply:**
- **Tools** are checked in tests; weak SQL ends the interview.
- **Domain** helps you spot a wrong number, for example a churn rate that is impossible for the business.
- **Rule of thumb (judgement, not a measured figure):** roughly 70 to 80% effort on tools and 20 to 30% on one chosen domain until you are employed.
- **Domain-heavy roles** (for example claims or credit risk) expect more; the job description shows it.
- **Combined proof:** one project that uses a tool well on domain data shows both.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can tools alone get a job?* Often yes for entry roles, but domain sets you apart. 2) *What minimum?* 20 terms, 5 metrics, 1 process map.
**Common mistakes:** Studying only the domain and neglecting SQL; or ignoring the domain entirely.

### Q11 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A new company database has no data dictionary. How do you learn what each table and column means?
**Asked at:** commonly asked in analyst case rounds (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you investigate systematically rather than guess.
**Say this in the interview:** "I profile each table: row count, key columns, nulls, value ranges and sample rows. Then I read existing queries and reports to see how columns are used, trace one real record end to end, and confirm uncertain columns with the data owner. I write the dictionary as I go."
**Explain simply:**
- **Profile:** how many rows, what makes a row unique, which columns are mostly empty, min and max dates.
- **Read existing work:** old queries, report definitions and column names (for example `status`, `amt`).
- **Trace a record:** pick one known customer or order and follow it through tables; the story it tells reveals meaning.
- **Confirm:** ask the owner only about leftovers, with your guess written down ("I think `status = 3` means cancelled, correct?").
- **Document:** table, column, meaning, confidence, who confirmed.
**Data and tables involved:** start with the main business table and the tables it joins to; first cuts are counts, distinct counts, null counts and date ranges.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the owner has left?* Rely on reports, tests with known cases and colleagues. 2) *How do you spot a code column?* Few distinct values repeated many times.
**Common mistakes:** Assuming column names mean what they say; never confirming guesses; documenting nothing.

### Q12 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What do you ask a domain expert in a first meeting, and how do you prepare so you do not waste their time?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); advice on preparing for domain experts appears in BA training material (Bridging the Gap, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Preparation, respect for time and the quality of your questions.
**Say this in the interview:** "I read the public material and existing reports first, then bring five written questions: what decision this data supports, how key terms are defined, what usually goes wrong in the data, which numbers they trust, and who else I should talk to. I send a summary afterwards."
**Explain simply:**
- **Prepare:** one-page map (Q5), glossary draft, the report you want explained, and your own guesses.
- **Ask:** "What do you use this for?" "How do you define X?" "What would surprise you in this data?" "What did past analysts get wrong?"
- **During:** repeat back in your words to check understanding.
- **After:** send notes within a day and list open points.
- **Time:** ask for 20 to 30 minutes and keep to it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they are impatient?* Start with the single most important question. 2) *Do you record the meeting?* Only with permission.
**Common mistakes:** Asking what a quick search answers; no written follow-up; asking five questions in one sentence.

### Q13 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How would you choose which domain to aim for as a career switcher, for example HR tech, banking, e-commerce or healthcare?
**Asked at:** commonly asked of career switchers (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That your choice is reasoned: link to background, demand, interest and entry barriers.
**Say this in the interview:** (Template: use your true reasons.) "I weighed four things: how close it is to my background, how many openings I see for juniors, how interested I am, and how steep the entry barrier is. [Your honest choice and reason]."
**Explain simply:**
- **Background fit:** HR tech or people analytics reuses your HR knowledge directly.
- **Demand:** count entry-level job postings for each domain in your city over one week (your own tally; in this project's job sample, finance appears frequently, which is a small sample and may be stale).
- **Interest:** pick a domain whose news you can read for months.
- **Barrier:** banking and healthcare have more regulation and vocabulary; e-commerce data is often easier to find publicly for practice.
- **Method:** score each 1 to 5 on the four factors, pick the top, and test it with a 2-week trial using Q14.
- **Stay flexible:** the first domain is not forever (see Q27).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can you apply to several?* Yes, with one main story and one backup. 2) *What if you pick wrong?* Skills transfer; your notes system carries over.
**Common mistakes:** Choosing by salary rumours alone; spreading across four domains; ignoring HR as an asset.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Design a 30-day plan to learn a new domain if you can give one hour on weekdays and three hours on weekends.
Weekday hours: 5 x 1 = 5 per week. Weekend hours: 3 per week. Over 4 weeks: (5 + 3) x 4 = 32 hours.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); first 30/60/90-day onboarding guides are widespread (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Realistic planning, output at every step and a final proof.
**Say this in the interview:** (Template; adapt the domain.) "I have 32 hours in 30 days. Week 1 vocabulary and business map, week 2 metrics, week 3 a dataset and first analysis, week 4 finish a mini-project, write a one-page summary and test myself."
**Explain simply:**
- **Budget:** 5 weekday hours + 3 weekend hours = 8 hours a week; 8 x 4 = 32 hours (checked).
- **Week 1 (8 h):** weekday hours: read overview sources, build 25-term glossary; weekend: draw business map (Q5) and read one annual report.
- **Week 2 (8 h):** weekdays: learn 6 core metrics with a worked number each on paper; weekend: read an earnings call and match its metrics.
- **Week 3 (8 h):** weekdays: pick a free dataset (Q8), read its description, profile it in SQL or Excel; weekend: compute the metrics from week 2.
- **Week 4 (8 h):** weekdays: write a 1-page findings note and 3 questions you would ask the business; weekend: self-check test (20 marks, see Q19), fix gaps, practise a 2-minute spoken summary.
- **Daily habit:** 5 minutes at the end to update the glossary.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you miss a week?* Drop reading, keep the project. 2) *How do you know it worked?* Self-test score and a project you can explain.
**Common mistakes:** All reading and no output; no end date; skipping the self-test.
