# Chapter 10: Tables and Matrices for Information

**What you will learn:** how to read a table, fill in missing cells from totals, and solve grid puzzles where clues tell you who has what.

## Concept in plain language

### What is a table, and what is a matrix?

A **table** is information arranged in rows (going across) and columns (going down). A **matrix** is just a table where both the rows and the columns name things, so that each box answers a question about one row and one column together. A box is called a **cell**.

| | Permanent | Contract |
|---|---|---|
| Payroll | 8 | 3 |
| Recruitment | 4 | 5 |

Read it like a map reference: row first, then column. Here the cell for Payroll and Contract says that Payroll has 3 contract staff (illustrative numbers). The row says things about Payroll; the column says things about Contract staff.

### Totals are a free check

- A **row total** adds the cells across a row. A **column total** adds the cells down a column.
- The **grand total** in the corner is both the sum of the row totals and the sum of the column totals. If your two sums disagree, a number is wrong somewhere.
- To find a missing cell: take its row (or column) total and subtract the cells you already know. If a row has one missing cell, that cell is fixed. Work with whichever row or column has only one blank, and repeat.

### The logic grid: a table of yes and no

In a grid puzzle you are not given numbers. You are given clues like 'Omar works in Training', and you build a table to hold what you learn. Each cell is answered Yes (write a tick) or No (write a cross).

Three rules do most of the work:

1. **One tick per row and per column.** If a person has a tick in one column of a category, every other cell in that row for that category becomes a cross, and the same for the column.
2. **Last one standing.** If a row has crosses everywhere except one cell, that cell must be a tick.
3. **A negative clue is a clue.** 'Naveen is not in Payroll' is one cross. A few crosses often force a tick.

### Puzzles with three or more categories

When there are three groups (say people, teams and shifts), pick one group as the anchor (usually the people) and give every other group its own column. Once Uma is in Payroll and Payroll works nights, Uma works nights: facts join through the anchor.

### Clues about order

Some clues say 'earns more than', 'one day after' or 'directly above'. Put the ordered things (days, floors, salary levels) as the rows or columns of your table and give each a slot number. 'Zoya is the day after Meera' then means: Zoya's slot is Meera's slot plus 1. Write inequalities as small chains such as `Chirag < Anil < Bhavna`.

### Method in six steps

1. Draw the grid (or a small table) with every group.
2. Fill in direct clues first ('Omar is in Training').
3. Add the crosses that follow from rule 1 above.
4. Use order clues to narrow down slots.
5. Apply last-one-standing; repeat until the table is full.
6. **Check:** read every clue once more against the finished table. A grid that satisfies every clue is the answer.

> **Why this matters for data work:** Real data lives in tables, and a pivot table or a summary report is a matrix of totals. Being comfortable reading a row against a column, filling a missing figure from totals, and checking that rows and columns add up to the same grand total is the quickest way to spot a wrong number in a report.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Who does which job? (two groups)

Three staff at a store, Kiran, Lata and Manoj, each do a different job: Cashier, Packer or Driver. (1) Manoj is the Driver. (2) Kiran is not the Cashier. Who does what?

**Step 1: draw the grid.** Rows are people, columns are jobs. *Why:* a grid shows at a glance which cells are still open.

**Step 2: use clue 1.** Manoj is the Driver: tick Manoj and Driver. By rule 1, cross the rest of Manoj's row and the rest of the Driver column. *Why:* a job has one person and a person has one job.

**Step 3: use clue 2.** Cross Kiran and Cashier. Kiran's row now has crosses under Cashier and Driver, so Kiran is the Packer (last one standing). Lata is left with Cashier.

| | Cashier | Packer | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kiran | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Lata | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| Manoj | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |

**Check:** (1) Manoj is the Driver, yes. (2) Kiran is the Packer, not the Cashier, yes.

### Example 2: Three groups

Neha, Om and Pooja each bought one item (Shirt, Shoes, Bag) and paid in a different way (Card, Cash, UPI). (1) The shoe buyer paid by card. (2) Om paid cash. (3) Pooja did not buy the shoes. (4) Om did not buy the shirt. Who bought what and paid how?

**Step 1: link two groups through the clue about shoes.** Shoes and Card belong to the same person. *Why:* clue 1 joins the item group to the payment group.

**Step 2: use clue 2.** Om paid cash, so Om did not pay by card, so Om did not buy the shoes (the shoe buyer paid by card). *Why:* a person cannot have two payment methods.

**Step 3: use clue 3.** Pooja did not buy the shoes either, so Neha bought the shoes and paid by card.

**Step 4: use clue 4.** Om bought neither the shoes nor the shirt, so Om bought the bag. Pooja is left with the shirt and with UPI.

| Person | Item | Payment |
|---|---|---|
| Neha | Shoes | Card |
| Om | Bag | Cash |
| Pooja | Shirt | UPI |

**Check:** all four clues hold. Notice that every clue was used, and removing any of them would leave more than one answer.

### Example 3: Filling a table from totals

Units sold (in hundreds, illustrative). Fill in the blanks.

| | Grocery | Clothing | Toys | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | 12 | ? | 9 | 30 |
| South | ? | 14 | 7 | 35 |
| East | 10 | 8 | ? | ? |
| Total | 36 | ? | 28 | 95 |

**Step 1: pick a row with one blank.** North has one blank: Clothing = 30 - 12 - 9 = **9**. *Why:* a row with one unknown can be solved on its own.

**Step 2: South.** Grocery = 35 - 14 - 7 = **14**.

**Step 3: use a column.** Toys column: East Toys = 28 - 9 - 7 = **12**. East Total = 10 + 8 + 12 = **30**.

**Step 4: the remaining totals.** Clothing total = 9 + 14 + 8 = **31**.

**Step 5: check the corner.** Row totals: 30 + 35 + 30 = 95. Column totals: 36 + 31 + 28 = 95. Both give 95, which matches the given grand total. *Why:* the two sums must agree; if they did not, one cell would be wrong.

### Example 4: Clues about order

Anil, Bhavna, Chirag and Divya earn different salaries. (1) Bhavna earns more than Anil. (2) Chirag earns less than Anil. (3) Divya earns more than Bhavna. List them from lowest to highest.

**Step 1: turn each clue into a small chain.** Clue 1: Anil < Bhavna. Clue 2: Chirag < Anil. Clue 3: Bhavna < Divya. *Why:* chains are easier to join than sentences.

**Step 2: join the chains** at the names they share: Chirag < Anil < Bhavna < Divya.

**Step 3: check that nothing is left loose.** Every person appears and every neighbouring pair is fixed by a clue, so the order is certain. Lowest is Chirag; highest is Divya.

### Example 5: Four people, two groups, order clues

Four trainers (Esther, Farhan, Gauri, Hemant) each teach one subject (Excel, SQL, Python, Stats) in a different session: Morning, Noon, Evening, Night (in that order through the day). (1) Farhan's session is earlier than Hemant's. (2) Hemant does not teach at night. (3) Stats is taught at night. (4) Gauri teaches in the morning. (5) Gauri does not teach Excel. (6) The SQL session is immediately after the Excel session.

**Step 1: put the sessions in order as the anchor** (Morning, Noon, Evening, Night). Gauri is Morning (clue 4) and Stats is Night (clue 3).

**Step 2: who is at night?** Not Gauri (Morning), not Hemant (clue 2). Farhan is earlier than Hemant, so Farhan cannot be last either (if Farhan were at night, nobody could be later). So Esther is at night and teaches Stats.

**Step 3: order Farhan and Hemant.** They take Noon and Evening, and Farhan is earlier: Farhan Noon, Hemant Evening.

**Step 4: subjects.** Excel, SQL, Python go to Morning, Noon, Evening. Gauri (Morning) is not Excel. If Excel were Evening, SQL would have to be Night, but Night is Stats. So Excel is Noon and SQL is Evening (immediately after). Python is left for Morning.

| Session | Trainer | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | Gauri | Python |
| Noon | Farhan | Excel |
| Evening | Hemant | SQL |
| Night | Esther | Stats |

**Check:** all six clues hold (Farhan Noon is earlier than Hemant Evening; Hemant is not Night; Stats is at Night; Gauri is Morning and teaches Python, not Excel; SQL in Evening follows Excel at Noon).

## 2 real-life scenarios

### Scenario 1 (commerce): where are the returns coming from?

An online store counts returned items last month by reason and category (illustrative numbers). Three cells were lost when the file was saved.

| | Shirts | Shoes | Bags | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wrong size | 48 | 30 | ? | 84 |
| Damaged | ? | 14 | 12 | 36 |
| Changed mind | 22 | ? | 22 | 60 |
| Total | 80 | 60 | 40 | 180 |

**Filling the gaps:** Wrong size, Bags = 84 - 48 - 30 = 6. Damaged, Shirts = 36 - 14 - 12 = 10. Changed mind, Shoes = 60 - 22 - 22 = 16. **Check with the columns:** Shirts 48 + 10 + 22 = 80, Shoes 30 + 14 + 16 = 60, Bags 6 + 12 + 22 = 40. All match.

**Reading the matrix:** 48 of the 80 shirt returns (60%) are for wrong size, but only 6 of the 40 bag returns (15%) are. In our suggestion, shirts are the sensible first place to look (for example, whether the size information on the shirt pages is clear), more so than bags. The gaps could be filled and the insight read only because rows and columns told the same story.

### Scenario 2 (HR): interview rooms

Three interviewers (Ravi, Sunita, Tej) each interview for one role (Analyst, Recruiter, Trainer) in one room (A, B, C). (1) Sunita is in Room B. (2) The Analyst interview is in Room A. (3) Tej is not interviewing for Recruiter. (4) The Trainer interview is not in Room C.

**Reasoning:** Sunita is in B, so Ravi and Tej take A and C. Room A holds the Analyst interview. The Trainer interview is not in C and not in A (A is Analyst), so it is in B: Sunita interviews for Trainer. The Recruiter interview is left for Room C. Tej is not the Recruiter, so Tej is in Room A (Analyst) and Ravi is in Room C (Recruiter).

| Interviewer | Role | Room |
|---|---|---|
| Ravi | Recruiter | C |
| Sunita | Trainer | B |
| Tej | Analyst | A |

**Check:** all four clues hold. A schedule built this way avoids double-booking: each interviewer, role and room appears once.

## Mistake log

1. **Forgetting rule 1.** After a tick, the rest of the row and column must be crossed. Skipping this is why puzzles seem to have too few clues.
2. **Treating a negative clue as unimportant.** 'X is not Y' is a real clue; write the cross.
3. **Misreading order words.** 'Earlier than' is not 'immediately before'; 'directly above' means exactly one floor.
4. **Not checking totals both ways.** A table can pass the row check and fail the column check; the grand total must match from both sides.
5. **Stopping before the final check.** Re-read every clue against your finished table; one wrong tick can survive until you do.

## Recap

- A table is rows and columns; a cell is where a row and a column meet.
- A missing cell in a table with totals is found by subtracting the known cells from the total.
- Row totals and column totals must give the same grand total; use that as your check.
- In a logic grid: tick, cross the rest of the row and column, and use last-one-standing.
- Turn order clues into slot numbers or small chains, and always check the answer against every clue.
