## Concept in plain language

### What is a seating puzzle?

A **seating puzzle** gives you a few people and a few facts about where they sit. Each fact on its own tells you very little. Together they usually leave **exactly one** possible arrangement. Your job is to draw the seats, place the people, and read the answer off the drawing.

The same skill works for any "who stands where" situation: a queue at a service counter, a meeting table, a training room with two facing rows.

### The three layouts

| Layout | What it looks like | What you draw |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear (a row)** | people side by side in a straight line | a line of numbered boxes |
| **Circular** | people around a round table | a circle with equal gaps |
| **Two-row** | two straight rows that face each other | two lines of boxes, one above the other |

There is also the **rectangular table** (people on both long sides and at the two short ends, called the **heads**). Treat it as a circle that has been stretched: you can walk around the edge and come back to where you started.

### Left and right: the whole chapter in one rule

"Left" and "right" always mean the left and right hand **of the person sitting there**, not yours. This is the number one source of slips, so this chapter fixes the conventions below. Learn them once and every puzzle becomes mechanical.

| Layout | Which way do people face? | Their right hand points to |
|---|---|---|
| Straight row | north (towards the top of the page) | east, which is the right side of your drawing |
| Round table, "facing the centre" | the middle of the table | the next seat **anticlockwise** (the opposite way to clock hands, seen from above) |
| Round table, "facing outward" | away from the middle | the next seat **clockwise** |
| Two rows, top row | south (down the page) | **west**, the left side of your drawing |
| Two rows, bottom row | north (up the page) | **east**, the right side of your drawing |
| Rectangular table | the centre of the table | the next seat anticlockwise, as in the round table |

Why does the top row of a two-row layout flip? Stand up, face the bottom of this page: your right hand now points to the left side of the page. The two rows face each other, so their right hands point opposite ways.

> Look at the diagram before you trust your memory. If a clue says "Kim sits to the right of Lee", first ask which way **Lee** is facing.

### Words you will meet

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **immediately right of X** | the seat touching X on X's right-hand side |
| **somewhere left of X** | on X's left side, but not necessarily touching |
| **second to the right of X** | skip one person, take the next: two seats along |
| **between A and B** (in a row) | in a seat with A on one side and B on the other |
| **exactly 2 between A and B** | 2 people sit in the gap, so their seat numbers differ by 3 |
| **next to / neighbour** | touching, on either side |
| **opposite** | across the table or across the gap in a two-row layout |
| **diagonally opposite** | in the other row, one seat to the side of the person facing you |
| **ahead of / behind** (queues) | closer to the front / closer to the back |

Handy arithmetic:

- If there are n people in a row and someone is a-th from the left, they are (n - a + 1)-th from the right.
- The number of people **between** seat a and seat b is the difference of the two seat numbers, minus 1.
- At a round table with an even number of people, the person opposite you is half the table away. With 6 people that is 3 seats round, with 8 people it is 4 seats.
- At a round table only the **relative** positions matter. Spin the whole table and nothing has changed, so you may place the first person anywhere you like and build from there.

### The method (five steps)

1. **Draw the layout first**: numbered boxes, a circle of dots, or two lines.
2. **Place the fixed facts first**: "sits at the left end", "is in seat 3", "sits opposite X". These are anchors.
3. **Handle clues that join people in blocks**: "A is immediately right of B" means the two must be written as a pair, B then A.
4. **Use the vague clues to choose between the options that are left**: "somewhere left of", "not next to". A "not" clue is used to cross out options.
5. **Check every clue against your final drawing**, one by one. This takes thirty seconds and catches nearly every mistake.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts constantly place items in an order or position under rules: shift rosters, queue positions, delivery slots, seat allocation, ranking rows in a report. The habit of fixing anchors, listing what is still possible, and re-checking every rule against the result is the same habit used to test whether a set of requirements has exactly one valid answer, none, or several.

## Five solved examples

### Example 1: a row of four

**Problem.** Pilar, Quincy, Rolf and Saskia sit in seats 1 to 4 of a row (seat 1 at the west end, everyone facing north). (1) Saskia sits at the east end. (2) Saskia sits immediately right of Pilar. (3) Quincy does not sit in seat 1. Who sits in seat 2?

1. Draw four boxes: `[1] [2] [3] [4]`. *Why:* a drawing keeps the facts in one place.
2. Clue 1: Saskia goes in seat 4. *Why:* it is a fixed anchor, the safest place to start.
3. Clue 2: Pilar is immediately to the left of Saskia, so Pilar is in seat 3. *Why:* "immediately" means touching, with no gap.
4. Seats 1 and 2 remain for Quincy and Rolf. Clue 3 says Quincy is not in seat 1, so Quincy is in seat 2 and Rolf in seat 1. *Why:* a "not" clue crosses out one option, and one option is all that is left.
5. Check: Rolf, Quincy, Pilar, Saskia. Saskia is at the end, Pilar touches her, Quincy is not in seat 1. All three clues hold.

**Answer:** Quincy. (This is the only arrangement that fits; all 24 possible orders were tested by script.)

### Example 2: a row of five with "exactly one between"

**Problem.** Wim, Xena, Yoel, Zita and Bram sit in a row of five seats, everyone facing north. (1) Exactly one person sits between Wim and Xena. (2) Yoel sits at the west end. (3) Zita sits immediately right of Xena. (4) Wim sits somewhere to the left of Bram. (5) Zita sits somewhere to the left of Wim. Who sits in the middle seat?

1. Draw `[1] [2] [3] [4] [5]`. Clue 2: Yoel in seat 1.
2. Clue 3 makes a block, `Xena Zita` (Xena first, Zita on her right).
3. Clue 5: Zita is left of Wim, so Wim is to the right of the block. The block `Xena Zita` therefore comes before Wim. *Why:* Zita is the right-hand member of the block, and Wim must be further right still.
4. Clue 1: exactly one person between Wim and Xena. Xena is the left end of the block and Zita is the person between. So the order is `Xena Zita Wim`, three seats side by side.
5. Seats 2, 3, 4 hold Xena, Zita, Wim. Bram takes seat 5, which agrees with clue 4 (Wim is left of Bram).
6. Check: Yoel, Xena, Zita, Wim, Bram. Clue 1: Zita is the only person between Xena and Wim. All five clues hold.

**Answer:** Zita. (All 120 orders were tested by script; exactly one fits.)

### Example 3: a round table of five

**Problem.** Hanne, Ilya, Jovan, Kemi and Leif sit around a round table, all facing the centre. (1) Ilya sits immediately right of Hanne. (2) Jovan sits immediately left of Hanne. (3) Kemi sits immediately right of Ilya. Who sits immediately left of Jovan?

1. Place Hanne anywhere. *Why:* spinning a round table changes nothing, so the first person can go anywhere.
2. Going anticlockwise from Hanne is her right-hand side. Put Ilya next, in her right-hand seat. *Why:* facing the centre, right means anticlockwise.
3. Jovan is on Hanne's left, which is the seat clockwise from her. That is the seat just before Hanne when you go round the table anticlockwise.
4. Kemi is immediately right of Ilya, so she is the next seat anticlockwise after Ilya.
5. The only seat left is for Leif. Going anticlockwise the order is: Hanne, Ilya, Kemi, Leif, Jovan, and then back to Hanne.
6. Jovan's left is the seat clockwise from him, which is Leif.

```
Anticlockwise order (right-hand direction):
Hanne -> Ilya -> Kemi -> Leif -> Jovan -> back to Hanne
```

**Answer:** Leif. (Checked by script; the arrangement is unique once Hanne's seat is fixed.)

### Example 4: two rows facing each other

**Problem.** Six people sit in two rows of three, with seats numbered 1 to 3 from the west. The north row faces south and the south row faces north, so seat 1 faces seat 1, and so on. (1) Mara sits at the east end of the north row. (2) Niko sits directly opposite Mara. (3) Olek sits at the west end of the south row. (4) Pru sits directly opposite Reva. (5) Reva sits in the south row. Who sits opposite Olek?

1. Draw two lines of three boxes.
2. Clue 1: Mara is north row, seat 3. Clue 2: Niko is south row, seat 3 (directly opposite means same seat number, other row).
3. Clue 3: Olek is south row, seat 1.
4. Reva is in the south row (clue 5), and the south row has only seat 2 free. So Reva is south seat 2.
5. Clue 4: Pru is opposite Reva, so Pru is north seat 2.
6. Quin is the only person left, so Quin takes north seat 1. *Why:* elimination, one person and one seat remain.

```
North row (faces south):  Quin  Pru   Mara
                           |     |     |
South row (faces north):  Olek  Reva  Niko
```

**Answer:** Quin. (All 720 placements were tested by script; one fits.)

### Example 5: queue arithmetic

**Problem.** In a queue, Kemal is 6th from the front and 9th from the back. Lena is 3rd from the back. How many people are in the queue, and how many people stand between Kemal and Lena?

1. Total people = (position from front) + (position from back) - 1 = 6 + 9 - 1 = 14. *Why:* Kemal is counted in both positions, so he is counted twice and we take one off.
2. Lena is 3rd from the back, so from the front she is 14 - 3 + 1 = 12th. *Why:* the conversion rule from the concept section.
3. People between Kemal (6th) and Lena (12th): 12 - 6 - 1 = 5.
4. Check by listing: positions 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 are between, which is 5 people.

**Answer:** 14 people in the queue, 5 between them.

## Two real-life scenarios

All numbers and roles below are invented (illustrative).

### Scenario 1: planning a meeting table (HR and data teams)

A six-person review meeting uses a rectangular table with a seat at each head and two seats on each long side. The six seats, going round the edge, are: West head, North 1, North 2, East head, South 2, South 1. The facilitator has these rules for the roles Chair, Presenter, Notes-taker, Finance lead, HR partner and IT lead:

1. The Chair sits at the West head.
2. The Presenter sits at the East head (so the screen is close).
3. The Notes-taker sits next to the Chair.
4. The Finance lead and the IT lead are not neighbours.

Counting every valid plan by script gives **8** plans, not one. A meeting planner meets this often: a rule set can be satisfied in many ways. Adding rules narrows it down:

5. The HR partner sits on the north side. This leaves **4** plans:

| Plan | West | North 1 | North 2 | East | South 2 | South 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Chair | Finance | HR | Presenter | IT | Notes |
| B | Chair | HR | Finance | Presenter | IT | Notes |
| C | Chair | HR | IT | Presenter | Finance | Notes |
| D | Chair | IT | HR | Presenter | Finance | Notes |

6. The Finance lead sits directly opposite the HR partner. In Plan A, Finance (North 1) faces Notes. In Plan B, Finance (North 2) faces IT. In Plan C, HR (North 1) faces Notes. Only Plan D works: Finance (South 2) faces HR (North 2).

Lesson: after each new rule, list what is still possible. When the list shrinks to one, you have a unique answer. If it reaches zero, the rules contradict each other and the facilitator must relax one.

### Scenario 2: queue management at a service counter (commerce)

A returns counter has one clerk who serves each customer in 4 minutes. Twelve customers are waiting, and customer number 9 asks how long until their turn starts. Customer 1 starts at minute 0.

- With one clerk, customer 9 has 8 people ahead, so the turn starts at minute 8 x 4 = 32.
- A second clerk opens at minute 12, working at the same speed. By minute 12, customers 1, 2 and 3 have started (at minutes 0, 4 and 8) and customer 4 starts right then.

| Customer | Starts at minute | Served by |
|---|---|---|
| 4 and 5 | 12 | clerk 1 and clerk 2 |
| 6 and 7 | 16 | clerk 1 and clerk 2 |
| 8 and 9 | 20 | clerk 1 and clerk 2 |

- Customer 9 now starts at minute 20, which is 12 minutes sooner than with one clerk.

Lesson: a queue is a row, so "position" arithmetic tells you the wait. A table of start times, written by hand, makes the effect of a change (a second clerk, someone leaving) easy to see and easy to check.

## Mistake log

1. **Using your own left and right.** Left and right belong to the person sitting there. In a two-row layout the two rows face opposite ways, so their right hands point opposite ways. Write the facing direction on your drawing.
2. **Treating "immediately" and "somewhere" alike.** "Immediately right of" means touching; "somewhere right of" means any seat further along. Mixing these up wipes out the one fact that fixes the answer.
3. **Forgetting that a circle has no start.** Spinning the table changes nothing, so fix one person anywhere. Also, the last seat is next to the first, so people at "both ends" of your list are neighbours.
4. **Miscounting "between".** The number between two seats is the gap in seat numbers minus 1. Seats 2 and 6 have 3 people between them, not 4.
5. **Not checking the final plan against every clue.** A drawing built from the first four clues can break the fifth. Re-read each clue against the finished picture.

## Recap

- Draw the layout first, then place anchors (fixed seats and opposite pairs), then blocks, then vague clues.
- Right and left belong to the sitter: check which way each row or person is facing.
- Round tables have no start, so fix one person anywhere; opposite seats are half the table apart.
- Position arithmetic in a queue: from front + from back - 1 = total, and people between = gap - 1.
- Re-check every clue against the finished arrangement, and list what is still possible after each new rule.

## Try it in a spreadsheet or SQL

A seating or queue plan is a small table: one row per person with a seat or position column. Sorting by that column gives the order, and a filter on the table shows who sits in a given place.
