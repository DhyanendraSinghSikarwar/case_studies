# Chapter 12: Syllogisms (All, Some, No; the Venn Method)

**What you will learn:** how to read statements such as "All...", "Some...", "No..." exactly, draw them as circles, and decide which conclusions are certain, which are only possible and which are impossible.

## Concept in plain language

### What is a syllogism?

A **syllogism** (say "SIL-uh-jiz-um") is a tiny argument made of two or more **statements** (the facts you are given) and a **conclusion** (a claim you must check). Your only job is to decide: does the conclusion **have to** be true, given only those statements?

Think of a security guard who only has a list of rules. The guard does not use what he knows about the world, only the list.

### Four ground rules for this chapter

| Rule | What it means |
|---|---|
| Believe the statements | Accept them even if they sound silly in real life. "All cats are bicycles" is allowed. Only the logic matters. |
| "Some" means "at least one" | "Some tea is hot" allows one cup, or all cups. It never says "not all". |
| Every named group has at least one member | If a statement mentions "interns", there is at least one intern. This is the usual convention in reasoning tests, and we use it in this book. |
| "Follows" means true in every possible picture | If you can draw even one picture that obeys the statements but breaks the conclusion, the conclusion does not follow. |

Each conclusion then gets one of three verdicts:

- **Follows (certain):** true in every picture that obeys the statements.
- **Possible (not certain):** true in some pictures and false in others.
- **False (impossible):** false in every picture.

### The four building blocks

A **group** is a collection of things, for example "invoices". Each group is drawn as a circle. A **member** is one thing inside the group.

| Statement | Plain meaning | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| All X are Y | Every X is also a Y | Circle X sits fully inside circle Y |
| No X are Y | Nothing is both | Circles X and Y are apart |
| Some X are Y | At least one thing is both | Circles X and Y overlap, with a mark in the overlap |
| Some X are not Y | At least one X lies outside Y | A mark inside X but outside Y |

Which ones can be turned round (reversed)?

| Statement | Can you swap X and Y? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Some X are Y | Yes: "Some Y are X" | The overlap is the same overlap |
| No X are Y | Yes: "No Y are X" | Apart is apart |
| All X are Y | **No** | X inside Y does not make Y inside X (all dogs are animals; not all animals are dogs) |
| Some X are not Y | **No** | The mark is in X; Y may have nothing outside X |

One more safe fact: if "All X are Y" is true and X has members, then "Some Y are X" is true too (the members of X are themselves in Y).

### Tricky wordings

| Wording | Means | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| Only X are Y | All Y are X | Circle **Y** inside circle X (the direction flips!) |
| None but X are Y | All Y are X | Same as "Only" |
| Every, each, any X is Y | All X are Y | X inside Y |
| Not all X are Y | Some X are not Y | A mark inside X, outside Y |
| X is never Y | No X are Y | Circles apart |

### The Venn method in five steps

1. **Draw one circle per group** named in the statements.
2. **Draw the statements.** Nest circles for "All", keep them apart for "No", overlap them for "Some".
3. **Draw loosely.** Place things apart or overlapping only when a statement forces it. Where nothing is forced, remember that the circles *could* overlap or not.
4. **Test the conclusion** against your picture.
5. **Try to break it.** Redraw the loose parts a different way. If any legal picture breaks the conclusion, it only "possibly" holds. If every legal picture agrees, it follows.

Step 5 is the step beginners skip, and it is the one that matters.

### Shortcuts for two statements (X and Z are the outer groups)

These come from the Venn method; you can always re-derive them. The right-hand column lists conclusions about X and Z that are certain.

| First statement | Second statement | Certain conclusions about X and Z |
|---|---|---|
| All X are Y | All Y are Z | All X are Z (and Some Z are X) |
| Some X are Y | All Y are Z | Some X are Z |
| All X are Y | No Y are Z | No X are Z |
| Some X are Y | No Y are Z | Some X are not Z |
| No X are Y | All Z are Y | No X are Z |
| All X are Y | Some Y are Z | Nothing certain |
| Some X are Y | Some Y are Z | Nothing certain |
| No X are Y | No Y are Z | Nothing certain |
| All X are Y | All Z are Y | Nothing certain |
| Some X are not Y | All Y are Z | Nothing certain |

(Every row was checked against all possible pictures by computer.)

### "Either I or II" (the either-or case)

Sometimes two conclusions are opposites, for example "All counters are open" and "Some counters are not open". One of them is always true, yet the statements do not say which. Neither is certain alone, but "either I or II" is certain. Choose it **only** when neither follows by itself.

> **Why this matters for data work:** Analysts constantly decide what a table or a rule really guarantees. "All flagged rows are duplicates" does not mean "all duplicates are flagged". Reading "all", "some" and "none" exactly protects you from wrong filters and wrong conclusions in reports.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: two "All" statements

**Statements:** All cheque payments are manual entries. All manual entries are approval items.
**Conclusions:** I. All cheque payments are approval items. II. All approval items are cheque payments.

1. **Draw:** the cheque-payments circle inside manual entries, which is inside approval items. *Why:* "All X are Y" always means a circle inside a circle.
2. **Test I:** cheque payments are inside the biggest circle, so I follows. *Why:* being inside a circle that is inside another circle means being inside both.
3. **Test II:** approval items could be bigger than cheque payments (other manual entries exist beyond the small circle). *Why:* the statements never say the circles are the same size, so II is only possible.

**Answer:** only I follows. (Computer check: I true in every picture, II true in some and false in others.)

### Example 2: "Some" with "All"

**Statements:** Some salespeople are bilingual people. All bilingual people are allowance receivers.
**Conclusions:** I. Some salespeople are allowance receivers. II. All salespeople are allowance receivers.

1. **Draw:** salespeople overlap bilingual people; the bilingual circle sits inside allowance receivers. *Why:* these are exactly the shapes of the two statements.
2. **Test I:** the salespeople inside the overlap are bilingual, hence inside allowance receivers. *Why:* a member of the small circle is also a member of the big one. I follows.
3. **Test II:** salespeople outside the overlap are not described at all. *Why:* nothing forces them into allowance receivers, so II is only possible.

**Answer:** only I follows.

### Example 3: "No" with "All"

**Statements:** No perishable goods are returnable items. All dairy products are perishable goods.
**Conclusions:** I. No dairy products are returnable items. II. Some returnable items are dairy products.

1. **Draw:** the perishable circle apart from returnable items; dairy products inside perishable. *Why:* "No" keeps circles apart, "All" nests them.
2. **Test I:** dairy products sit inside a circle that never touches returnable items, so they never touch it either. I follows.
3. **Test II:** it says dairy products and returnable items overlap, which the picture forbids. II is definitely false. *Why:* a conclusion that contradicts a drawn fact is impossible, not just uncertain.

**Answer:** only I follows; II is false.

### Example 4: two "Some" statements prove nothing

**Statements:** Some drivers are parents. Some parents are volunteers.
**Conclusions:** I. Some drivers are volunteers. II. Some volunteers are drivers.

1. **Draw picture A:** one person who is a driver, a parent and a volunteer. Both statements hold, and I and II are true.
2. **Draw picture B:** a parent who drives (not a volunteer), and a different parent who volunteers (not a driver). Both statements hold, but no driver is a volunteer. *Why:* the overlap with the middle circle can be in two different places.
3. **Conclude:** I and II are true in A and false in B, so both are only possible.

**Answer:** neither follows. (Computer check over 160 possible pictures.) Two "Some" statements never link the outer groups.

### Example 5: the either-or case

**Statement:** Some products are seasonal items.
**Conclusions:** I. All products are seasonal items. II. Some products are not seasonal items.

1. **Test I:** possible (picture: every product is seasonal). Not certain, since other pictures have non-seasonal products.
2. **Test II:** possible (picture with a non-seasonal product), but not certain either.
3. **Look at both together:** every picture has either every product seasonal, or at least one product not seasonal. There is no third option. *Why:* these two claims are exact opposites about the same group.

**Answer:** neither follows alone, but **either I or II** follows. (Computer check: neither is certain; at least one is true in every picture.)

## 2 real-life scenarios

All numbers below are invented and labelled illustrative.

### Scenario A: a training register (HR, illustrative)

A company has 120 employees (illustrative). Rules in the policy file: **All employees who completed fire-safety training are on the register. No one on the register has an expired badge.**

- 45 employees completed training, the register lists 60 people, and 20 employees have an expired badge (illustrative).
- **Can a trained employee have an expired badge?** Trained, so on the register; on the register, so not expired. No, it cannot. This is Example 3 in real clothes.
- **How many people on the register have not completed training?** The 45 trained people are inside the register's 60, so 60 minus 45 = 15.
- **Do all 20 expired badges sit outside the register?** Yes. The register holds 60, so 60 people are outside it, and all 20 expired badges are among those 60. The other 40 outside people have valid badges or no mention.
- **Trap:** "Everyone on the register is trained" does not follow. The 15 untrained register members show why.

### Scenario B: filtering a product table (data and commerce, illustrative)

A product table has 200 rows (illustrative). The data dictionary says: **Every row marked Fresh has a shelf-life value. No row with a shelf-life value belongs to Electronics.** Suppose 50 rows are Fresh and 80 rows have a shelf-life value (illustrative).

- **Does "No Fresh row is Electronics" hold?** Fresh sits inside shelf-life, which never meets Electronics. Yes.
- **How many rows have a shelf-life value but are not Fresh?** 80 minus 50 = 30.
- **At most how many Electronics rows could there be?** Electronics rows cannot have a shelf-life value, so they must be among the other 200 minus 80 = 120 rows. The most is 120.
- **Trap:** "Every row with a shelf-life value is Fresh" does not follow. The 30 rows show why.

(All counts were checked by listing every possible split of the rows by computer.)

## Mistake log

1. **Reversing "All".** "All coupons are offers" does not give "All offers are coupons". Check the direction of every circle.
2. **Joining two "Some" statements.** "Some A are B" and "Some B are C" never prove anything about A and C. The overlaps can sit in different places.
3. **Using real-world knowledge.** If a statement says "All kiosks are pop-ups", accept it. The question tests logic, not facts.
4. **Mixing up "Some X are not Y" and "No X are Y".** The first says at least one X is outside Y; the second says no X is inside Y. They are far apart.
5. **Stopping at the first picture.** A conclusion that works in one drawing may fail in another. Always try to redraw the loose parts.

## Recap

1. A syllogism asks what must be true from the given statements only; "follows" means true in every possible picture.
2. All = circle inside circle; No = circles apart; Some = circles overlap; Some not = a member outside.
3. Only "Some" and "No" can be reversed; "All" cannot, and "Only X are Y" means "All Y are X".
4. Two "Some" statements or two "No" statements prove nothing; check the shortcut table or redraw.
5. Draw loosely and try to break the conclusion; use "either I or II" only when neither follows alone.

## Try it in a spreadsheet or SQL

To test "All trained employees are on the register" on a real table, count the counter-examples (trained but not on the register). If the count is zero, the statement holds. For spreadsheets see Book 1 (COUNTIFS); for SQL see Book 2 (WHERE and COUNT).

```sql
-- Count trained employees who are NOT on the register.
-- If the answer is 0, "All trained employees are on the register" holds.
SELECT COUNT(*) AS counter_examples
FROM employees
WHERE completed_training = 1
  AND on_register = 0;
```

With six sample rows where one trained employee is missing from the register, this returns 1 (the statement fails). After removing that row, it returns 0 (the statement holds).

```
=COUNTIFS(B2:B7, "Yes", C2:C7, "No")
```

In Excel, column B holds "completed training" and column C holds "on register". The result is the same count of counter-examples.
