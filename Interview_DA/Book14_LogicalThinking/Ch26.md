# Chapter 26: Evaluating Claims, Sources and Evidence

**What you will learn:** a simple five-question routine for deciding whether a claim deserves your belief, how to judge a source, how to read "studies show" and "X% more" statements, and how to spot a forwarded message that should not be trusted or passed on.

## Self-check (3 questions)

1. A message says "Experts confirm: free recharge for everyone, forward to 10 friends today!" Name two warning signs.
2. A shampoo advert says "Reduces hair fall by 50%". What two extra facts do you need before the number means anything?
3. You want the number of employees who left last quarter. Which is the more reliable source: the dated HR system report, or a colleague's memory?

## Concept in plain language

### A claim is not a fact until it is checked

A **claim** is a statement that says something is true. Claims arrive everywhere: forwards on your phone, adverts, slides in meetings, a colleague in the corridor. **Evidence** is what you can point to that supports the claim. **A source** is where the claim or the evidence came from.

Your job is not to call every claim false. Your job is to decide **how much to believe it right now**, and to say what would change your mind.

### Four kinds of claims

| Kind | Example | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact about the world** | "The office has 120 employees" | Look at a records source |
| **Cause claim** | "The new roster cut errors" | Needs a fair comparison (see Chapter 24) |
| **Prediction** | "Sales will double next year" | Look at the reasoning and past accuracy; nobody can verify the future now |
| **Opinion or value** | "This is the best canteen" | Not true or false; ask for the criteria |

### The five-question routine

1. **WHO says it?** Is there a named person or organisation, and are they in a position to know? Do they gain from you believing it?
2. **WHAT evidence is given?** A number, a document, an example, or just "everyone knows"?
3. **HOW was it measured?** Who was asked or counted, how many, when, and what exactly was counted?
4. **WHAT is missing?** A comparison group, the denominator (out of how many), the time period, the cases that went the other way?
5. **CAN I CHECK it elsewhere?** Search for the original source yourself, in a new tab or by asking the owner. Do not rely on a link inside the message.

### Judging sources: closer is better

| Better | Weaker |
|---|---|
| Original record, dated, owned by someone accountable (an HR system export, an official notice on the organisation's own site) | A copy of a copy, no date, no author |
| Someone who measured it themselves and explains how | Someone repeating "I heard that..." |
| Several independent sources that agree | Many posts that all trace back to one unknown origin |
| A source with nothing to gain | A source that sells the thing they praise |

Many repeats are not many sources. If 20 posts all quote the same unnamed forward, you still have one claim.

### A ladder of evidence strength (weakest to strongest)

1. A single story or anecdote.
2. An expert's opinion without data.
3. A survey (check who answered and how many).
4. A comparison of groups seen in existing data (check for other explanations).
5. A controlled test where people are assigned by chance to the options being compared.

Higher rungs are not always possible, and a lower rung is not worthless. It simply tells you how cautious to be.

### Red flags in forwarded messages

- Urgency: "today only", "before midnight".
- A request to forward to many people.
- No author, no date, no named organisation.
- "Scientists say", "experts confirm", "officials announced" with no names.
- Strong emotion (fear or outrage) used instead of facts.
- A link that asks for money, passwords, bank or ID details.

### Numbers inside claims

- **Relative versus absolute change.** "Reduced by 50%" can mean from 2 in 1,000 to 1 in 1,000. Ask for both the starting and ending numbers.
- **Sample size.** Nine out of ten sounds strong; it may be ten people.
- **Who answered.** An online poll shared by a company shows the views of people who chose to answer.
- **Denominator.** "Most complaints came from branch A" tells you nothing until you know how big branch A is.

Three possible verdicts you can honestly give: **supported**, **not supported**, or **cannot tell yet** (with the evidence you would need).

> **Why this matters for data work.** Analysts constantly receive numbers and claims from others (a manager's slide, a vendor's brochure, an old report). Asking where a figure came from and how it was measured is part of the job, and it protects both you and your report from passing on mistakes.

## 5 solved examples

All claims below are invented for practice.

### Solved example 1: the forwarded ATM message

*Message: "URGENT: From tomorrow all bank ATMs will charge ₹50 per withdrawal. Forward to everyone you know!"*

1. **Who says it?** No author, no bank named. Without a source we cannot even ask who is accountable.
2. **What evidence?** None. No notice number, no date.
3. **Red flags.** Urgency, "forward to everyone", vague "all banks".
4. **Check elsewhere.** Open your own bank's website or app (typed in yourself) and look for a notice; or phone the bank.
5. **Verdict.** Cannot tell, and the message itself earns no trust. Do not forward until a bank's own notice confirms it. The "why": passing on an unverified message makes you the next link in the chain.

### Solved example 2: "9 out of 10 managers prefer our software"

*A vendor's brochure says this. The small print says: "Survey of managers who attended our demo day."*

1. **Who says it?** The seller, who gains if you believe it.
2. **Evidence:** a survey.
3. **How measured:** the people asked had chosen to attend the seller's demo, so they were already interested; and the number asked is not stated. If it was 10 managers, then 9 out of 10 is 90%, but ten people is a very small sample.
4. **Missing:** how many were asked, how they were chosen, the question wording, a comparison with competing products.
5. **Verdict.** Cannot tell; weak evidence. Ask for the sample size and the wording of the question.

### Solved example 3: "Errors cut by 50%"

*A team lead says a new data-entry checklist "cut errors by 50%". On asking, errors went from 2 per 1,000 entries to 1 per 1,000 entries.*

1. **Check the arithmetic.** From 2 to 1 is a drop of 1, and 1 divided by 2 is 0.5, so 50% is a true relative figure.
2. **Convert to absolute terms.** 2 per 1,000 is 0.2%; 1 per 1,000 is 0.1%. The absolute drop is 0.1 percentage points (1 fewer error in every 1,000 entries).
3. **Ask about noise.** With so few errors, one week might see 3 per 1,000 by chance.
4. **Ask for more.** How many entries were counted, over how long, and did the work mix stay the same?
5. **Verdict.** The 50% is correct but sounds bigger than it feels. The checklist may well help; the claim "cut errors by half" is not yet well supported until the sample is shown.

### Solved example 4: three sources for one number

*You need the current headcount. (A) HR system report, dated this month, exported by the HR analyst. (B) A former employee's social media post from last year. (C) An anonymous forum comment.*

1. **Closeness to the record.** A is the original record; B is second-hand; C is unknown.
2. **Date.** A is current; B is a year old and headcount changes.
3. **Accountability.** A has a named owner; B has a named person but no access to current data; C has nobody to ask.
4. **Rank:** A, then B, then C.
5. **Verdict.** Use A. Use B only as background and C not at all. If A disagreed with an independent source of equal quality you would investigate the difference.

### Solved example 5: matching the claim to the evidence

*Claim: "Our sales training raised sales." Evidence: trainees' average monthly sales rose from 100 to 110 units.*

1. **What was measured?** A 10% rise (110 divided by 100 = 1.10) among trainees.
2. **What is the claim?** A cause: the training did it.
3. **What is missing?** A comparison group. Suppose employees who did not attend rose from 100 to 108 in the same period (a busy season).
4. **Compute the comparison.** Trainees +10%, non-trainees +8%; the gap is only 2 percentage points.
5. **Verdict.** Most of the rise happened without training. The training may have added a little, but "raised sales" is overstated. The "why": the evidence must fit the strength of the claim.

## 2 real-life scenarios (illustrative numbers)

### Scenario 1: the recruiter message

A candidate receives a message: "You are shortlisted for a high-paying work-from-home job. Pay a ₹2,000 registration fee today to confirm your slot. Offer closes in 2 hours."

- **WHO:** the sender gives no company name or official email.
- **EVIDENCE:** no job description or interview.
- **Red flags:** urgency, a fee payment, a high salary promised with no interview.
- **Check:** search for the company on its own website and contact it through the number listed there, not the number in the message.
- **Verdict:** treat as unsafe; do not pay. Genuine employers do not normally charge candidates for being shortlisted (a general rule of thumb, so verify with the company).

### Scenario 2: "Complaints are down 30%" on a slide

A slide says "Customer complaints fell 30%", from 100 last quarter to 70 this quarter. You ask for the order counts: 5,000 orders last quarter, 3,000 this quarter.

- Complaints per 1,000 orders last quarter: 100 divided by 5,000 = 0.02, so 20 per 1,000.
- This quarter: 70 divided by 3,000 = 0.02333, so about 23.3 per 1,000.
- The complaint rate actually rose, because orders fell faster than complaints.
- The 30% fall was correct in raw counts but missed the denominator.

Lesson: always ask "out of how many?" before accepting a drop or a rise.

## Practice questions

**Easy**

**Q1** 🟢 A message reads: "The government will give a free laptop to every graduate. Click this link, enter your bank details and forward to 15 groups." List the red flags and say what you would do.

**Q2** 🟢 A phone advert says "Experts say this is the best phone this year." Which of the five questions does this claim fail most clearly, and what would you ask?

**Q3** 🟢 A restaurant sign says "Rated No. 1 by customers". Write three questions that you would need answered before believing it.

**Q4** 🟢 You need the number of leavers last quarter. Rank these from most to least reliable and say why: (a) a screenshot with no name or date, (b) the dated HR system export owned by the HR analyst, (c) what a colleague remembers.

**Q5** 🟢 Classify each statement as a fact claim, a cause claim, a prediction or an opinion: (a) "The canteen food is delicious." (b) "Attendance improved because of the new app." (c) "We will hire 50 people next year." (d) "Our office has 4 floors."

**Medium**

**Q6** 🟡 An app shows a rating of 4.8 from 10 reviews. You learn that all 10 were written by the company's own staff. An honest reviewer group of 5 people now adds 2 stars each. (a) What is the sum of the original ten ratings? (b) What is the new average across all 15 reviews (round to two decimal places)? (c) What does this teach about small samples and who writes the reviews?

**Q7** 🟡 A bank says "our new login reduces failed payments by 40%". Failed payments went from 5 per 1,000 to 3 per 1,000. (a) Check that the 40% is correct. (b) What is the absolute change, as a percentage of all payments? (c) How many fewer failed payments is that out of 100,000 payments? (d) Is the claim honest, and what would you still ask?

**Q8** 🟡 A shop owner posts: "Sales rose 25% after we changed our logo." Sales this quarter were 500 against 400 last quarter. You find that in the same two quarters a year earlier, sales were 380 and 475. (a) Check the 25%. (b) What was the rise in the same period a year earlier? (c) What explains the rise better than the logo?

**Q9** 🟡 An email survey of 500 employees reports "80% of employees are happy with the new policy". In fact 50 people replied and 40 of them said they were happy. (a) Show how 80% arises. (b) What is the response rate? (c) If all 450 non-responders were unhappy, what share of all 500 would be happy? If all 450 were happy, what share? (d) What does this range tell you about the claim?

**Q10** 🟡 A blog says "According to a report, 70% of customers leave after one bad experience." There is no link and no report title. When you search, you find that the "report" is mentioned only in other blogs that repeat the same sentence. (a) How many independent sources do you actually have? (b) What would you need to find to take the figure seriously? (c) What verdict would you give?

**Hard**

**Q11** 🔴 Two reports on delivery times in a city seem to disagree. Report A says "average delivery takes 34 minutes". Report B says "typical delivery takes 27 minutes". Ten sample deliveries (minutes) are: 20, 22, 24, 25, 26, 28, 30, 35, 40, 90. (a) Compute the mean and the median. (b) Show how both reports can be honest about the same data. (c) List two further reasons (other than mean versus median) that two honest reports on "delivery time" could differ.

**Q12** 🔴 A report claims "Department A had 9 safety incidents and Department B had 6, so B is the safer department." Department A staff worked 90,000 hours in the year; Department B staff worked 40,000 hours. (a) Compute the incidents per 10,000 hours for each department. (b) Which department has the higher incident rate? (c) What is wrong with the original claim and what question would have prevented the mistake?

**Q13** 🔴 A vendor's brochure: "Our fraud detector is 99% accurate", quoted by the vendor's own chief executive. Suppose 1% of 1,000 claims are fraudulent. (a) How many claims are fraudulent and how many are genuine? (b) A useless tool that labels every claim "genuine" gets how many right? What accuracy percentage is that? (c) What conflict of interest is present, and what other measures would you ask the vendor to show?

**Q14** 🔴 A colleague claims, "Remote work reduced attrition in our firm." Write a short verification plan: what data you would ask for, what comparisons you would make, which other explanations you would test, and where on the evidence ladder your plan would sit.

**Q15** 🔴 A forwarded message says: "As per Ministry of Labour notice 2026/44, all employees are entitled to 3 extra paid holidays. Share before the notice is withdrawn." The notice number is made up for this exercise. Describe step by step how you would verify it, what counts as sufficient confirmation, and what you would do if you cannot confirm it.

## Mistake log

- **Believing because many people forward it.** Repetition is not confirmation; trace the origin.
- **Trusting a link inside the message.** The link may be part of the trick. Search for the source yourself.
- **Accepting a percentage without the starting number.** Always ask for the numbers behind a "% change".
- **Treating "expert" or "study" as proof.** Ask which expert, which study, who ran it, how many people.
- **Swinging to the other extreme.** Doubting everything is as lazy as believing everything. Aim for "supported", "not supported" or "cannot tell yet", with a plan to find out.

## Recap

1. A claim is not a fact until you know who said it, what evidence it rests on and how it was measured.
2. Use the five questions: who, what evidence, how measured, what is missing, can I check elsewhere.
3. Prefer sources that are close to the original record, dated, accountable and independent of each other.
4. Read numbers with their denominators, sample sizes and absolute changes beside any percentage.
5. Give an honest verdict (supported, not supported, cannot tell yet) and do not pass on what you have not checked.
