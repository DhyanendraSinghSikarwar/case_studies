# Chapter 24: Correlation vs Causation

**What you will learn:** how to tell "two things move together" from "one thing makes the other happen", and how to say so clearly in meetings and interviews.

## Concept in plain language

**Correlation** means two things tend to move together. When one is high, the other is usually high (or usually low). It is a pattern in the data.

**Causation** means one thing actually makes the other happen. If you change the first, the second changes as a result.

Correlation is easy to see in a table or chart. Causation needs more: you must rule out the other explanations.

### The four explanations to check

When A and B move together, there are four main stories. We use these labels in the practice questions.

| Label | Story | Example (invented) |
|---|---|---|
| **P** (plausible cause) | A really does cause B, and the evidence supports it (for example a fair experiment) | Coupons given at random raised next-week purchases |
| **R** (reverse) | B actually causes A | People with poor sleep take sleeping pills |
| **T** (third factor) | A hidden factor C causes both A and B (also called a confounder) | Hot weather drives both ice-cream sales and swimming accidents |
| **C** (coincidence) | No real link: it happened by chance, or both simply drift up over time | Two unrelated numbers rose over the same years |

### Why a third factor matters

A **confounder** is a hidden factor that affects both things you are comparing. If you ignore it, you may believe A causes B when really C causes both. The cure is to compare like with like: for example, compare only hot months with hot months.

### How do we get closer to causation?

None of these alone is a proof, but together they build confidence:

- **A fair experiment:** split people **at random** into two groups, change one thing for one group only, and compare. Random splitting makes the groups alike in everything else.
- **A fair comparison group:** a before-and-after change with no comparison group can be fooled by seasons and trends.
- **Time order:** the cause must come first.
- **More cause, more effect (dose and response):** a steady pattern across levels supports a cause.
- **A believable mechanism:** a sensible way the cause could work.
- **Repeats:** the pattern appears again in other places and times.

### Safe wording

- Weak: "Spending causes revenue."
- Better: "Spending and revenue move together; we cannot yet say which drives which."
- When a test supports it: "In a random test, the coupon group bought more, so the coupon likely caused it."

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Almost every dashboard shows things moving together, and managers love to read causes into them. Saying "this is a correlation, here is what we would test" protects decisions from hidden third factors.

## Solved examples

### Example 1: Ice-cream sales and drownings (third factor)

Invented figures for 12 months in one city:

| Group | Monthly ice-cream sales (units) | Monthly drownings |
|---|---|---|
| 6 hot months | 950, 1100, 900, 1050, 1000, 980 | 9, 9, 10, 8, 10, 9 |
| 6 cool months | 320, 450, 380, 500, 300, 420 | 3, 2, 3, 3, 2, 3 |

- **Step 1, notice the pattern:** hot months average about 997 units and 9.2 drownings; cool months about 395 units and 2.7 drownings. Spreadsheet CORREL for all 12 months is about 0.95: a strong correlation.
- **Step 2, think of explanations:** does ice cream cause drowning (P)? Do drownings make people buy ice cream (R)? Is there a hidden factor (T)?
- **Step 3, test the third factor:** hot weather makes people eat ice cream and swim. Look inside the hot months only: the month with the most ice cream (1,100) had 9 drownings, while the month with the least (900) had 10. No sign that more ice cream brings more drownings.
- **Answer: T (third factor: temperature).**
- **Why:** comparing like with like (hot with hot) breaks the false link.

### Example 2: Marketing spend and revenue (reverse)

Invented figures for four shops (thousands of rupees):

| Shop | Last quarter revenue | Marketing budget this quarter |
|---|---|---|
| A | 400 | 20 |
| B | 620 | 31 |
| C | 800 | 40 |
| D | 1,000 | 50 |

- **Step 1:** bigger budget, bigger revenue: they move together.
- **Step 2:** check the rule: 20 is 5% of 400, 31 is 5% of 620, 40 is 5% of 800, 50 is 5% of 1,000. The budget was **set as 5% of last quarter's revenue**.
- **Step 3:** so revenue decided the budget, not the other way round.
- **Answer: R (reverse).** Spending might also help revenue, but this table cannot show it.
- **Why:** always ask how each number was decided.

### Example 3: Training and promotion (third factor, selection)

*Claim (invented):* "Employees who volunteered for the leadership course were promoted twice as often, so the course causes promotion."

- **Step 1:** the course was voluntary.
- **Step 2:** the kind of person who volunteers (ambitious, hard working) may also be promoted more.
- **Step 3:** that hidden factor, ambition, explains both.
- **Answer: T (third factor).** Course effects could still be real (P); a fair test would assign places at random among equally keen volunteers.
- **Why:** when people choose themselves into a group, the groups are not alike.

### Example 4: Two lines rising (coincidence)

*Claim (invented):* "From 2015 to 2024, the number of new cafes in our city and the number of film festivals both rose every year. Cafes cause film festivals."

- **Step 1:** two lines that both rise will always correlate strongly.
- **Step 2:** no mechanism connects the two and only 10 yearly points exist.
- **Step 3:** a shared drift (city growth) or pure chance explains it.
- **Answer: C (coincidence or shared trend).**
- **Why:** many unrelated things trend upward; correlation between trends is cheap.

### Example 5: A coupon test (plausible cause)

*Study (invented):* an app picked 400 customers and, using a random draw, gave a coupon to 200 of them. The next week, coupon customers placed on average 3.1 orders and the others 2.4.

- **Step 1:** the groups were formed by random draw, so they should be alike (age, habits, season).
- **Step 2:** only the coupon differs, and the difference is 3.1 minus 2.4, which is 0.7 orders per customer.
- **Step 3:** time order is right and the mechanism (a discount makes buying cheaper) is sensible.
- **Answer: P (plausible cause).** A good analyst still adds "in this test, for this week".
- **Why:** randomisation is the strongest everyday tool for ruling out third factors.

## Two real-life scenarios (all numbers illustrative)

### Scenario 1: Gym membership and sick days (HR)

The HR team finds employees who attended an optional money-management webinar made on average 2 salary-advance requests last year and others made 5. A manager says: "The webinar cuts advance requests; make everyone attend."

*Analysis:* people who sign up may already be careful with money (T). Also staff short of money may be working extra hours and unable to attend (T again, a hidden factor). A fair test: invite a random half of the volunteers to the webinar and compare advance requests next year, then decide.

### Scenario 2: Staff numbers and sales (commerce)

Stores with more staff report higher sales. The manager wants to add staff everywhere.

*Analysis:* busy stores may hire more staff because they are already selling a lot (R). Large city stores may both have more staff and more footfall (T). A fair check: add staff in a random sample of stores and compare with stores that did not get extra staff.

## Mistake log

1. **Treating a strong correlation as proof.** A correlation of 0.95 can still come from a third factor.
2. **Assuming the direction.** Ask which came first and how each number was decided; the arrow may point the other way.
3. **Forgetting self-selection.** If people chose the group they are in, the groups are not comparable.
4. **Believing a before-and-after change with no comparison.** Seasons and trends move numbers on their own.
5. **Over-correcting.** Some correlations are causal. Evidence from fair experiments, dose and response and mechanism can support a cause; do not say "correlation is never causation".

## Recap

1. Correlation means two things move together; causation means one makes the other happen.
2. Check four stories: A causes B, B causes A, a third factor causes both, or coincidence.
3. Ask how the groups or numbers were formed (choice, rule, random draw).
4. Random assignment, fair comparison, time order, dose and response and mechanism build the case for cause.
5. Use safe wording: "move together" until a fair test says more.
