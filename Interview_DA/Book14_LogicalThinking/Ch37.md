## Concept in plain language

### Why keep an error log at all?

Everyone makes mistakes. The trouble is that a mistake you fix and forget tends to come back in a new disguise. An **error log** is a simple table where you write down each mistake once, say what kind it was, and note what you will do differently. Over a few weeks the table shows **patterns**: perhaps half of your slips are unit slips, or you often skip the second case. A pattern is something you can fix with one habit, which is far easier than fixing 30 separate mistakes.

Think of it like a household budget. One purchase tells you little; a month of purchases shows where the money goes. The log is a budget for your mistakes.

### The error types (our taxonomy)

A **taxonomy** is just a list of categories. These eight come from the mistakes we met in earlier chapters (see especially Chapter 34 on finding the first faulty step). The list is our own working tool, not a standard.

| Error type | Plain meaning | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Misread the question | You answered a different question from the one asked | Re-read the last sentence: does your answer match what it asks for? |
| Wrong assumption | You treated something as true that the problem did not say | Write down what you assumed. Is it stated anywhere? |
| Skipped case | The method was fine, but you did not cover every possible case | Did the problem contain "or", a square, an absolute value, "at least", an edge value? |
| Sign or direction slip | A plus/minus, bigger/smaller or before/after was turned around | Does the sign of your answer match the story (a fall should be negative)? |
| Arithmetic slip | The method was right but a calculation was wrong | Redo each calculation on its own |
| Tried to be too fast | You gave the first answer that came to mind, with no working and no check | Plug your answer back into the problem |
| Fallacy missed | You accepted (or used) a flawed argument pattern, such as "after, therefore because" | Is there a named reasoning trap (Chapters 21 to 26)? |
| Unit or convention mistake | Mixed units, or used a rule of the field the wrong way (percent versus percentage points, inclusive versus exclusive) | Do all quantities share the same unit and the same definition? |

### Choosing one type: a decision order

Some mistakes could fit two labels. Ask these questions in order and take the **first** "yes":

1. Did the answer address something other than what was asked? Then **misread the question**.
2. Is there no working at all and the answer fails a quick plug-back? Then **tried to be too fast**.
3. Was the only wrong thing a calculation, with the method right? Then **arithmetic slip**.
4. Is the wrong thing a unit, a definition or a counting convention? Then **unit or convention**.
5. Is the wrong thing a turned-around sign or direction? Then **sign or direction slip**.
6. Did you bring in a fact or rule that nobody gave you (even silently)? Then **wrong assumption**.
7. Did you draw a conclusion from the facts given using a named bad argument pattern (such as "after, therefore because")? Then **fallacy missed**.
8. Otherwise, did you leave out a case? Then **skipped case**.

You will not need this order for most mistakes; it is there for the tie-breakers. A log works best when you pick a label quickly and move on. A reasonable label now beats a perfect label never.

### The two parts that matter most: root cause and fix rule

- **Root cause in one sentence** says why it happened, not just what happened. "I read the percent as a plain number because I was in a hurry" is a root cause. "Got it wrong" is not.
- **Fix rule I will follow** is a small, concrete action that is easy to check next time. Good: "Before finishing, write the unit next to every number." Weak: "Be more careful."

### The weekly review loop

1. **Log** each mistake soon after you find it, while you remember why it happened.
2. **Classify** it using the eight types.
3. **Write** the root cause and a fix rule.
4. **Re-test** on the re-test date: solve the same question again (or a similar one) without looking at the answer.
5. **Review** the log weekly: count errors by type, look at the biggest group, and sharpen its fix rule.
6. **Retire or keep** each rule: if you passed the re-test, keep the rule but check it again later; if you failed, rewrite the rule.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts keep bug logs and post-mortems for the same reason: repeated small mistakes (wrong units, skipped edge cases, an assumption nobody checked) cost more than rare big ones. A habit of naming mistakes and writing a checkable rule transfers directly to your own queries, formulas and reports. This chapter makes no promise about how much it will improve your results; it simply gives you a structure to notice patterns.

|---|---|---|

| B | Chapter / question | Short reference |

| E | Error type | Use a drop-down list with the eight types spelled exactly the same each time |
| F | Root cause (one sentence) | Text |
| G | Fix rule | Text |
| H | Re-test date | Date |
| I | Re-test result | Drop-down: Pass, Fail, Not yet done |

Two simple formulas (**from memory, not run here**; test them on a few rows before you rely on them):

```
Count of all errors of one type (type text typed in the formula):
=COUNTIF(E:E, "Arithmetic slip")

Count of one type that failed its re-test:
=COUNTIFS(E:E, "Unit or convention mistake", I:I, "Fail")
```

Put the eight type names in a small summary table (one per row) and copy the first formula down next to them, pointing at the name in each row instead of typing it. The biggest number is your biggest pattern.

## A weekly 30-minute review and a monthly review

This is **our suggestion, not research**. Change the times to fit your life.

**Weekly (30 minutes, same day each week)**

| Minutes | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Add any mistakes you did not log yet. Fill every column except re-test result. |
| 5 to 15 | Count errors by type (formula above or tally marks). Circle the top one or two types. |
| 15 to 25 | Re-test every row whose re-test date has come: redo the question without looking. Mark Pass or Fail. |
| 25 to 30 | For each Fail, rewrite the fix rule so it is more specific and easier to check. Pick one rule to focus on next week. |

**Monthly (about 45 minutes)**

1. Count errors by type for the whole month and compare with last month.
2. Read the fix rules of the top type. Merge duplicates and delete rules you never used.
3. Pick the one or two rules that you will print or write on a card by your desk.
4. Note any type that no longer appears; keep its rule on the list for another month, then check it again.

## 5 solved examples

For each mistake we (a) find the faulty step, (b) pick one error type, (c) write a root cause, and (d) write a fix rule. All names and numbers are invented.

### Example 1: The satisfaction share

**Question.** Of 80 survey replies, 28 said "Not satisfied". What percent were satisfied?
**Wrong solution.** "28 / 80 = 0.35, so 35%."

- The division is correct. The faulty step is the choice of what to divide: 28 is the "not satisfied" group, but the question asks for "satisfied".
- Correct: 80 - 28 = 52 satisfied; 52 / 80 = 0.65 = 65%.
- **Type:** misread the question (rule 1 of the decision order: the answer addresses another quantity).
- **Root cause:** I grabbed the only number the question gave and did not check which group it described.
- **Fix rule:** Before writing the answer, say aloud "the asked quantity is ___" and check that my number is that quantity.

### Example 2: Divisible by 2 or 5

**Question.** How many whole numbers from 1 to 10 are divisible by 2 or by 5?
**Wrong solution.** "Divisible by 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. That is 5 numbers."

- The listing for 2 is right. The faulty step is stopping there: the word "or" has a second case.
- Divisible by 5: 5 and 10. The list of numbers that qualify is 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 (10 appears once), so 6 numbers.
- **Type:** skipped case. The method was right; one case was never examined.
- **Root cause:** I answered the first half of the "or" and treated the job as done.
- **Fix rule:** When a question contains "or", write one line for each side before combining, and count shared items once.

### Example 3: Fifteen percent of 240

**Question.** A discount is 15% of a price of 240. How big is the discount?
**Wrong solution.** "0.15 x 240 = 26."

- The method (percent as a decimal times the price) is right. The multiplication is wrong: 0.15 x 240 = 36.
- Check: 10% of 240 is 24, 5% is 12, and 24 + 12 = 36.
- **Type:** arithmetic slip (rule 3: only the calculation is wrong).
- **Root cause:** I did the multiplication mentally and mixed up 36 with 26.
- **Fix rule:** Cross-check every percent calculation by splitting it (10% plus 5%) and compare.

### Example 4: Winners all have dark mode

**Question.** "The five most downloaded apps in our study all have a dark mode. Does dark mode make an app popular?"
**Wrong solution.** "Yes: all five top apps have it, so it must be the reason they are popular."

- The faulty step is the jump from "all five have it" to "it is the reason". We did not look at the many less popular apps, which may have dark mode too; and many things differ between top apps.
- **Type:** fallacy missed. The reasoning pattern is "looking only at winners" (a kind of selection bias) combined with treating a shared feature as a cause.
- **Root cause:** I counted what the winners have in common and never asked about the others.
- **Fix rule:** Before claiming a feature explains success, ask "how many unsuccessful ones also have it?"

### Example 5: Shipping by weight

**Question.** A parcel weighs 2.5 kg. Shipping costs 40 per 500 g. What is the cost?
**Wrong solution.** "2.5 / 500 = 0.005, then 0.005 x 40 = 0.2."

- The method (how many 500 g units, times 40) is right. The faulty step is dividing kilograms by grams as if they were the same unit.
- Correct: 2.5 kg = 2,500 g; 2,500 / 500 = 5 units; 5 x 40 = 200.
- **Type:** unit or convention mistake (rule 4).
- **Root cause:** I did not convert before dividing.
- **Fix rule:** Write the unit next to every number and convert everything to one unit first.

## 2 real-life scenarios

Numbers are **illustrative**.

### Scenario 1 (data): an analyst keeps a bug log

Dev is a junior analyst who writes a small log of every mistake found in a weekly sales report. After four weeks it has eight rows. Tallying by type gives:

| Error type | Count |
|---|---|
| Unit or convention mistake | 3 |
| Skipped case | 2 |
| Arithmetic slip | 1 |
| Misread the question | 1 |
| Wrong assumption | 1 |

Three of eight rows are unit or convention problems: two were dates counted as inclusive in one place and exclusive in another, and one was thousands mixed with single units. Dev writes one fix rule for the group: "Every report gets a 'definitions' line listing units and whether date ranges include both ends." The next four weeks of the log are used to see whether that rule is being followed, using the re-test column. The log did not make Dev cleverer; it showed where one habit would cover several mistakes.

### Scenario 2 (team): a post-mortem

A team sends a monthly report whose total is 10,000 too high (illustrative). In the review meeting they follow a **blameless** format: the goal is to find the first faulty step, not a person to blame.

1. **Timeline:** data pulled, table merged, total calculated, report sent.
2. **First faulty step:** the merge. A customer with two addresses appeared twice and was counted twice.
3. **Type:** skipped case (the case "one customer, two rows" was never tested).
4. **Root cause:** nobody checked whether the customer key was unique.
5. **Fix rule:** before any merge, count rows and count distinct keys; they must match.
6. **Re-test:** the next month's report is checked with that rule and the result is written in the log.

A team log is the same table as a personal log with one extra column for "owner". If your team has one, you can add your own rows to it.

**Q8** 🟡 *Question:* "The mean of four numbers is 10. Three of them are 8, 9 and 11. What is the fourth?" *Wrong solution (no working shown):* "10."

**Q10** 🟡 *Question:* "The refund rate rose from 4% to 6%. By how much did it rise, in the standard way of reporting a change between two percentages?" *Wrong solution:* "6 - 4 = 2, so the refund rate rose by 2%."

**Q11** 🔴 *Question:* "Daily sales for five days were 12, 18, 9, 15 and 36. By how much did the best day beat the **median** day?" *Wrong solution:* "Step 1: total is 90, so the mean is 90 / 5 = 18. Step 2: the best day is 36. Step 3: 36 - 18 = 18."

**Q12** 🔴 *Question:* "A helpdesk received 60, 50, 30, 25, 20, 20, 18 and 17 tickets on days 1 to 8 of a 30-day month (240 in total). **Q13** 🔴 *Question:* "Find all x with 5 - 2x > 11." *Wrong solution:* "Step 1: subtract 5 from both sides: -2x > 6. Step 2: divide both sides by -2: x > -3."

## Mistake log

1. **Logging "got it wrong" and nothing else.** Without a type and a root cause there is no pattern to find.
2. **Fix rules that are wishes.** "Be more careful" cannot be checked. Write an action: "write the unit next to every number".
3. **Never re-testing.** A fix rule only counts once you have solved a similar question again without help.
4. **Spending a long time on the label.** If two types fit, use the decision order and move on.
5. **Logging only big mistakes.** Small slips repeat the most; those are the ones the log helps with.

## Recap

1. An error log turns scattered mistakes into patterns you can act on.
2. Each row has the question, your answer, the correct answer, an error type, a one-sentence root cause and a fix rule.
3. The eight types are: misread the question, wrong assumption, skipped case, sign or direction slip, arithmetic slip, tried too fast, fallacy missed, unit or convention mistake.
4. A good fix rule is a small action you can check; the re-test date tells you whether it worked.
5. Review weekly (our suggestion: 30 minutes) and monthly, and keep the log going after the book ends.
