## Concept in plain language

### What is logic?

**Logic** is the study of good reasoning. It asks one simple question: *do the reasons we are given really support the claim being made?*

You do not need maths for this. You already reason all day: "The road is jammed, so I will take the metro." Logic simply gives names to the parts so you can check them carefully.

### What is an argument?

In everyday speech, "argument" means a quarrel. In logic it means something calmer:

> An **argument** is a small piece of text where some statements (the reasons) are offered to support another statement (the claim).

An argument has two kinds of parts:

| Part | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Premise** | A reason or piece of evidence offered | "The warehouse floods every monsoon." |
| **Conclusion** | The claim the premises are meant to support | "So we should move the stock upstairs." |

An argument can have one premise or many, but it always has a conclusion.

### Clue words

Writers often (not always) give signals.

| Signals a conclusion | Signals a premise |
|---|---|
| therefore, so, hence, thus, as a result, it follows that, this shows that, consequently | because, since, for, given that, as, the reason is, after all |

Two warnings:

- The conclusion can be first, middle or last. "Rohan is late because the train was delayed" has the conclusion first.
- Sometimes there is no clue word at all. Then ask: *"Which sentence is the writer trying to convince me of?"* That one is the conclusion.

### Is it even an argument?

Not every passage is an argument. Use the **"Why should I believe that?" test**. If one sentence is being pushed as a claim and the others are given to back it up, it is an argument. If the passage only describes, lists, reports events or explains something that everybody already accepts, it is **not** an argument.

| Type of passage | Example | Argument? |
|---|---|---|
| Description or list | "The office has three floors and a cafe." | No |
| Report of events | "The vendor called, we paid, and the goods arrived." | No |
| Explanation of an accepted fact | "The meeting moved because the manager's flight was late." (the move is already known) | No |
| Reasons for a claim | "The meeting will move, because the manager's flight is late and he chairs it." | Yes |

### Deduction and induction

Arguments come in two broad strengths.

- **Deduction**: the author claims that *if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false*. It is a guarantee.
  Example: "All sales staff get a phone allowance. Meera is in sales. So Meera gets a phone allowance."
- **Induction**: the author claims that the premises make the conclusion *likely*, but a surprise is still possible. It usually goes from observed cases to a general or future case.
  Example: "The last five Mondays were busy, so this Monday will probably be busy."

Quick way to tell them apart: ask *"Even if all the premises are true, could the conclusion still turn out false?"* If the answer is "no, impossible", it was meant as a deduction. If "yes, possible but unlikely", it is induction. Words like "probably", "likely", "seems", "most" often point to induction.

> Important: in this chapter we only **find** the parts. We do **not** yet judge whether the premises are true or whether the argument is good. That comes in Chapters 6 and 23 onwards.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Reports, dashboards and emails all end with a claim ("sales fell because of X"). Splitting the claim from its reasons is the first step in checking it. Most mistakes in analysis hide in the gap between the evidence and the claim.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: An advertisement

> "Our tea is made from hand-picked leaves, so it tastes fresher."

1. **Look for clue words.** "So" signals a conclusion.
   *Why:* clue words are the quickest first check.
2. **Conclusion:** "it tastes fresher". It is the claim being pushed.
3. **Premise:** "Our tea is made from hand-picked leaves". It is the reason offered.
4. **Type:** the premise does not guarantee freshness, it only makes it plausible, so this is **induction**.
   *Why:* hand-picking leaves could still be followed by slow shipping, so a surprise is possible.

We have not checked whether the tea really is hand-picked. That is not our job yet.

### Example 2: Conclusion comes first

> "Ravi will not get the bonus, because he joined after the cutoff date, and only employees who joined before the cutoff get it."

1. **Clue word:** "because" signals premises, so the conclusion is *before* it.
   *Why:* "because" points to a reason, and the thing being explained comes before it.
2. **Conclusion:** "Ravi will not get the bonus".
3. **Premises:** (a) "He joined after the cutoff date." (b) "Only employees who joined before the cutoff get it."
4. **Type:** if both premises are true, Ravi cannot get the bonus. That is a guarantee, so **deduction**.

### Example 3: No clue word

> "The server was down on each of the last three Mondays. It is likely to be down this Monday as well."

1. **Search for clue words.** None.
2. **Ask:** which sentence is trying to convince me of something? The second one: it predicts.
   *Why:* when signals are missing, the claim is usually the sentence that goes beyond the facts.
3. **Conclusion:** "It is likely to be down this Monday as well." **Premise:** "The server was down on each of the last three Mondays."
4. **Type:** a pattern from past cases to a future case, with the word "likely": **induction**.

### Example 4: The clue word is at the start

> "Since every invoice above Rs 50,000 needs director approval, and this invoice is Rs 72,000, it needs director approval."

1. **Clue word:** "Since" opens the sentence and introduces the premises.
2. **Premises:** (a) every invoice above Rs 50,000 needs director approval; (b) this invoice is Rs 72,000.
3. **Conclusion:** "it needs director approval".
4. **Check the fit:** 72,000 is above 50,000, so the rule applies. If both premises are true, the conclusion is forced: **deduction**.
   *Why:* the rule covers every invoice above the limit, and this one is above it.

### Example 5: Is it an argument at all?

Passage A: "The store opens at 9 am. It has two floors, and the cafe is on the ground floor."
Passage B: "The store is crowded on Saturdays, because most people are off work then."

1. **Passage A:** no sentence is being defended. It just describes. **Not an argument.**
   *Why:* there is no claim that needs a reason.
2. **Passage B:** "because" appears, but ask the "Why should I believe that?" test. The writer gives a reason for why the store is crowded. If we treat "the store is crowded on Saturdays" as an accepted fact, B is an **explanation**, not an argument.
   *Why:* the clue word alone does not make an argument. What matters is whether a claim is being proved or a known fact is being explained.
3. **Contrast:** "Expect the store to be crowded this Saturday, because most people are off work then" is an argument (a prediction with a reason).

## 2 real-life scenarios

### Scenario 1: A flash-sale email (commerce)

> Subject: "Only 3 hours left! Over 500 happy customers (illustrative) bought this blender last week. It must be a great blender. Order now."

- **Conclusion:** "It must be a great blender." (the sentence trying to convince you)
- **Premise:** "Over 500 customers bought it last week."
- **"Order now"** is a command, not a premise or a conclusion.
- **Type:** induction (many buyers makes "great" plausible, not certain).
- **What a careful reader asks next:** buying is not the same as being happy. Book 14 returns to this in Chapters 23 and 26.

### Scenario 2: A line in an HR report (data)

> "Attrition fell from 18% to 12% (illustrative) after we introduced flexible hours, so flexible hours reduce attrition."

- **Conclusion:** "flexible hours reduce attrition".
- **Premise:** "Attrition fell from 18% to 12% after flexible hours were introduced."
- **Type:** induction. One before-and-after case supports the claim only weakly.
- **Why splitting helps:** once the premise is written alone ("it fell after"), you can see it says nothing about *why* it fell. Hiring slowdown, a bonus or the season could also be reasons. Chapter 24 teaches this.

## Practice questions

For each passage: (a) say whether it is an argument; (b) if yes, write the premise(s) and the conclusion; (c) say deduction or induction. Passages are invented.

### Easy

**Q1** 🟢 "All employees in the sales team get a phone allowance. Meera is in the sales team. Therefore Meera gets a phone allowance."

**Q2** 🟢 "Every time the office printer was refilled on a Friday, it jammed by Monday. It was refilled this Friday, so it will probably jam by Monday."

**Q3** 🟢 "Rohan must be at the airport by 6 am, because his flight leaves at 8 am and check-in closes two hours before departure."

**Q4** 🟢 "No visitor may enter the lab without a badge. Anil has no badge. Hence Anil may not enter the lab."

**Q5** 🟢 "The cafeteria serves breakfast from 8 to 10 am. Lunch is served from 12 to 2 pm. Dinner is not served."

### Medium

**Q6** 🟡 "Customers who got a reminder call renewed their plans. Most of those who did not get a call let their plans lapse. It seems the calls work."

**Q7** 🟡 "Since the warehouse gets flooded every monsoon, and this year's monsoon is forecast to be heavy, we should move the stock upstairs before June."

**Q8** 🟡 Asha says: "I checked 40 of the 500 expense claims, picked at random, and all 40 had receipts attached. So nearly all 500 claims have receipts."

**Q9** 🟡 "Either the payment failed or the bank is down. The bank is not down. So the payment failed."

**Q10** 🟡 "The team meeting was moved to Thursday because the manager's flight was delayed."

### Hard

**Q11** 🔴 "Priya has worked here for eight years, so she is eligible for the long-service award." (Besides the usual parts, write the unstated premise that makes this a deduction.)

**Q12** 🔴 "Under our policy, a refund request is valid if it is filed within 30 days and the item is unopened, and every valid request is paid. Sana filed within 30 days and her item is unopened. **Q14** 🔴 "The server crashed at noon, so the team restarted it, and the report went out at 1 pm."

## Mistake log

1. **Thinking the conclusion is always last.** It can be first or in the middle. Use clue words and the "what is the writer trying to convince me of?" question.
2. **Treating every "because" or "so" as proof of an argument.** "The server crashed, so the team restarted it" only reports events.
3. **Counting a quoted opposing view as a premise.** "Many people say X, but I think Y" supports Y with what follows "but", not with "many people say".
4. **Judging truth too early.** At this stage you are only labelling parts. A false premise still is a premise.
5. **Mixing up "unlikely to fail" with "cannot fail".** Only the second is deduction.

## Recap

1. Logic checks whether reasons really support a claim.
2. An argument has premises (reasons) and a conclusion (claim).
3. Clue words help, but the conclusion can be anywhere; ask what the writer wants you to believe.
4. Descriptions, lists, event reports and explanations of known facts are not arguments.
5. Deduction guarantees the conclusion; induction only makes it likely.
