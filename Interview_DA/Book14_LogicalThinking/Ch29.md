## Concept in plain language

In Chapter 28 you learned the four steps. Step 2 (Plan) asks you to name a method. This chapter gives you five methods that cover a large share of everyday puzzles.

| Strategy | Plain meaning | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Work backward** | Start at the end and undo each step in reverse order | You know the final result and want the start, or you have a deadline and want the latest start time |
| **Simplify** | Solve the same problem with smaller numbers first | Numbers are big or the situation is messy |
| **Find patterns** | Solve several small cases, write results in a row, spot the rule | The problem grows step by step (rows, rounds, sizes) |
| **Casework** | Split into separate, non-overlapping cases and handle each | The answer depends on a choice that can go a few different ways |
| **Elimination** | Cross out options that break a clue, until one is left | You have a list of candidates and several clues |

### A few tips for each

- **Work backward:** each step is undone with its *opposite*. Adding becomes subtracting, doubling becomes halving, "spent half" becomes "multiply what is left by 2".
- **Simplify:** pick a tiny version (2, 3 or 4 instead of 200) where you can see everything. Then find what changes as the size grows.
- **Find patterns:** write small cases in a table. Check the rule on one more case *before* trusting it, then use it for the big case.
- **Casework:** the cases must not overlap (no double counting) and must cover everything (no case missed). Pick the "splitting question" carefully, for example "how many 50-rupee notes are used?"
- **Elimination:** go clue by clue and cross out. Keep a visible list so you never forget what is still alive.

### How to choose

1. Is the end known and the start unknown? Work backward.
2. Are the numbers large but the rule simple? Simplify, then look for a pattern.
3. Is there a list of possible answers? Elimination.
4. Does the answer depend on a small choice? Casework.
5. Stuck? Try another strategy. The same problem often yields to two or three.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts rarely meet a problem that is labelled with a method. Having a short list of strategies to try keeps you moving when you are stuck. Working backward from a target helps in planning and deadlines; casework matches how analysts split data into groups; elimination mirrors how you narrow down the cause of a number that looks wrong.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Work backward

**Problem.** A training budget is spent in three stages. First, half of it goes on laptops. Then 3,000 goes on desks. Then one third of what remains goes on training courses. After all this, 4,000 is left. What was the starting budget?

**Step by step (using the four steps).**

1. **Understand.** Unknown: the starting budget. We know the end (4,000) and each step. That points to working backward.
2. **Plan.** Undo the stages from the last one to the first.
3. **Execute.**
   1. After the courses: 4,000 left. The courses took one third of the amount before, so 4,000 is the other two thirds. Why: spending a third leaves two thirds.
   2. Amount before the courses: 4,000 divided by 2/3 = 4,000 x 3/2 = 6,000.
   3. Undo the desks: add back 3,000, giving 9,000.
   4. Undo the laptops: half was spent, so 9,000 is the other half. Starting budget: 9,000 x 2 = 18,000.
4. **Review.** Go forward: 18,000, half spent leaves 9,000; minus 3,000 leaves 6,000; a third of 6,000 is 2,000 spent, leaving 4,000. Correct.

**Answer: 18,000.**

### Example 2: Simplify

**Problem.** A straight garden path is 200 metres long. A street lamp stands at the very start, then every 8 metres, with one at the very end too. How many lamps?

1. **Understand.** Unknown: the number of lamps. The tempting answer is 200 / 8 = 25, but is a lamp at both ends counted?
2. **Plan.** Simplify: use a tiny path of 16 metres and count by drawing.
3. **Execute.**
   1. A 16 m path with lamps at 0, 8 and 16. That is 3 lamps, while 16 / 8 = 2 gaps.
   2. So lamps = gaps + 1. Why: with a lamp at both ends, every gap has a lamp at its end, and the very first lamp is extra.
   3. For the 200 m path: gaps = 200 / 8 = 25. Lamps = 25 + 1 = 26.
4. **Review.** Check the rule on another tiny case: 8 m path has 1 gap and 2 lamps (0 and 8). Fits. The lamps are at 0, 8, 16, ..., 200; the last is 8 x 25 = 200, so there are 26 positions.

**Answer: 26 lamps.**

### Example 3: Find patterns

**Problem.** Nine colleagues meet and every pair plays one table-tennis game against each other (each pair only once). How many games are played in total?

1. **Understand.** Unknown: total games. Each *pair* plays exactly once.
2. **Plan.** Count for 2, 3, 4, 5 people, write the results, find the rule, then extend to 9.
3. **Execute.** Think of adding people one at a time. A new person plays against everyone already there.

| People | New games when the last person joins | Total games |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 1 |
| 3 | 2 | 3 |
| 4 | 3 | 6 |
| 5 | 4 | 10 |

   Pattern: the n-th person adds (n - 1) games. So for 9 people: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 = 36.
4. **Review.** Second route: each of 9 people plays 8 others, 9 x 8 = 72 plays counted, but every game is counted twice (once for each player), so 72 / 2 = 36. Same.

**Answer: 36 games.**

### Example 4: Casework

**Problem.** A shopkeeper has plenty of 10, 20 and 50 rupee notes. In how many different ways can exactly 70 rupees be paid? (Only the number of notes of each kind matters, not the order.)

1. **Understand.** Count the combinations (tens, twenties, fifties) that total 70.
2. **Plan.** Split into cases by how many 50 notes are used. Why: the 50 is the biggest piece, so it limits the other choices most.
3. **Execute.**
   - **0 fifty notes:** pay 70 with tens and twenties. Use 0, 1, 2 or 3 twenty notes (4 twenties would be 80, too much). The rest must be tens: 70, 50, 30, 10 left, all fine. That gives 4 ways.
   - **1 fifty note:** 20 remains. Twenties: 0 or 1. With 0 twenties, 2 tens; with 1 twenty, 0 tens. That gives 2 ways.
   - **2 fifty notes:** 100, too much. Stop.
   - Total: 4 + 2 = 6.
4. **Review.** List all six and check each adds to 70:
   - 7 tens (70)
   - 5 tens and 1 twenty (50 + 20)
   - 3 tens and 2 twenties (30 + 40)
   - 1 ten and 3 twenties (10 + 60)
   - 1 fifty and 2 tens (50 + 20)
   - 1 fifty and 1 twenty (50 + 20)

   Six distinct combinations, none repeated. A computer count over all combinations also gives 6.

**Answer: 6 ways.**

### Example 5: One problem, three different ways

**Problem.** A clerk has an unlimited supply of stamps worth 5 and stamps worth 8. How many of each are needed to make exactly 47? (Find the numbers.)

**Way 1: Casework.** Split by the number of 8-stamps, b.
- b = 0: 47 left, not a multiple of 5. No.
- b = 1: 39 left. No.
- b = 2: 31 left. No.
- b = 3: 23 left. No.
- b = 4: 15 left, which is 3 stamps of 5. Yes.
- b = 5: 7 left. No. (b = 6 gives 48, too much.)
Answer: 3 stamps of 5 and 4 stamps of 8.

**Way 2: Elimination using the last digit.** A multiple of 5 ends in 0 or 5. The total 47 ends in 7. So the 8-stamps must add up to something ending in 2 or 7. The multiples of 8 are 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48. Only 32 ends in 2 (and 8 x 9 = 72 is too big). So the 8-stamps total 32, which is 4 stamps. The rest is 47 - 32 = 15 = 3 stamps of 5. Same answer.

**Way 3: Work backward.** Start at 47 and keep taking away an 8-stamp until what is left is a multiple of 5: 47, 39, 31, 23, 15. After four 8s we reach 15, a multiple of 5. So 4 eights and 15 / 5 = 3 fives. Same answer.

**Review.** 3 x 5 = 15, 4 x 8 = 32, 15 + 32 = 47. Three methods agree, which is far stronger evidence than one.

**Why bother with three ways?** When you have time, a second method is the best checker. In an interview, if the first method gives a number you are unsure about, quickly solve it another way.

## 2 real-life scenarios

Numbers are invented and labelled "illustrative".

### Scenario 1 (HR, casework): who attends the conference

*Illustrative.* A team of five (Kavya, Leo, and three others) can send two people to a conference. The manager wants at least one of Kavya or Leo to go. How many different pairs are possible?

- Cases (no overlap, nothing missed): **only Kavya** (she plus one of the three others: 3 pairs), **only Leo** (3 pairs), **both** (1 pair).
- Total: 3 + 3 + 1 = 7.
- Check by elimination of the bad pairs: all pairs of five is 10; pairs with neither Kavya nor Leo (two of the three others) is 3; so 10 - 3 = 7. Same.

### Scenario 2 (data, work backward): tracing a cleaning pipeline

*Illustrative.* A data file went through three cleaning steps. Step 1 removed 20 percent of the rows as duplicates. Step 2 removed 40 blank rows. Step 3 kept only three quarters of what was left. The final file has 450 rows. How many rows did it start with?

- Undo step 3: 450 is three quarters, so before it there were 450 / (3/4) = 600 rows.
- Undo step 2: add back 40, giving 640.
- Undo step 1: 640 is 80 percent of the start, so the start was 640 / 0.8 = 800.
- Check forward: 800 minus 20 percent (160) gives 640; minus 40 gives 600; three quarters of 600 is 450. Correct.

## Mistake log

1. **Undoing steps in the wrong order when working backward.** The last step must be undone first. Fix: write the steps in a list, then read the list from the bottom.
2. **Trusting a pattern from too few cases.** Two or three cases can fit many rules. Fix: test your rule on one extra small case you worked out separately.
3. **Overlapping or missing cases in casework.** Fix: choose one splitting question (for example "how many 50 notes?") so every possibility lands in exactly one case.
4. **Off-by-one counting.** Lamps, cuts and floors are counted at the points, while distances are counted in the gaps. Fix: simplify to a tiny example.
5. **Stopping at the first answer.** Solve one problem a second way when time allows.

## Recap (5 lines)

1. Work backward: start from the end and undo each step with its opposite, in reverse order.
2. Simplify: solve a tiny version first, then see what changes as the size grows.
3. Find patterns: tabulate small cases, state the rule, test it on one more case.
4. Casework: split by one clear question into cases that do not overlap and miss nothing.
5. Elimination: cross out options clue by clue, and confirm a result with a second method.
