## Concept in plain language

### What a "blood relation" question really is

A blood-relation question gives you a few facts about a family in words and asks: "How is person X related to person Y?" Nobody needs to know the family. You only need to **draw the family** from the words, then read the answer off your drawing.

Think of a family tree as a tiny database. Each person is a row. Each fact ("Lila is the mother of Omar") links two rows. The relationship word you are asked for ("uncle", "cousin", "sister-in-law") is just a **name for a path** through the tree: go up one step to the parent, sideways to the brother, down one step to his child, and so on.

### The building blocks

Everything else in the chapter is built from four basic links.

| Link | Plain meaning | Tells you the gender? |
|---|---|---|
| Parent | The person who gave birth to you or fathered you | No (father is male, mother is female) |
| Child | The person you are the parent of | No (son is male, daughter is female) |
| Sibling | Someone who has the same two parents as you | No (brother is male, sister is female) |
| Spouse | The person you are married to | No (husband is male, wife is female) |

From these four, longer words are built.

| Word | Built from | Example path starting from "me" |
|---|---|---|
| Grandfather / grandmother | Parent's parent | my father's mother |
| Grandson / granddaughter | Child's child | my daughter's son |
| Uncle / aunt | Parent's brother or sister (also: parent's sibling's spouse) | my mother's brother |
| Nephew / niece | Sibling's child | my sister's daughter |
| Cousin | Child of your parent's sibling | my father's sister's son |
| Father-in-law / mother-in-law | Spouse's parent | my wife's mother |
| Brother-in-law / sister-in-law | Spouse's sibling, or sibling's spouse | my wife's brother, my sister's husband |
| Son-in-law / daughter-in-law | Child's spouse | my daughter's husband |

**Paternal and maternal.** "Paternal" means "on the father's side", "maternal" means "on the mother's side". A paternal grandmother is your father's mother. A maternal uncle is your mother's brother. Many questions are won or lost on this one word.

### Rules everybody quietly assumes

Questions of this type do not repeat these every time. Learn them once:

- Brothers and sisters are **full** siblings: same father and same mother.
- Each person has one marriage. Nobody marries a blood relative.
- "Husband" means a man and "wife" means a woman, so those words tell you the gender.
- Words like "cousin", "sibling", "parent", "child", "spouse" do **not** tell you the gender. If a question never says, you must not guess.
- The family you are told about is the whole story. Nobody hidden is secretly involved.

### The word "only"

"My father's brother" could be one of several men. "My father's **only** brother" is exactly one man. "Only" is what lets you keep going ("his wife", "his son") and still land on one specific person.

### The five-step method

1. **Underline the chain** and split it at each "'s" or "of".
2. **Start at the person who speaks** (or at the end of the sentence, depending on how it is phrased) and take the links **one at a time, in order**.
3. **Draw** each person as a short label with M or F next to it, and use a line up for "parent", sideways for "sibling or spouse", down for "child".
4. **Mark the gender** only when a word tells you (father, wife, son, sister ...). Leave a question mark otherwise.
5. **Name the path** between the two people you were asked about, then **check by reading the path backwards** (if X is the uncle of Y, then Y is the nephew or niece of X).

A text drawing is fine. Here is a three-generation tree for "Omar's father is Piotr, Omar's mother is Lila, Omar's sister is Quincy":

```
Piotr (M) ===== Lila (F)        <- "=====" means married
        |
   +----+----+
   |         |
 Omar (?)  Quincy (F)           <- "Omar" has no gender word, so ?
```

### Reading phrases from the inside out

For a phrase like "my father's brother's wife's son", you handle one link at a time starting from "my":

1. my father (one person)
2. his brother (one person, if the sentence said "only", or the question makes it clear)
3. that brother's wife
4. her son

The **collapse trick**: sometimes a middle part collapses into a person you already have. Example: "my mother's only daughter" is **me**, if I am a woman. Spotting this saves you a whole drawing.

### Symbol-coded relations

Some questions replace words with symbols: "P & Q means P is the mother of Q". You translate each symbol into a link, draw the chain left to right, then ask your question. The symbols give you gender information only through the word they stand for (mother = F, brother = M).

---

## Why this matters for data work

- Turning a messy sentence into a small structure (people and links) is the same habit you use to read an organisation chart, a parent-child table, or a "manager of" column.
- Careful reading of qualifiers like "only" and "at least" is the habit that protects you from wrong filters and wrong totals.
- Refusing to guess a missing detail (a gender, a date, a number) is a data-quality habit, not only a puzzle habit.

## Five solved examples

### Example 1: Reading a direct fact

**Question.** Omar's mother is Lila. Lila's husband is Piotr. Omar's sister is Quincy. How is Piotr related to Quincy?

**Step 1: Draw what you know.** Lila is a mother (F), Piotr is her husband (M).

*Why:* "Husband" tells you Piotr is male, and a married couple is the natural top of the tree.

**Step 2: Place the children.** Omar and Quincy are brother and sister, so they have the same parents. Their mother is Lila.

*Why:* By our rule, siblings are full siblings, so Quincy's mother is also Lila.

**Step 3: Link the father.** Lila's husband Piotr is also the father of Lila's children, so he is Quincy's father.

*Why:* Under the "one marriage" rule, the husband of the children's mother is their father.

**Answer.** Piotr is Quincy's **father**.

**Check.** Reverse it: Quincy is Piotr's daughter (she is a "sister", so female). Consistent.

---

### Example 2: A four-link chain

**Question.** Chen (a man) says: "Zane is my father's brother's wife's son." How is Zane related to Chen?

**Step 1: Take "my father".** Call him the father (M).

*Why:* The phrase starts at Chen, so the first link is Chen's father.

**Step 2: "his brother".** The father's brother is Chen's paternal uncle (M).

*Why:* A parent's brother is an uncle. It is the paternal side because it goes through the father.

**Step 3: "his wife".** The uncle's wife is Chen's aunt (F).

*Why:* The uncle is married, and his wife is a woman by the word "wife".

**Step 4: "her son".** Her son is also the uncle's son (one marriage rule).

*Why:* A couple's children belong to both parents in these questions.

**Step 5: Name the path.** Chen to his father, sideways to the father's brother, down to that brother's son. Up one, across one, down one is the pattern for a **cousin**.

**Answer.** Zane is Chen's **cousin** (a male cousin, because Zane is a "son").

**Check.** Reverse: Chen is the son of Zane's father's brother. That is also a cousin. Consistent.

---

### Example 3: When "only" makes the answer definite

**Question.** Joel (a man) points to a woman and says, "She is the wife of my mother's only brother." How is she related to Joel?

**Step 1: "my mother".** Joel's mother (F).

*Why:* Start at the speaker and take the first link.

**Step 2: "only brother".** Her one brother is Joel's maternal uncle (M).

*Why:* "Only" says there is exactly one such man, so "his wife" will be one woman.

**Step 3: "wife of".** That uncle's wife.

*Why:* Marriage links the uncle to a woman who is not Joel's blood relative.

**Step 4: Name her.** The wife of a parent's brother is an **aunt** (by marriage), here a maternal aunt by marriage.

**Answer.** She is Joel's **aunt** (his mother's brother's wife; often called a maternal uncle's wife).

**Check.** Reverse: Joel is the nephew of the woman (her husband's sister's son). Consistent.

---

### Example 4: A coded relation

**Question.** In a code, A & B means "A is the mother of B" and A ~ B means "A is the brother of B". If K & L ~ M, how is K related to M?

**Step 1: Translate the first link.** K & L: K is the mother of L. So K is female and L is her child.

*Why:* Each symbol is a plain link, so translate before thinking.

**Step 2: Translate the second link.** L ~ M: L is the brother of M. So L is male, and L and M are siblings.

*Why:* A brother is a male sibling, which tells you L's gender and M's parent.

**Step 3: Combine.** K is L's mother. M is L's sibling, so K is also M's mother.

*Why:* Siblings share both parents.

**Answer.** K is the **mother** of M.

**Check.** Reverse: M is K's child (gender of M is not given, so "child").

---

### Example 5: Counting brothers and sisters

**Question.** Yara has 2 sisters and 1 brother. How many daughters does the family have, and how many sisters does her brother have?

**Step 1: Count the girls.** Yara plus her 2 sisters = 3 girls.

*Why:* "Has 2 sisters" does not include Yara herself, so you add her.

**Step 2: Count the boys.** She has 1 brother, so the family has 1 son.

*Why:* All other children are brothers or sisters of Yara, and every child is either one or the other.

**Step 3: Count what the brother sees.** The brother has all 3 daughters as sisters, because he is not a girl and has no brothers in the family.

*Why:* A person is never counted as their own sibling.

**Answer.** **3 daughters**, **1 son**, and the brother has **3 sisters**.

**Check.** Total children = 4. Yara sees 3 others (2 sisters and 1 brother). Consistent.

## Two real-life scenarios

### Scenario 1: Cleaning an emergency-contact column (illustrative)

An HR team collects "relationship to employee" as free text. Three entries (all invented for this example) read:

- "my husband's sister"
- "my wife's mother's husband"
- "my brother's son"

To store these in one standard column, someone must turn each into one standard word. Using the table: husband's sister is a **sister-in-law**, wife's mother's husband is a **father-in-law** (the wife's father), and brother's son is a **nephew**. Notice the word "wife's" means the speaker is writing from a man's point of view, and the standard column is only useful if you name the path, not guess.

### Scenario 2: A family plan on a phone account (illustrative)

A phone company offers a "family plan" and the account holder, Kofi, lists members as "my sister's husband", "my father's brother's daughter" and "my son's wife". Mapping these to standard words gives **brother-in-law**, **cousin**, and **daughter-in-law**. A data analyst who wants to count "family members who are blood relatives" must know that the brother-in-law and daughter-in-law are linked by marriage, not by blood, while the cousin is a blood relative. The counting rule depends on the family tree, not on how familiar the word sounds.

## Mistake log

1. **Reading the chain from the wrong end.** The phrase "my father's sister's husband" starts at "my", then father, then sister, then husband. Starting at "husband" and going backwards gives a different person.
2. **Ignoring "only".** "My father's brother" may be several men; "my father's only brother" is exactly one. Also watch for chains that collapse back to a person you already have (for example "my mother's only daughter" is you, if you are a woman).
3. **Guessing a gender.** Words like "cousin", "sibling", "child" and "spouse" do not tell the gender. If nothing says, write "?" and keep the answer general ("uncle or aunt").
4. **Mixing paternal and maternal.** Going through the father is paternal, through the mother is maternal. A father's sister and a mother's sister are both "aunt", but the path is different.
5. **Counting the person themselves.** "Has 3 sisters" means three others, so a girl who says it belongs to a group of four girls.

---

## Recap

1. A blood-relation question is a family tree in words: draw it, then read the path.
2. Take the chain one link at a time, starting from the speaker.
3. Only use a gender when a word tells you (father, wife, sister ...); otherwise leave a question mark.
4. "Only" fixes exactly one person; spotting a collapse (such as "mother's only daughter") saves steps.
5. Check every answer by reading the path backwards; if it does not make sense reversed, redraw.
