# Chapter 21: Statement-Assumption, Conclusion and Course of Action

**What you will learn:** how to read a short statement and decide what it silently takes for granted (assumption), what must be true because of it (conclusion), and what sensible step it calls for (course of action).

## Self-check (3 questions)
Try these before reading. Answers are in the answer key.
1. Statement: "The company will send salary slips by email." Is "Employees have working email addresses" an assumption (something silently taken for granted)? Answer yes or no.
2. Statement: "Of 10 interns, 6 are from commerce." Does "4 interns are not from commerce" follow? Answer yes or no.
3. Problem: "Customers complain that the website is slow." Is "Shut down the website for ever" a sensible course of action? Answer yes or no.

## Concept in plain language

These three question types all start with a short **statement** (a few sentences describing a fact, a plan or a problem). Then you judge proposed items against it. You are not asked what you personally believe. You are asked what the statement itself supports.

### Type 1: Assumption
An **assumption** is something the speaker takes for granted without saying it, and without which the statement would make no sense.

**The negation test.** Flip the proposed assumption to its opposite and ask: does the statement now look pointless or silly? If yes, the assumption is implicit (hidden but needed). If the statement still makes sense, it is not an assumption.

Example: "Please bring your ID card to the office." Proposed: "Employees have ID cards." Flip it: "Employees do not have ID cards." Now the request is pointless. So it is an assumption.

What is usually **not** an assumption:
- A new fact the statement never needed.
- An extreme claim ("everyone", "always", "never", "only", "forever").
- A result the speaker only hopes for (it may or may not happen).

### Type 2: Conclusion (inference)
A **conclusion** here means something that must be true because of the statement alone. Use only what is written. Ask: "Would this be true in every situation where the statement is true?"

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Follows | It is certainly true given the statement |
| Does not follow | It is false, or it might be true but the statement does not guarantee it |

Traps:
- **Reversal.** "All dogs are animals" does not give "All animals are dogs". Likewise "Only people with X can apply" does not mean "everyone with X can apply".
- **Cause.** A statement that two things happened does not say one caused the other.
- **Numbers.** If the statement gives numbers, compute. "Most" means more than half.

### Type 3: Course of action
A **course of action** is a step to fix a problem or improve a situation. A proposed step "should be followed" only if it passes these checks:
1. **Relevant:** it deals with the stated problem.
2. **Practicable:** the people concerned can really do it.
3. **Proportionate:** not extreme (not "close everything", "fire everyone").
4. **No new harm:** it does not create a worse problem (for example hiding the error, or skipping safety checks).

You judge using only the facts in the statement. If an idea would need many facts the statement does not give, it is probably not the best answer.

### The answer format in this chapter
Most questions give two items, I and II, and four options:
- (a) Only I
- (b) Only II
- (c) Both I and II
- (d) Neither I nor II

Mixed questions at the end give one statement with three items; you say Yes or No to each.

> **Why this matters for data work:** Analysts constantly read a stakeholder's sentence (such as "sales fell after we changed the price") and must separate what it says, what it silently assumes and what it does not prove. The same habit helps you write measured recommendations that fit the problem instead of overreacting.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Assumption
**Statement:** A pharmacy displays a board: "Please bring your prescription."
**I.** A prescription is needed for the staff to give certain medicines.
**II.** All visitors to the pharmacy are ill.
1. Test I by flipping it: "A prescription is not needed for any medicine." Then the board makes no sense. So I is implicit.
2. Test II by flipping it: "Some visitors are not ill." The board still makes perfect sense (a visitor may buy soap or collect medicine for a relative). So II is not implicit. Also "all" is extreme.

**Answer: (a) Only I.**

### Example 2: Conclusion with numbers
**Statement:** Of the 80 laptops in an office, 20 are older than 4 years.
**I.** 60 laptops are not older than 4 years.
**II.** Most of the laptops are old.
1. 80 - 20 = 60, so I is certainly true. It follows.
2. "Most" means more than half, that is more than 40. Only 20 are old. So II is false. It does not follow.

**Answer: (a) Only I.**

### Example 3: Conclusion with a reversal trap
**Statement:** All payroll staff are trained in the new software. Divya is trained in the new software.
**I.** Divya is payroll staff.
**II.** Anyone who is payroll staff is trained in the new software.
1. The statement says payroll staff are trained. It does not say only they are trained. People from other teams could be trained too. So Divya could be from another team. I does not follow.
2. II just repeats the first sentence in other words. It follows.

**Answer: (b) Only II.**

### Example 4: Course of action
**Statement (the problem):** Many customers return a brand of shoes of size 8 because they run small.
**I.** The seller should update the size chart and product description to say the shoes run small.
**II.** The seller should stop selling shoes altogether.
1. Check I: relevant (it deals with returns caused by wrong expectations), practicable, proportionate, no new harm. It should be followed.
2. Check II: extreme. One brand's size problem does not justify ending all shoe sales. It fails the "proportionate" check.

**Answer: (a) Only I.**

### Example 5: Mixed questions on one statement
**Statement:** A gym notes that attendance at its 6 am batch fell from 40 to 25 after the road outside was dug up for repairs.
- **(i) Conclusion:** Attendance fell by 15. 40 - 25 = 15. **Yes.**
- **(ii) Conclusion:** The road repair is the only reason for the fall. The statement shows the fall came after the repair, not that it is the only reason. **No.**
- **(iii) Course of action:** Ask members of that batch whether the road works affect them and think about options. Relevant, practicable, mild. **Yes.**

## 2 real-life scenarios (all numbers illustrative)

### Scenario 1: A work-from-home rule (HR policy)
**Statement:** "From next month, employees may work from home on Fridays if their manager agrees."
**I (assumption):** Managers will be able to judge whether a Friday at home suits the work.
**II (assumption):** All employees will work from home on every Friday.
**III (conclusion):** An employee whose manager does not agree must come to the office on Fridays.
1. I: flip it, "Managers cannot judge this". Then asking for manager agreement would be pointless. Implicit.
2. II: extreme ("all", "every"); the rule is optional and needs agreement. Not implicit.
3. III: without agreement the permission does not apply, so working from home is not allowed on that Friday. Strictly the statement does not say the person must be physically in the office (they might be on leave or travelling for work). So we say: **it does not definitely follow**. A careful reader notices the gap between "not allowed to work from home" and "must be in the office".

### Scenario 2: A price cut at a grocery store (commerce)
**Statement:** A grocery store reduced the price of rice from Rs 60 to Rs 54 per kg. It sold 500 kg a week before and 650 kg a week after.
**I:** The price fell by 10 percent. (60 - 54 = 6, and 6 / 60 = 10 percent.) **Follows.**
**II:** Weekly quantity sold rose by 30 percent. (650 - 500 = 150, and 150 / 500 = 30 percent.) **Follows.**
**III:** Weekly rice revenue rose. Before: 60 × 500 = Rs 30,000. After: 54 × 650 = Rs 35,100. It rose by Rs 5,100. **Follows.**
**IV:** The price cut was the reason sales rose. The numbers do not prove it (a festival or a competitor's stock-out could also explain it). **Does not follow.**

## Mistake log
1. **Adding your own facts.** Judge only from the statement. "It is probably true in real life" is not enough for "follows".
2. **Accepting extreme words.** "All", "every", "never", "only", "for ever" almost always sink an assumption or a course of action unless the statement itself uses them.
3. **Reversing a statement.** "All A are B" and "only A can do B" are not the same as their reverses. Check direction.
4. **Confusing a hope with an assumption.** "Sales will rise because of the sheet" is the speaker's hoped result, not something needed for the sentence to make sense. Use the negation test.
5. **Picking a drastic course of action.** Choose the step that is relevant, doable and proportionate, not the dramatic one.

## Recap
1. Assumption: something taken for granted that the statement needs; use the negation test.
2. Conclusion: true in every case where the statement is true; use only the statement and compute any numbers.
3. Course of action: relevant, practicable, proportionate and not creating a new harm.
4. Watch for extreme words, reversals, and cause claims that the statement does not make.
5. Judge the statement, not your opinion of it.
