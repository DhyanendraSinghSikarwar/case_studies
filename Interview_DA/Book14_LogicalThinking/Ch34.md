## Concept in plain language

### What does "debugging your reasoning" mean?

In computing, a **bug** is a mistake in a program, and **debugging** is finding and fixing it. You can do the same with a piece of reasoning: a written-out solution is a chain of steps, and if the final answer is wrong, at least one step has a bug.

A **flawed solution** is a worked answer that looks reasonable but contains a mistake. Reading flawed solutions is excellent practice, because it trains you to catch your own mistakes before anyone else does.

### The key idea: find the FIRST faulty step

Mistakes spread. If step 2 is wrong, steps 3, 4 and 5 may be perfectly calculated and still give a wrong answer. So:

1. Go through the steps in order.
2. Stop at the **first** step that is not correct.
3. Name the type of mistake.
4. Repair that step and recompute everything after it.

The first faulty step is the root cause. Later steps are just carrying the damage.

### The debugging routine

| Check | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Re-read | Did I answer the question asked? | Asked for "per month", answered "per year" |
| Re-do each step | Is each calculation right on its own? | 90 / 12 is 7.5, not 8.5 |
| Units | Do units match and convert properly? | 2 h 30 min is 2.5 hours, not 2.30 |
| Base | A percent of WHAT? | 10% of the new price, not the old |
| Plug in simple numbers | Does the method work with easy values? | Try 100 as the starting price |
| Reverse check | Put the answer back into the problem | Does 52.5 for 7 pens fit 90 for 12? |
| Extremes | Is the answer possible? | A share cannot exceed 100% |
| Hidden assumption | Did the solution assume something the problem never said? | "The sample represents everyone" |

### A catalogue of common bugs

| Bug type | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Arithmetic slip | A calculation is simply wrong |
| Unit mismatch | Mixing minutes with hours, or decimal time with clock time |
| Wrong base | A percentage taken of the wrong starting number |
| Average of averages | Averaging group averages without weighting by group size |
| Off-by-one (fencepost) | Miscounting by one when counting items or gaps |
| Wrong operation | Adding when you should multiply, and so on |
| Converse error | Treating "if A then B" as if it said "if B then A" |
| Double counting | Counting the same thing twice (or an ordered pair as two) |
| Hasty generalisation | Making a claim about everyone from a few cases |
| Unsupported step | Drawing a link the facts do not give you |
| Misread condition | Using a number or rule the problem did not state |

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts check their own formulas, queries and summaries constantly. Most wrong numbers in reports come from small bugs of exactly these kinds: a wrong base, a mixed unit, an average of averages, a miscounted range. Learning to find the first faulty step makes you faster at fixing your own work and better at reviewing other people's.

## 5 solved examples

All names and numbers are invented.

### Example 1: Percent up, percent down

**Flawed solution.** "A price rises 25% and then falls 20%. 25 - 20 = 5, so the price is 5% higher than at the start."

- Step 1 is the fault: subtracting percents is wrong because they are percents of **different bases**. The 25% is of the original price; the 20% is of the higher price.
- Test with 100: after the rise it is 125. A 20% fall is 20% of 125 = 25, so the price is 100.
- **Corrected:** 1.25 x 0.80 = 1.00. The price is back to where it started, no change.
- *Bug type:* wrong base.

### Example 2: Fence posts

**Flawed solution.** "A straight fence is 100 m long with a post every 5 m, including both ends. Posts = 100 / 5 = 20."

- 100 / 5 = 20 counts the **gaps** between posts, not the posts. There is one more post than gaps.
- Test with a short fence: 10 m with a post every 5 m has posts at 0, 5 and 10, so 3 posts but only 2 gaps.
- **Corrected:** 20 gaps + 1 = 21 posts.
- *Bug type:* off-by-one (fencepost).

### Example 3: Turning a rule around

**Flawed solution.** "Rule: every promoted employee had a rating of 4 or more. Priya has a rating of 4.5, so Priya was promoted."

- The rule says promoted implies rating 4 or more. It does not say the reverse.
- Many people may have a rating of 4 or more and not be promoted. Test: if 10 people scored 4 or more and only 3 were promoted, the rule is still true.
- **Corrected:** no conclusion about Priya's promotion can be drawn.
- *Bug type:* converse error.

### Example 4: Average of averages

**Flawed solution.** "Team A has 10 people with an average score of 80. Team B has 30 people with an average score of 60. The overall average is (80 + 60) / 2 = 70."

- Step 1 is the fault: the two teams are different sizes, so they should not count equally.
- Total score: 10 x 80 = 800 and 30 x 60 = 1,800. Together 2,600 over 40 people.
- **Corrected:** 2,600 / 40 = 65.
- *Reasonableness check:* most people are in Team B (average 60), so the overall figure must be closer to 60 than to 80. 65 fits; 70 does not.
- *Bug type:* average of averages.

### Example 5: A unit slip

**Flawed solution.** "A clerk files 3 documents a minute. In 2 hours that is 3 x 2 = 6 documents."

- The rate is per **minute** but the time was used in **hours**.
- **Corrected:** 2 hours = 120 minutes, so 3 x 120 = 360 documents.
- *Reasonableness check:* 6 documents in two hours means one every 20 minutes, far slower than 3 a minute.
- *Bug type:* unit mismatch.

## 2 real-life scenarios

Numbers are **illustrative**.

### Scenario 1 (commerce): margin or markup?

**Flawed claim.** A shop buys a shirt for 800 and sells it for 1,000. The manager says, "Our margin is 25%." Here **margin** means profit as a share of the **selling price**.

- Profit = 1,000 - 800 = 200.
- 200 / 800 = 25%: this divides profit by the **cost**, which is called the **markup**.
- Margin as defined: 200 / 1,000 = 20%.
- *Bug type:* wrong base. The number 25% is real, but it answers a different question. Always ask "percent of what?" and agree on definitions first.

### Scenario 2 (data and HR): the happy survey

**Flawed claim.** An HR survey went to 200 employees. 50 replied, and 40 of those said they were satisfied. The report says, "80% of employees are satisfied."

- 40 / 50 = 80% is true of the **respondents** only.
- 150 people did not reply and we know nothing about them.
- Across all 200 employees, the share satisfied lies anywhere from 40 / 200 = 20% (if no non-respondent is satisfied) up to (40 + 150) / 200 = 95% (if all are).
- *Bug type:* hasty generalisation. A fair statement is "80% of those who replied (25% of staff replied) were satisfied".

## Mistake log

1. **Fixing only the last step.** The visible error is often downstream; trace back to the first wrong step.
2. **Trusting a tidy layout.** A solution can look neat and still be wrong. Re-do each calculation.
3. **Not testing with simple numbers.** Plugging in 100, or a tiny case, exposes many bugs in seconds.
4. **Naming the symptom, not the bug.** "The answer is too big" is a symptom; "wrong base" is the bug.
5. **Stopping after finding one bug.** Re-run the corrected solution; there can be a second error.

## Recap

1. A written solution is a chain of steps; a wrong answer means a bad step somewhere.
2. Find the FIRST faulty step; everything after it is just carrying the damage.
3. Name the bug type: arithmetic, unit, base, average of averages, off-by-one, converse, and so on.
4. Check with simple numbers, units, a reverse check and reasonableness.
5. Rebuild the solution from the repaired step and check it again.
