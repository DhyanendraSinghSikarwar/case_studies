# Chapter 19: Truth-Teller and Liar Puzzles

**What you will learn:** how to work out who is telling the truth and who is lying when each person makes a statement, using one reliable method (assume, follow, check) and a full table of cases when the puzzle is bigger.

## Self-check (3 questions)

1. A person who always lies says: "It rained yesterday." Did it rain yesterday?
2. Tomas always tells the truth. He says: "Uma is a liar." Uma says: "The shop opens at 8." Does the shop open at 8?

## Concept in plain language

### The two kinds of people

In these puzzles every person is one of two kinds, and the kind never changes during the puzzle:

- A **truth-teller** says only true statements.
- A **liar** says only false statements.

A **statement** is a sentence that is either true or false (Chapter 2). The whole puzzle rests on one link:

| Speaker is a | Then the statement they make is |
|---|---|
| truth-teller | true |
| liar | false |

And the link works backwards too: if a statement is true, the speaker is a truth-teller; if it is false, the speaker is a liar. Your job is to find a way of labelling everyone "truth-teller" or "liar" so that every statement matches this table.

### What "false" really means

This is where most slips happen. A liar's statement is **false**, which is not the same as "the opposite of everything".

| What the liar says | What is actually true |
|---|---|
| "Ravi did it." | Ravi did not do it. |
| "Ravi or Sana did it." | Neither Ravi nor Sana did it. (Both parts must be false.) |
| "Ravi and Sana did it." | At least one of them did not do it. (It could be both, it could be one.) |
| "Everyone here is honest." | At least one person here is a liar (not necessarily all). |
| "At least 2 of us are liars." | Fewer than 2 are liars (0 or 1). |

Notice the pattern: to find what is true when a liar speaks, **negate the statement** (Chapter 5). Negating "and" gives "at least one is not", and negating "or" gives "neither".

### The method: assume, follow, check

1. **List** the people and write each statement in short form.
2. **Pick** one person and **assume** a kind: "suppose she is a truth-teller".
3. **Follow** the consequences: if her statement is true, what does it tell you about others? Take each one in turn.
4. **Check** every statement against the table above. If one statement does not match its speaker, you have a **contradiction**, so the assumption was wrong.
5. **Flip** the assumption and repeat. If both assumptions fail, the puzzle has no answer; if both work, it has two answers and needs another clue.
6. When one assumption survives, still try the other to **prove it is the only one**.

### The full-table method (brute force)

With n people there are 2 x 2 x ... x 2 = 2^n ways to label them (2 people: 4 ways, 3 people: 8, 4 people: 16, 5 people: 32). For small puzzles you can write them all down, as in a truth table (Chapter 3), and test each row. It is slow but cannot go wrong, and it is how every puzzle in this chapter was checked.

| Person 1 | Person 2 | Person 3 |
|---|---|---|
| truth-teller | truth-teller | truth-teller |
| truth-teller | truth-teller | liar |
| ... | ... | ... |
| liar | liar | liar |

### Extra information that helps

- **Facts about the world** (who did it, which day, how many): the statements also say things about these. A puzzle then looks for the one version of the facts and the one labelling that fit together.
- **Count clues**, for example "exactly one of the three is lying". Cross out every row of the table that breaks the count.
- **Chains**: "A says B is a liar, B says C is a liar". Start from anyone whose kind is already known and walk along the chain.

### Does the puzzle have exactly one answer?

A good puzzle has one answer. A puzzle where two different labellings both fit is **not finished**: you need one more clue. All puzzles in this chapter have exactly one answer.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts often get two sources that disagree: a billing system and a courier report, or a badge log and a supervisor sheet. The "assume one is reliable, follow the consequences, check for contradiction" habit is the same one used to find out which source is faulty. A source that is wrong today is not necessarily wrong everywhere, so these puzzles train careful, case by case checking rather than trust or distrust in a person.

## Five solved examples

### Example 1: one speaker (a known liar)

**Problem.** Jaya is known to be a liar. She says: "I checked the invoice." Did Jaya check the invoice?

1. A liar's statement is false. *Why:* that is the whole rule for liars.
2. So "I checked the invoice" is false.
3. Jaya did not check the invoice.

**Answer:** No, she did not.

### Example 2: two people talking about each other

**Problem.** Meena says: "Naveen is a liar." Naveen says: "We are both liars." Who is who?

1. **Assume Meena is a truth-teller.** Then her statement is true, so Naveen is a liar. *Why:* a truth-teller's statement is true.
2. Check Naveen: he says "we are both liars". Meena is a truth-teller, so this is false. A liar says false statements, so this fits Naveen being a liar. No contradiction.
3. **Assume Meena is a liar.** Then "Naveen is a liar" is false, so Naveen is a truth-teller. *Why:* a liar's statement is false.
4. Check Naveen: he says "we are both liars", which must then be true. But Meena is a liar and Naveen is a truth-teller, so "both liars" is false. A truth-teller cannot say a false statement. Contradiction, so this assumption fails.
5. Only step 1 survives. *Why:* we tried both options for Meena and exactly one works.

**Answer:** Meena is a truth-teller, Naveen is a liar. (Checked against all 4 possible labellings.)

### Example 3: a culprit and a count clue

**Problem.** Money went missing from a till. The thief is exactly one of Rina, Sam and Tara. Exactly one of the three is lying. Rina says: "It was Tara." Sam says: "It was not me." Tara says: "Sam is lying." Who took the money?

1. Try each possible thief and mark which statements come out true.

| If the thief is | Rina ("Tara") | Sam ("not me") | Tara ("Sam is lying") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rina | false | true | false (Sam is truthful, so "Sam is lying" is false) |
| Sam | false | false | true (Sam's statement is false, so he is lying) |
| Tara | true | true | false (Sam's statement is true, so he is not lying) |

2. Count the false statements in each row (they are the liars): Rina row 2 liars, Sam row 2 liars, Tara row 1 liar. *Why:* the clue says exactly one liar.
3. Only the "Tara" row has exactly one liar.

**Answer:** Tara took the money (and Tara is the one who lied). Checked: the other two rows give 2 liars.

### Example 4: a chain with a counting statement

**Problem.** Oma says: "Pavan is a liar." Pavan says: "Qadir is a liar." Qadir says: "Exactly one of Oma and Pavan is a liar." Who are the liars?

1. **Assume Oma is a truth-teller.** Then Pavan is a liar. Pavan's statement is then false, so Qadir is a truth-teller. Check Qadir: "exactly one of Oma and Pavan is a liar" is true (Pavan only). Matches a truth-teller. No contradiction.
2. **Assume Oma is a liar.** Then "Pavan is a liar" is false, so Pavan is a truth-teller, so "Qadir is a liar" is true, so Qadir is a liar. Check Qadir: he says "exactly one of Oma and Pavan is a liar". Oma is a liar and Pavan is a truth-teller, so exactly one is a liar: the statement is true. But Qadir is a liar and cannot say a true statement. Contradiction.
3. Only step 1 survives.

**Answer:** Oma and Qadir are truth-tellers; Pavan is the only liar.

### Example 5: numbers and one liar

**Problem.** Between 1 and 20 forms were mis-filed. Exactly one of three clerks is lying. Rao says: "More than 10 forms." Sen says: "The number is a multiple of 5." Tara says: "Exactly 15 forms." How many forms were mis-filed?

1. Exactly two statements are true. List which numbers from 1 to 20 make each true: Rao: 11 to 20. Sen: 5, 10, 15, 20. Tara: only 15.
2. If all three were true the number would be 15, but that gives zero liars, which breaks the clue. *Why:* the clue demands exactly one liar.
3. Tara cannot be telling the truth, or the number would be 15. So Tara is the liar, and Rao and Sen are truthful: the number is more than 10, a multiple of 5, and not 15.
4. Multiples of 5 above 10 are 15 and 20. Not 15 leaves 20.

**Answer:** 20 forms. (Checked: if either Rao or Sen were the liar, no number fits.)

## Two real-life scenarios

All names and numbers are invented (illustrative).

### Scenario 1 (commerce): the missing parcel

Parcel 4410 is somewhere in the warehouse: the front desk, the loading bay or the mailroom. Three staff were asked. Pooja: "It is at the front desk or the mailroom." Raghu: "It is at the front desk or the loading bay." Sunil: "It is not at the loading bay." The supervisor knows exactly one of the three is wrong.

- Try the front desk: Pooja right, Raghu right, Sunil right (0 wrong). Does not fit.
- Try the loading bay: Pooja wrong, Raghu right, Sunil wrong (2 wrong). Does not fit.
- Try the mailroom: Pooja right, Raghu wrong, Sunil right (1 wrong). Fits.
- Result: check the mailroom. Raghu was the wrong one.
- Lesson: when the number of errors is known, the table of cases settles it without guessing.

### Scenario 2 (data and HR): which system is faulty today?

Was Tanvi present on Monday? The badge log says "present". The supervisor sheet says "absent". The payroll system says "the badge log is reliable". Exactly one of the three sources is faulty today.

- The badge log and the supervisor sheet disagree, so at least one is faulty. Since only one is faulty, payroll is reliable.
- Payroll is reliable and says the badge log is reliable, so the badge log is right: Tanvi was present.
- Then the supervisor sheet (which said "absent") is the faulty one.
- Lesson: when two records conflict, a third independent record that you trust breaks the tie, and you should note the faulty source for follow up.

## Mistake log

1. **Thinking a liar says the opposite of everything.** A liar's statement is false, and that is all. A false "A and B did it" only means at least one of them did not.
2. **Stopping at the first assumption that works.** Try the other assumption too, so you know the answer is unique and you do not miss a contradiction.
3. **Checking only the speaker you assumed.** After following a chain, go back and check every statement against its speaker's kind.
4. **Forgetting the count clue.** "Exactly one is lying" removes every labelling with two or zero liars; use it early.
5. **Treating "not a truth-teller" as "a liar" when there are more than two kinds.** That shortcut works in the two-kind puzzles of this chapter but not when a third kind (like the switcher in Q19) exists.

## Recap

- A truth-teller's statements are all true; a liar's statements are all false.
- To use a liar's statement, negate it ("or" becomes "neither", "and" becomes "at least one is not").
- Assume a kind, follow the consequences, check every statement, and flip if there is a contradiction.
- For small puzzles list all 2^n labellings and cross out rows that break a statement or a count clue.
- Always confirm that the surviving answer is the only one.
