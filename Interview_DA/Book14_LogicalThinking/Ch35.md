# Chapter 35: Mixed Case Studies (Commerce plus Data)

**What you will learn:** how to take a short, messy business scenario and attack it with every logic tool from Chapters 1 to 34: parse the claim, list the premises, check conditions and edge cases, test with a small table, check causation and bias, and conclude with the limits stated.

---

## Concept in plain language

Real work does not arrive labelled "this is a truth table question". A manager sends you three sentences and a small table and asks, "Is this right?". Inside that one request there may be an if-then rule, a group count, a chart that looks odd, and a hidden bias. A **mixed case** trains you to spot which tool fits which sentence.

All names, shops and numbers in this chapter are **invented and illustrative**. No real company or person is meant.

### The six-step attack

| Step | Question to ask yourself | Tools from earlier chapters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Parse the claim | What exactly is being asserted? Is it "all", "some", "none", "if then"? | Statements, quantifiers, negation |
| 2. List the premises | What facts are given, and what is quietly assumed? | Arguments, hidden assumptions |
| 3. Check conditions and edge cases | When does the rule apply? What about exactly 50, zero, empty, or negative? | If-then, converse and contrapositive, boundary cases |
| 4. Test with a small table | Put 4 to 8 rows in a table (or a truth table or Venn counts) and compute by hand | Truth tables, sets, ordering, algorithm tracing |
| 5. Check causation and bias | Could something else explain it? Who is missing from the data? Is the chart fair? | Correlation, confounders, sampling bias, misleading statistics |
| 6. Conclude with limits | Say what the data supports, what it does not, and what you would check next | Calibrated conclusions |

Two habits make this work:

- **Write the sub-claims separately.** A scenario with one big conclusion usually hides three smaller claims. Judge each one on its own.
- **Always end with a limit.** "Based on these 6 orders, the rule held, but 6 orders cannot show it holds for all orders" is a stronger answer than a flat "yes".

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts rarely get a clean question. Stakeholders hand over a claim plus a table, and the job is to decide what the table can and cannot support. A fixed routine (parse, premises, edge cases, small table, bias, limits) keeps you from jumping to the first number that looks convincing.

## 2 worked examples

### Worked example 1: "Coupons make customers spend more"

**Scenario (illustrative).** A shop's report says: "Customers who used the coupon spent more, so the coupon increases spending." The coupon email was sent only to customers who had spent more than 80 in the previous quarter. Last month's data:

| Customer | Used coupon? | Spend last month |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Yes | 90 |
| C2 | Yes | 120 |
| C3 | Yes | 105 |
| C4 | No | 50 |
| C5 | No | 70 |
| C6 | No | 60 |

**Step 1: parse the claim.** There are two claims glued together: (a) coupon users spent more, and (b) the coupon *caused* the extra spending. (a) is a fact about the table; (b) is a causal claim. *Why:* splitting them lets you accept the first and question the second.

**Step 2: test (a) with the table.** Coupon users: (90 + 120 + 105) / 3 = 315 / 3 = 105. Non-users: (50 + 70 + 60) / 3 = 180 / 3 = 60. The difference is 45. So (a) is true for these six customers. *Why:* check the number before arguing about its meaning.

**Step 3: list the premises behind (b).** To say "the coupon caused it", we need the coupon users and non-users to be alike before the coupon. *Why:* causal claims need a fair comparison.

**Step 4: check that premise.** The email went only to people who already spent more than 80 earlier. So the coupon group was selected for being big spenders. That is a **selection bias** (the groups were different before the coupon existed). *Why:* a "big spender" label alone could produce the 105 versus 60 gap.

**Step 5: conclude with limits.** The data supports "coupon users spent more". It does not support "the coupon increased spending". A fair test would give the coupon to a random half of similar customers and compare. With only six customers, even that would be a weak test.

### Worked example 2: "The new subject line doubled opens"

**Scenario (illustrative).** An email team reports: "Subject line B doubled our open rate compared with subject line A."

| Version | Emails sent | Opened | Open rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 180 | 36 | 20% |
| B | 10 | 4 | 40% |

**Step 1: parse the claim.** "Doubled" is a relative statement: 40% is 2 times 20%. *Why:* "doubled" and "up 20 points" sound different, so name which one is meant.

**Step 2: recompute.** 36 / 180 = 0.20 and 4 / 10 = 0.40. The gap is 20 **percentage points** (40 minus 20), which is a 100% relative increase (20 / 20). *Why:* both ways of saying it are correct; the second sounds bigger.

**Step 3: check the denominators.** B was sent to only 10 people. *Why:* a rate from 10 people can swing wildly. If just 2 fewer people had opened, B would show 2 / 10 = 20%, the same as A.

**Step 4: test the edge case.** One person more or less changes B's rate by 10 points. For A, one person changes the rate by about 0.56 points (1 / 180). *Why:* this shows how unstable the B figure is.

**Step 5: conclude with limits.** The arithmetic is right, but the evidence is thin. The honest statement: "B looked better in a 10-email trial; send both versions to comparable groups of a few hundred before deciding."

Each case is a short scenario with a small table of **invented, illustrative numbers** and several sub-questions that need different tools. Work with a pencil. **Case 1: The free-shipping rule**

An online shop (illustrative) publishes the rule: "If an order total is 50 or more, then shipping is free." The support team pulls six orders to see whether the system follows it.

| Order | Total | Shipping free? |
|---|---|---|
| O1 | 62.00 | Yes |
| O2 | 48.00 | Yes (customer used a promo code) |
| O3 | 50.00 | No |
| O4 | 35.00 | No |
| O5 | 80.00 | Yes |
| O6 | 49.90 | No |

Let A = "total is 50 or more" and B = "shipping is free".

**1a.** Which orders, if any, break the published rule? Explain in one sentence.

**1b.** O2 has free shipping with a total below 50. Does O2 break the rule? Why or why not?

**1c.** Fill in a count of the six orders in each of the four truth-table rows (A true or false, B true or false). In which row can the rule A implies B be false?

**1d.** A manager says: "So every free-shipping order must be 50 or more." Which logical form is this (converse, inverse or contrapositive of the rule)? Is it supported by the table?

**1e.** Write the contrapositive of the published rule in plain words.

**Case 2: Where do our shoppers buy?**

A retailer (illustrative) surveys 200 customers about the channels they used last year: the mobile app, the website, or a physical store. Some use more than one.

| Group | Customers |
|---|---|
| Used the app | 120 |
| Used the website | 90 |
| Used a store | 70 |
| App and website | 40 |
| App and store | 30 |
| Website and store | 25 |
| All three | 10 |

The analyst writes: "Almost every shopper uses at least one digital channel (app or website), and the app users are mostly also web users."

**2a.** How many customers used at least one of the three channels? How many used none?

**2b.** How many used exactly one channel? Exactly two channels?

**2c.** Is the claim "app users are mostly also web users" supported? Give the percentage.

**2d.** Evaluate: "Every store customer also uses the app or the website." True or false? State the count that decides it.

**2e.** How many customers used a digital channel (app or website, with or without a store)? Is "almost every shopper" a fair summary of the 200?

**Case 3: The packing-line order**

A small warehouse (illustrative) handles each parcel in six tasks by one worker, in some order, with these durations and rules.

| Task | Code | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| Receive goods | R | 2 |
| Count items | C | 1 |
| Pack | P | 3 |
| Label | L | 1 |
| Quality check | Q | 1 |
| Dispatch | D | 2 |

Rules: R comes before C and before P. P comes before L. C comes before Q. L and Q both come before D.

**3a.** Is the order R, C, Q, P, L, D allowed? Is R, P, C, L, D, Q allowed?

**3b.** How many different valid orders exist in total?

**3c.** If six workers could each take one task at the same time (respecting the rules), what is the shortest total time for the whole job?

**3d.** One worker does everything, starting at 07:00. The truck leaves at 14:00 and takes only finished parcels. If the worker stays strictly on one task at a time, is the statement "the parcel will make the truck" true or false?

**3e.** In the six-worker plan of 3c, which tasks could start late without delaying the finish? By how many hours?

**Case 4: The soaring revenue chart**

A newsletter shows a bar chart titled "Revenue skyrockets" (illustrative). The numbers behind it:

| Month | Revenue |
|---|---|
| January | 98,000 |
| February | 99,500 |
| March | 101,000 |
| April | 102,500 |

The vertical axis of the chart starts at 97,000, not at 0, so each bar's height is its revenue minus 97,000.

**4a.** What is the true percentage growth from January to April (one decimal place)?

**4b.** What ratio does the April bar height have to the January bar height? What impression does that give?

**4c.** Finish the sentence with "if" and "then": a chart's bar heights are proportional to the values only if ______.

**4d.** The editor says "growth is steady, so revenue will be 114,500 in December". What rule of thumb is being used, and what are two reasons to treat it with caution?

**4e.** Rewrite the chart title and axis so the picture is honest.

**Case 5: Which campaign worked better?**

A shop (illustrative) sent two email campaigns, A and B, and counted purchases. Customers are split into **returning** (bought before) and **new**.

| Campaign | Returning: emailed | Returning: bought | New: emailed | New: bought |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 20 | 450 | 30 |
| B | 1,000 | 380 | 100 | 6 |

**5a.** Compute the overall purchase rate for A and for B. Which looks better?

**5b.** Compute the rate for A and B inside each group. Which campaign is better in each group?

**5c.** Evaluate: "Campaign A beat campaign B in every group." True or false?

**5d.** Explain in plain words why the overall winner and the group winners disagree. Name the effect.

**5e.** Which recommendation would you give, and what limits would you state? (Hint: who chose which customers got which email?)

**Case 6: The cart-discount algorithm**

A checkout script (illustrative) follows these steps:

1. Set total = 0 and discount_given = No.
2. For each item price in the cart, in order: add it to total. If total is greater than 100 and discount_given is No, subtract 10 from total and set discount_given = Yes.
3. Show total.

| Cart | Prices in order |
|---|---|
| X | 40, 35, 30, 20 |
| Y | 60, 40 |
| Z | 120, -30 |
| Z2 | -30, 120 |

(A negative price is a refund line.)

**6a.** Trace the algorithm for carts X and Y and give each final total.

**6b.** Why does Y get no discount even though its prices sum to exactly 100? What single change in step 2 would give it one?

**6c.** Trace Z and Z2. They hold the same prices in a different order. What are the totals?

**6d.** For carts with only positive prices, does the order of items change the final total? Explain.

**6e.** What does the algorithm output for an empty cart? Is that sensible?

**Case 7: How many packers do we need?**

A new meal-kit service (illustrative) plans its packing room. The planner writes down these assumptions:

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Households in the delivery area | 250,000 |
| Share who subscribe | 4% |
| Boxes per subscriber per week | 2 |
| Packing days per week | 5 |
| Boxes one packer packs per hour | 30 |
| Productive hours per packer per day | 6 |

**7a.** Estimate boxes per week, boxes per day, and boxes one packer packs per day.

**7b.** How many packers are needed? Round sensibly and say which direction you rounded and why.

**7c.** The subscriber share is uncertain, between 3% and 5%. Give the range of packers needed.

**7d.** A colleague says "8 packers will be plenty". What share of the weekly boxes could 8 packers handle under these assumptions?

**7e.** Name two assumptions in the table that you would check first, and why.

**Case 8: The review page**

A product page (illustrative) shows eight reviews.

| Review | Stars | Verified purchase? | Helpful votes |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | 5 | Yes | 12 |
| R2 | 5 | Yes | 8 |
| R3 | 5 | No | 1 |
| R4 | 4 | Yes | 3 |
| R5 | 2 | Yes | 6 |
| R6 | 1 | No | 9 |
| R7 | 5 | Yes | 0 |
| R8 | 3 | No | 2 |

**8a.** Decide true or false for each: (i) "Every 5-star review is a verified purchase." (ii) "Some verified review has fewer than 3 stars." (iii) "No unverified review has more than 2 stars."

**8b.** Write the negation of statement (i) in plain words.

**8c.** Compute the average stars for all reviews, for verified reviews only, and for unverified reviews only.

**8d.** The seller advertises "rated 4.2 stars by customers". Which of your averages is that, and what is misleading about it?

**8e.** Name one bias that makes review pages unrepresentative of all buyers.

**Case 9: The average customer spends 153**

A loyalty report (illustrative) says "the average customer spends 153 per month". The nine customers in the sample:

| Customer | Spend |
|---|---|
| K1 | 12 |
| K2 | 15 |
| K3 | 18 |
| K4 | 20 |
| K5 | 22 |
| K6 | 25 |
| K7 | 30 |
| K8 | 35 |
| K9 | 1,200 |

A separate gold-offer rule says: a customer is eligible if (they are a member AND spend 50 or more) OR they were referred by a friend, but never if their account is flagged. Let M = member, S = spend 50 or more, R = referred, F = flagged.

**9a.** Verify the mean and find the median.

**9b.** Which one describes a typical customer better, and why? What happens to the mean if K9 is removed?

**9c.** Write the gold-offer rule as a logical expression using M, S, R, F.

**9d.** Of the 16 truth-table rows for M, S, R, F, in how many is a customer eligible?

**9e.** Is a flagged, referred, non-member customer eligible? Is an unflagged non-member with spend 80 and no referral eligible?

**Case 10: The fraud flag**

A payments team (illustrative) runs a fraud detector on 10,000 orders. Past audits suggest 1% of orders are fraud. The detector flags 95% of true fraud and also wrongly flags 5% of genuine orders.

| | Flagged | Not flagged | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fraud | ? | ? | 100 |
| Genuine | ? | ? | 9,900 |
| Total | ? | ? | 10,000 |

A manager says: "The detector is 95% accurate, so any flagged order is 95% likely to be fraud."

**10a.** Fill in the table (counts, not percentages).

**10b.** Of the flagged orders, what share are really fraud?

**10c.** Which logical mistake is the manager making? (Think about "if fraud then flagged" versus "if flagged then fraud".)

**10d.** If a reviewer needs 6 minutes per flagged order, how many hours of review does the flagged list need?

**10e.** If the false-flag rate on genuine orders is lowered to 2% (same 95% catch rate), what happens to the share of flagged orders that are fraud?

## Mistake log

1. **Judging "the rule is broken" when the "if" part is false.** An if-then rule says nothing about orders that fail the condition (Case 1, O2). Only "condition true, result false" breaks it.
2. **Trusting a total or an average without looking at groups.** Overall rates can reverse inside groups (Case 5), and one huge value can drag a mean far from typical (Case 9).
3. **Reading a chart by its bar heights instead of its axis.** Always ask where the axis starts and what the real percentage change is (Case 4).
4. **Mixing up "A implies B" with "B implies A".** A 95% catch rate is not a 95% chance that a flagged item is fraud (Case 10).
5. **Forgetting the boundary.** "Over 100" and "100 or more" differ at exactly 100, and order or empty inputs can change algorithm output (Case 6).

## Recap

1. Parse first: split every claim into small, separate sub-claims (facts, rules, causes).
2. List the premises and test the edges: exactly at the limit, empty, negative, reordered.
3. Put a few rows in a small table (truth table, Venn counts, group rates) and compute before opinions.
4. Ask who is missing, who chose the groups, where the axis starts, and how big the sample is.
5. End with a conclusion plus its limits: what the data supports, what it does not, and what to check next.
