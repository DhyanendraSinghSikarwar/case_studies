# Chapter 22: Data Sufficiency

**What you will learn:** how to decide whether the information you have is enough to answer a question, without having to work out the answer itself.

## Concept in plain language

### The idea in one sentence

Before you calculate anything, ask: **"Do I have enough information to get exactly one answer?"**

That is all "data sufficiency" means. Sufficient means enough. Insufficient means not enough.

A beginner's instinct is to rush into calculating. This chapter trains the opposite habit: stop, look at what you were given, and judge whether it is enough. In real work this saves time. A manager who says "tell me the attrition rate" may have given you the number who left but not the headcount. You cannot calculate it, and the right reply is not a guess but "I need one more number."

### What "enough" means

Information is **sufficient** when it leaves only **one possible answer**.

- For a question that asks for a **value** ("What is the price?"), sufficient means exactly one value fits.
- For a **yes/no** question ("Is the team bigger than 10?"), sufficient means the answer is **always yes** or **always no**. If the facts allow a yes in one situation and a no in another, the facts are **not sufficient**.

Notice that "always no" is a perfectly good, sufficient answer. Students often think "no" means "I could not find out". It does not. "Definitely no" is a firm answer. "Maybe" is the only non-answer.

### The standard question format

Most data sufficiency questions have three parts:

- **The question** (and sometimes some background facts in brackets, which count as true for everything below).
- **Statement I** and **Statement II**, two extra facts.
- **Five answer choices**, always the same.

| Choice | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Statement I alone is sufficient; Statement II alone is not. |
| B | Statement II alone is sufficient; Statement I alone is not. |
| C | Neither alone is sufficient, but both together are. |
| D | Each statement alone is sufficient. |
| E | Even both together are not sufficient. |

Some books list these in a different order or wording. The meanings are the same; always read the options given. In this chapter we use the table above.

### The method (four steps)

1. **Read the question and background.** Underline what is being asked: a value or a yes/no. Note facts in brackets; they are free information that always applies.
2. **Test Statement I alone.** Pretend Statement II does not exist. Can you get exactly one answer? Write "yes" or "no" for I.
3. **Test Statement II alone.** Forget Statement I completely (do not carry anything across). Write "yes" or "no" for II.
4. **Only if both were "no", test them together.** If together give one answer, choose C. If not, choose E.

A quick way to read the result:

| I alone | II alone | Together | Choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| sufficient | not | (not needed) | A |
| not | sufficient | (not needed) | B |
| not | not | sufficient | C |
| sufficient | sufficient | (not needed) | D |
| not | not | not sufficient | E |

### Tools to test "is it one answer?"

- **Count the unknowns.** If a quantity depends on two unknowns and you have only one fact linking them, there are many answers.
- **Try two different examples.** To show a statement is *not* sufficient, find two situations that both obey the statement but give different answers. To show it *is* sufficient, explain why no second answer can exist.
- **Look for the answer without calculating it.** You only need to know that a single answer exists, not what it is.

### Hidden helpers and traps

- **Background facts count.** "(All packs cost the same.)" or "(12 equal monthly payments, no bonus.)" is information. Use it.
- **Whole numbers can narrow things down.** If a quantity must be a whole number (people, boxes), a statement that allows many real-number answers may allow only one whole-number answer.
- **Repeating is not adding.** "Twice as much" and "half as much" can be the same fact in different words. Two statements that say the same thing together are no stronger than one.
- **Irrelevant facts.** A true statement can be useless for the question (the colour of a van does not tell you its speed).
- **Do not import your own assumptions.** If the question does not say every item costs the same, do not assume it.
- **Do not carry Statement I into the test of Statement II.** Each statement is tested alone first. Only step 4 uses both.

### Sufficiency in real decisions

Outside tests, the same habit asks: **"What is the smallest extra fact that would settle this?"** Good analysts name the missing piece instead of guessing. Two things to keep apart:

- **Enough to answer this exact question** (what this chapter practises).
- **Enough to be sure the world is that way** (a bigger topic: sample sizes, bias, measurement). You will meet that in Part 4, Critical Thinking.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts are often handed a question and a partial table. Checking whether the data can answer the question, before building a chart or a formula, avoids confident answers built on missing information. Naming the one missing fact is also a useful thing to say when you ask a colleague for data.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Choice A (the first statement is enough)

**Question:** What is Kavya's daily wage? (She is paid the same amount for every day she works.)
**Statement I:** She earned Rs 7,500 for 5 days of work.
**Statement II:** She worked 5 days last week.

1. **Statement I alone.** Same pay each day, so Rs 7,500 over 5 days gives 7,500 / 5 = Rs 1,500 a day. *Why:* the background fact "same every day" turns total pay into a single daily rate. Sufficient.
2. **Statement II alone.** It says how many days she worked, but nothing about money. The daily wage could be Rs 800 or Rs 2,000. *Why:* a fact about days cannot fix an amount. Not sufficient.
3. Only I is sufficient, so the answer is **A**.

### Example 2: Choice C (need both)

**Question:** How many people are in the Support team? (Everyone is either full-time or part-time.)
**Statement I:** 12 people are part-time.
**Statement II:** The part-time people are one quarter of the team.

1. **I alone.** 12 are part-time, but the full-time count is unknown. The team could be 20 or 100. Not sufficient.
2. **II alone.** "One quarter are part-time" gives a ratio only. A team of 40 (10 part-time) and a team of 80 (20 part-time) both fit. Not sufficient. *Why:* a ratio without one real count cannot give a size.
3. **Together.** 12 is one quarter of the team, so the team is 12 x 4 = 48. One answer. *Why:* the ratio supplies the missing multiplier and the count supplies the scale.
4. Neither alone, both together: **C**.

### Example 3: Choice B (a yes/no question with a firm "no")

**Question:** Did the store meet its monthly target of 500 units?
**Statement I:** The store sold at least 450 units.
**Statement II:** The store sold at most 480 units.

1. **I alone.** 450 units is below target, 520 is above target; both obey "at least 450". The answer is sometimes no, sometimes yes. Not sufficient.
2. **II alone.** The most it could have sold is 480, which is below 500. So the answer is **always no**. *Why:* a firm "no" is a definite answer, so II is sufficient.
3. Only II works: **B**.

### Example 4: Choice D (each alone is enough)

**Question:** What is the side of a square display board? (A side length is always a positive number.)
**Statement I:** Its perimeter is 36 cm.
**Statement II:** Its area is 81 square cm.

1. **I alone.** A square has 4 equal sides, so side = 36 / 4 = 9 cm. Sufficient.
2. **II alone.** Area = side x side = 81. Positive numbers whose square is 81: only 9. *Why:* the background fact "positive" rules out minus 9, which would otherwise have been a second answer. Sufficient.
3. Both alone work: **D**.

### Example 5: Choice E (even together, not enough)

**Question:** What is the average salary of a company's 10 staff?
**Statement I:** The highest salary is Rs 90,000.
**Statement II:** The lowest salary is Rs 30,000.

1. **I alone** and **II alone.** One end of the range tells you nothing about the middle. Not sufficient either way.
2. **Together.** Test with two different pay lists that both obey I and II:
   - List 1: nine people on Rs 30,000 and one on Rs 90,000. Average = (9 x 30,000 + 90,000) / 10 = Rs 36,000.
   - List 2: eight people on Rs 90,000 and two on Rs 30,000. Average = (8 x 90,000 + 2 x 30,000) / 10 = Rs 78,000.
3. Both lists have the highest at 90,000 and the lowest at 30,000, yet the averages differ. *Why:* two different outcomes under the same facts is exactly the proof that the facts are not enough.
4. Answer: **E**.

## 2 real-life scenarios (all numbers are invented, "illustrative")

### Scenario 1: The attrition question (HR)

A manager asks: "Was the Support team's attrition last year higher than the company's 15%?" (Illustrative: attrition = people who left divided by the opening headcount.)

Your data says: **9 people left the Support team.** Is that enough?

- If the team started the year with 50 people: 9 / 50 = 18%, higher than 15%.
- If it started with 80 people: 9 / 80 = 11.25%, lower than 15%.

Same 9 leavers, two different yes/no answers, so the data is **not sufficient**. The missing fact is the opening headcount. After you ask and learn it was 48: 9 / 48 = 18.75%, above 15%, so now the answer is a firm yes. (A useful by-product: for 9 leavers, the team is above 15% only if its opening headcount was below 60, because 9 / 60 = 15% exactly.)

**Lesson:** you could tell it was not enough without ever computing the "real" rate.

### Scenario 2: Will 200 packets last the week? (commerce and daily life)

A small shop owner has 200 packets of a snack and asks: "Will this last 7 days?" (Illustrative.)

- **Fact 1:** "Last Monday we sold 60 packets." One day says little about the week: 60 a day would need 420 packets, but one busy Monday may not repeat. Not sufficient.
- **Fact 2:** "Over the last four weeks, we never sold more than 28 packets on any day." Worst case: 28 x 7 = 196 packets in a week, which is under 200. So the answer is **always yes, it will last**. Sufficient.

Fact 2 does not tell you exactly how many packets will be sold; it only needs to pin down the yes/no. This is the yes/no idea again: you do not need the exact number, only enough to decide. (A caution: Fact 2 assumes the next week behaves like the past four. Whether that is a safe assumption is a separate question, which Part 4 returns to.)

## Mistake log

1. **Solving when you only need to decide.** Spending two minutes finding the value, when the task was to say whether one value exists. Fix: look for a second possible answer instead.
2. **Carrying Statement I into the test of Statement II.** Each statement is tested alone first. Fix: cover the other statement with your hand.
3. **Treating "always no" as "not sufficient".** A firm no is a sufficient answer. Only "sometimes yes, sometimes no" is insufficient.
4. **Assuming facts that are not given.** For example, assuming every item costs the same, or that numbers are whole, when the question never said so. Fix: only use background facts that are written down.
5. **Counting the same fact twice.** Two statements that say the same thing in different words add nothing together. Fix: ask "does the second statement tell me anything new?"

## Recap

1. Data sufficiency asks whether the facts give exactly one answer, not what the answer is.
2. For a yes/no question, "always yes" and "always no" are both sufficient; "sometimes" is not.
3. Test Statement I alone, then Statement II alone, and only then both together.
4. To show "not sufficient", find two situations that obey the facts but give different answers.
5. In real work, name the one missing fact instead of guessing.
