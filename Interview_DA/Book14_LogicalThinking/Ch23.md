# Chapter 23: Logical Fallacies

**What you will learn:** how to spot the 15 most common faulty-reasoning patterns (ad hominem, straw man, false cause, bandwagon and more) in ads, speeches, meetings and social media, and how to answer them calmly.

## Concept in plain language

**An argument** is a set of reasons (premises) offered to support a conclusion. You met this in Chapter 1 and Chapter 6.

**A fallacy** is a pattern of bad reasoning: the reasons look like they support the conclusion, but they do not. Fallacies are tempting because they often feel persuasive, sound confident or touch our emotions.

Two important warnings before the list:

- **A fallacy does not prove the conclusion is false.** It only means this particular argument fails to support it. The conclusion might still be true for other reasons.
- **Naming a fallacy is not a way to win a fight.** Use it to check your own thinking first, and to ask politely, "What is the evidence?"

### The 15 fallacies of this chapter

| # | Fallacy | One-line meaning | Invented mini example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ad hominem | Attack the person, not the argument | "Her report is wrong because she is new here." |
| 2 | Straw man | Twist someone's view into a weaker version, then attack that | "You want longer breaks? So you want nobody to work." |
| 3 | False cause | Assume A caused B just because B came after A | "I wore my lucky shirt, then we won the bid." |
| 4 | Bandwagon | It must be right because many people do or believe it | "Everyone is switching, so it must be good." |
| 5 | Appeal to false authority | Use someone famous or senior who is not an expert on this topic | "A film star says this loan is the best." |
| 6 | False dilemma | Offer only two options when more exist | "Either we cut salaries or we shut down." |
| 7 | Slippery slope | Claim one small step will inevitably lead to a disaster, with no evidence for the chain | "One late day and soon nobody will work." |
| 8 | Hasty generalisation | Draw a broad rule from too few or unrepresentative cases | "Two rude riders, so all riders are rude." |
| 9 | Circular reasoning | The conclusion is already hidden inside the premise | "It is fair because it is just." |
| 10 | Appeal to emotion | Use pity, fear or guilt instead of evidence | "Think of the sad puppy, now buy our product." |
| 11 | Red herring | Change the subject to something irrelevant | "Why worry about delays? We won an award." |
| 12 | Appeal to tradition | It is right because it has always been done this way | "We have always done it, so we must continue." |
| 13 | Appeal to ignorance | No one has disproved it, so it is true (or no one has proved it, so it is false) | "Nobody proved it is unsafe, so it is safe." |
| 14 | Loaded question | A question with an unfair assumption built in | "Have you stopped cheating on timesheets?" |
| 15 | Tu quoque ("you too") | Dismiss advice by pointing out the speaker does not follow it | "You tell me to save, but you spend too." |

### How to check any passage: the 3-step routine

1. **Find the conclusion.** What is the speaker trying to make me believe or do?
2. **List the reasons given.** Cover the emotional words and the names; what facts remain?
3. **Ask: do these reasons actually support the conclusion?** If the reason is about the person, the crowd, the past or a different topic, a fallacy is likely.

### Some arguments are fine

An expert who is relevant, checkable and supported by evidence is not a "false authority". A choice that really has only two options is not a "false dilemma". A chain of steps that each has real evidence is not a "slippery slope". Always check before you shout "fallacy".

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts read claims all day: "everyone prefers this", "it worked last time", "the expert said so". Spotting weak reasoning helps you ask for evidence before you build a chart or a model on top of a claim.

## Solved examples

### Example 1: The new analyst

*Passage (invented):* "Do not bother reading Kavita's attendance analysis. She only joined last week, so she cannot know anything."

- **Step 1, conclusion:** Do not read the analysis.
- **Step 2, reasons:** She joined last week.
- **Step 3, support?** Her joining date says nothing about whether the numbers in the analysis are right. The attack is on the person.
- **Answer: Ad hominem.** A fair response: "Let us check her numbers, whoever produced them."
- **Why this step order:** finding the conclusion first stops you being distracted by the insult.

### Example 2: The shorter workday trial

*Passage:* Priya: "I suggest a four week trial where the office closes at 5 pm instead of 6 pm." Dev: "So Priya wants us to work half days and let customers wait. That would ruin the company."

- **Step 1:** Dev's conclusion: the suggestion would ruin the company.
- **Step 2:** His reason is about "half days", which Priya never said. She said one hour earlier, as a trial.
- **Step 3:** He replaced her actual idea with an exaggerated one and attacked that.
- **Answer: Straw man.**
- **Why:** to detect a straw man, always compare what was said with what was attacked.

### Example 3: The coupon and the sales

*Passage (numbers illustrative):* "We sent a discount coupon on 1 October. Sales for October were 4,800 units against 4,500 in September. The coupon raised sales by 300 units."

- **Step 1:** Conclusion: the coupon caused the 300 extra units.
- **Step 2:** Reason: October came after the coupon and sales went up.
- **Step 3:** Sales may rise for other reasons (festival season, a new product, a longer month). Check: 4,800 minus 4,500 is 300, so the arithmetic is right, but the cause is unproven. The argument assumes "after" means "because of".
- **Answer: False cause** (also called "post hoc", Latin for "after this").
- **Why:** timing is only one clue for cause; Chapter 24 shows what else is needed.

### Example 4: Two options, or only two?

*Passage:* "Either we hire three more recruiters now, or our hiring targets will fail." Later the same manager says: "Our one printer is broken: either we repair it today or we cannot print the offer letters before noon." (Assume there really is no other printer, no email option and no one else's printer.)

- **First sentence:** other options exist (train current staff, use a recruiting agency, extend the deadline). Only two are offered. **False dilemma.**
- **Second sentence:** the situation is fully described: no other way to print. The two options really are the only ones. **Not a fallacy.**
- **Why:** the test is not "does it use either/or?" but "are there other options that were left out?"

### Example 5: A sound argument

*Passage:* "The payroll auditor, who has checked our files for twenty years, says March salaries match the bank statement. I also compared five random employees myself and they matched. So I believe March salaries are correct."

- **Step 1:** Conclusion: March salaries are correct.
- **Step 2:** Reasons: an expert in exactly this topic, plus the speaker's own independent check.
- **Step 3:** Both reasons are relevant and checkable. The expert is an authority on payroll, so this is not "false authority". The sample of five is small, so the speaker says "I believe", not "this is certain".
- **Answer: No fallacy.**
- **Why:** if you only hunt for fallacies, you will invent them. Good reasoning exists and you should be able to recognise it.

## Two real-life scenarios (all numbers illustrative)

### Scenario 1: The hiring meeting (HR)

Four people discuss whether to drop the written test for candidates:

- A: "We have used this test for ten years. Ten years! It must stay." (Appeal to tradition)
- B: "Tanvir wants to drop the test? He also wants lower standards, so he does not care about quality." (Straw man)
- C: "The last two hires who failed the test left within six months, so test failers always leave." (Hasty generalisation)
- D: "Please bring evidence: what did the test score predict in past hires?" (Not a fallacy: it asks for evidence)

*Lesson:* three of four comments sound reasonable at speed. Only D moves the decision forward.

### Scenario 2: A social media ad (commerce)

An invented ad for "Zing Tea" says: "10,000 people bought Zing Tea this week (bandwagon). A celebrity chef drinks it (false authority if the claim is about health). Do you still want to risk your family's health with ordinary tea? (appeal to emotion, and a false dilemma)."

*Lesson:* one short ad can contain several fallacies. A data person asks: "Bought 10,000 out of how many visitors? Compared with what? Health claims according to which evidence?"

## Mistake log

1. **Calling any disagreement a fallacy.** A strong, evidenced argument you dislike is not a fallacy.
2. **Thinking a fallacy means the conclusion is false.** It only means the reasons fail; the conclusion needs other support.
3. **Mixing straw man with ad hominem.** Straw man misstates the idea; ad hominem attacks the person.
4. **Rejecting all experts or all crowds.** Relevant experts and well-documented majority experience are evidence, only irrelevant or unchecked ones are fallacies.
5. **Forcing a label.** If you cannot find a gap between reasons and conclusion, the honest answer is "None".

## Recap

1. A fallacy is a pattern of reasoning where the reasons do not support the conclusion.
2. A fallacy does not prove the conclusion false; it shows this argument is weak.
3. Check the conclusion, then the reasons, then whether they connect.
4. Learn the 15 patterns, but also learn when an argument is sound.
5. Respond with a question for evidence, not with an insult.
