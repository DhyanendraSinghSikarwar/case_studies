# Chapter 23 answer key: Logical Fallacies

## Self-check answers

1. A logical fallacy is a pattern of bad reasoning: the reasons offered look as if they support the conclusion but do not.
2. Ad hominem. The reason given (she is late for meetings) is about the person, not about the budget idea.
3. False. A fallacy only shows that this argument fails to support the conclusion. The conclusion may still be true for other reasons.

## Practice answers

Method for every question: find the conclusion, list the reasons, check whether the reasons connect to the conclusion.

### Easy

**Q1 Ad hominem (1).** Conclusion: do not listen to the proposal. Reason: Mira talks fast and dresses oddly. Neither says anything about the leave tracker idea itself.

**Q2 Bandwagon (4).** Conclusion: you should switch. Reason: neighbours switched. The number of neighbours says nothing about whether the bulbs suit you.

**Q3 False cause (3).** Conclusion: green juice cures colds. Reason: it was followed by recovery. Colds usually pass in a few days anyway, so "after" is not "because".

**Q4 False dilemma (6).** Only two options are offered (raise for all, or half quit). Others exist: targeted raises, other benefits, discussions.

**Q5 Appeal to tradition (12).** Reason is only "we have always done it". There is no argument that Friday printing is useful.

**Q6 Appeal to ignorance (13).** "Not proved unsafe" is used as proof of safe. Absence of proof is not proof of absence; safety needs positive evidence.

**Q7 Straw man (2).** A asked for a quiet-hour trial. B replaced it with "ban all talking" and attacked that.

**Q8 Hasty generalisation (8).** Two cases are used to describe all riders. The sample is tiny and may be unrepresentative.

**Q9 Circular reasoning (9).** The reason ("treats everyone fairly") just restates the conclusion ("is fair").

**Q10 Appeal to emotion (10).** Pity for the puppy replaces any fact about the insurance (price, cover, claims).

### Medium

**Q11 Loaded question (14).** "Have you stopped" assumes the person did pad claims. Any yes or no answer admits it.

**Q12 Red herring (11).** The safety award has nothing to do with the payroll delays; it changes the subject.

**Q13 Tu quoque (15).** The rule is dismissed because the speaker also broke it. The rule is still valid whoever says it. (Not ad hominem, because the specific attack is hypocrisy.)

**Q14 Slippery slope (7).** One person on Fridays leads, with no evidence, through "everyone" and "nobody" to "collapse". Each step is just asserted.

**Q15 Appeal to false authority (5).** A film star is not an expert on home loans. (Not bandwagon: it is one person's status, not the crowd.)

**Q16 Ad hominem (1).** The reason is the leader's pay, not the offer. (This is the circumstantial form: attacking motive.) The offer should be judged on its numbers.

**Q17 Straw man (2).** "Sandals in summer" became "swimwear". The extreme version was never proposed.

**Q18 False cause (3).** Conclusion: green paint raises sales. Reason: sales rose that week. Other causes (season, promotion) are ignored.

**Q19 Hasty generalisation (8).** Five friends are a tiny, similar group, not the city.

**Q20 False dilemma (6).** "With us or against the company" leaves out cautious support, concerns, or asking for changes.

### Hard

**Q21 None.** The reasons are a record of 40 orders (39 on time) plus an independent check of 10 against separate warehouse records, and the conclusion is cautious ("usually", "expect"). The reasons connect to the conclusion, so it is not hasty generalisation.

**Q22 None.** The majority view here is backed by direct evidence: reports plus a documented own trial (6 of 8 cold mornings jammed). It is not bandwagon because the reason is evidence, not popularity.

**Q23 Circular reasoning (9).** The handbook's claim of being correct is used to prove it is correct. No outside evidence.

**Q24 Bandwagon (4) and appeal to false authority (5).** "Everyone is buying" is the crowd; a dentist is not an expert on heart health.

**Q25 Appeal to tradition (12) and appeal to emotion (10).** "Our grandparents trusted it" appeals to the past; "warm family dinners, smell of home" appeals to feelings. No fact about the product is given.

**Q26 Appeal to ignorance (13).** Lack of disproof is taken as a reason to act as if the ghost exists.

**Q27 (a) Straw man (2).** A said "review reporting lines"; B turned it into "reorganising was a mistake and leaders are incompetent". **(b)** Example: "I am not saying the reorganisation was wrong; I am asking us to check whether the new reporting lines relate to the attrition rise." (Any sentence that restates the real claim and asks for evidence.)

**Q28 Hasty generalisation (8).** The sample is only 40 and self-selected (brand followers), so it cannot represent all customers. Also a form of biased sampling.

**Q29 None.** The chain (skip backup, lose file, late report) is supported by recorded cases, and the conclusion is cautious ("may be late"). A slippery slope would claim a catastrophic chain with no evidence.

**Q30** Comment 1: ad hominem (1), the attack is on his seniority. Comment 2: bandwagon (4), competitors' actions are the only reason. Comment 3: false dilemma (6), adopting fully this month versus not caring leaves out pilots and other ways to show care.
