# Chapter 25 answer key: Cognitive Biases

## Self-check answers

1. **Confirmation bias.** Kavya reads only what agrees with her choice, so she never meets evidence that could change her mind.
2. **No.** The ₹5,000 is gone whether he continues or not (sunk cost). The only fair question is: from now on, is the course worth his time and any further cost? If not, stop.

## Practice answers

**Q1.** Confirmation bias. Ravi keeps the evidence that fits (centuries) and explains away the rest (zeros). To test fairly: list all innings of the captain for a full season, compute the average, and compare with the averages of other top players using the same method. Decide in advance what result would make him change his view.

**Q2.** Sunk cost fallacy. The ₹300 is spent whether she stays or leaves. The sensible question: "From now on, would I rather spend the remaining time on this film or on something else?"

**Q3.** Anchoring: the seller's first number (₹50,000) made ₹40,000 feel like a good deal. Market price ₹30,000, so the buyer paid 40,000 minus 30,000 = ₹10,000 more. As a percentage of the market price: 10,000 divided by 30,000 = 0.333, about 33.3% more. Repair: research the market price before hearing the seller's number.

**Q4.** The missing group is the old buildings that were badly built and have already collapsed or been demolished; we only see the ones that lasted. This is survivorship bias. Today's buildings include the weak ones (not yet failed), so the comparison is unfair.

**Q5.** Halo effect. One good trait (appearance) spreads to unrelated traits (punctuality, accuracy, client skills). Fix: judge each trait from its own evidence.

**Q6.** Hindsight bias ("knew it all along"). A habit that prevents it: write predictions and their reasons before the event, with dates, then compare afterwards. Without a written record, memory rewrites itself.

**Q7.**
- (a) **Recency**: the latest weeks outweigh the full year.
- (b) **Overconfidence**: certainty far above the track record (about half right).
- (c) **Base-rate neglect**: ignores how rare real guilt is among all employees.
- (d) **Availability**: two vivid friends' stories make scams feel common.
- (e) **Framing**: 1 in 20 late = 5% late = 95% on time; same fact, different wording. 95% on time sounds better.

**Q8.**
(a) Total = 10 x 90 + 60 + 70 = 900 + 130 = 1,030. Average = 1,030 divided by 12 = 85.83 units.
(b) (60 + 70) divided by 2 = 65 units.
(c) Difference = 85.83 minus 65 = 20.83. 20.83 divided by 85.83 = 0.2427, so about **24.3%** below.
(d) Recency bias. The last two months are the weakest and they overshadow ten steady months. A fair review shows the full year and also notes the recent dip as a separate point to investigate.

**Q9.**
(a) Referral: 2 divided by 25 = 0.08 = 8%. Walk-in: 6 divided by 60 = 0.10 = 10%.
(b) Walk-ins have the higher leaving rate (10% against 8%).
(c) Availability bias: one vivid bad hire made the pattern feel common. (Also note the groups are small, so even the 2 percentage point gap is not strong proof either way.) Repair: compare rates, not stories.

**Q10.**
(a) X: 95% on time means 100 minus 95 = 5% late. Y: 1 in 20 = 1 divided by 20 = 0.05 = 5% late. Z: 4 in 100 = 4% late.
(b) Z is best (4% late). X and Y are equal (5% late).
(c) Framing effect (also the "1 in 20" form hides the size of the percentage). Repair: convert every offer to the same wording and unit before comparing.

**Q11.**
(a) Finish: benefit 4 minus future cost 5 = minus ₹1 lakh. Stop: 0 (no further cost, no further benefit). The ₹8 lakh already spent is the same in both. So **stop**; finishing loses another ₹1 lakh.
(b) Yes. Finish: 7 minus 5 = plus ₹2 lakh, which beats 0, so finish. The decision changed because of the future numbers, not the past spending.
(c) "We have already put in ₹8 lakh, we cannot waste that, so let us finish." This is sunk cost thinking: it looks backwards.

**Q12.**
(a) Anchoring.
(b) Group A saw 40% first, and its average guess (30%) is the higher one: pulled upwards. Group B saw 10% first, and its guess (15%) is the lower: pulled downwards. (The true value is unknown here; we only see the groups were pulled in opposite directions by different starting numbers.)
(c) Ask people to write their own estimate privately before anyone mentions a number; or collect estimates from several independent sources; or use the actual data.

**Q13.**
(a) Campaign weeks: 5 divided by 8 = 0.625 = 62.5%. Non-campaign weeks: 5 divided by 8 = 62.5%.
(b) Sales rose equally often with and without the campaign, so the data do not show a campaign effect.
(c) Confirmation bias: looking only at campaign weeks gives a number that looks supportive and hides the comparison that would disagree.

**Q14.**
(a) 20 divided by 5,000 = 0.004 = 0.4%.
(b) The 4,980 early leavers who did not become successful (and the people who stayed in university and succeeded, for a fair comparison).
(c) Survivorship bias. Out of every 1,000 early leavers only about 4 succeeded, so leaving early is not a good predictor of success.

**Q15.**
(a) Misusers = 5% of 2,000 = 100. Honest = 2,000 minus 100 = 1,900.
(b) Misusers flagged = 80% of 100 = 80. Honest flagged = 5% of 1,900 = 95.
(c) Total flagged = 80 + 95 = 175. Real misusers among flagged = 80 divided by 175 = 0.457, so **45.7%**.
(d) Base-rate neglect. The 80% is how often the detector catches misusers, not the chance that a flagged person is a misuser. Because honest employees are so many (1,900), even a 5% false alarm rate flags more honest people (95) than the detector catches misusers (80). So a flag means roughly a 46% chance, not 80%.

**Q16.** Three biases: (1) **sunk cost fallacy** ("already spent two years and a huge sum, cannot drop it"); (2) **survivorship bias** (one friend who succeeded stands in for all similar attempts; the failed ones are invisible); (3) **confirmation bias** (reads only praising comments and dismisses the negative ones). A fourth reading, overconfidence ("ours will too"), is also acceptable, but the three above are the clearest.

**Q17.**
(a) (20 + 30 + 50) divided by 3 = 100 divided by 3 = 33.3 days.
(b) (16 + 18 + 20) divided by 3 = 54 divided by 3 = 18 days.
(c) Drop = 33.33 minus 18 = 15.33 days. As a percentage: 15.33 divided by 33.33 = 0.46, so **46%**.
(d) Anchoring. The boss's number pulled everyone's estimate towards 15. Rule: collect written estimates privately first, discuss afterwards, and let the most senior person speak last.

**Q18.**
(a) 80% of 300 = 240.
(b) 20% of 100 = 20.
(c) (240 + 20) divided by 400 = 260 divided by 400 = 0.65 = **65%**.
(d) Survivorship bias. The 80% describes only people who stayed; the people most likely to be unhappy are the ones who left and were not asked. Note the 20% figure is hypothetical; the point is that the headline could be far too rosy, and an exit survey of leavers is needed.

**Q19.**
(a) A 70% chance of success means a 30% chance of failure. In 10 similar pilots about 10 x 0.3 = 3 would fail even with good decisions.
(b) Outcome bias (a close relative of hindsight bias): judging the decision by its result instead of by the information available at the time.
(c) Yes. Good decisions can fail through chance, and bad decisions can succeed through luck. Judge the quality of a decision by asking: was the information reasonable, were the options compared well, were the risks weighed? The result is one more data point, not the whole verdict.

**Q20.** Model answer (any sensible five steps with the right bias match are fine):
1. **Fix criteria and weights before seeing candidates.** Targets halo effect and recency; each trait gets its own score with agreed weights.
2. **Ask every candidate the same questions in the same order.** Reduces confirmation bias (no chasing questions that confirm a first impression).
3. **Score privately and in writing before any discussion.** Prevents anchoring on the first or most senior opinion.
4. **Actively look for disconfirming evidence:** each panel member lists one reason not to hire the favourite. Targets confirmation bias.
5. **Review hires later against the scores** (for example after six months) to learn which scores predicted success. Targets overconfidence and hindsight bias; also, include candidates who left or were rejected where possible so that you do not learn only from the survivors.
