# Chapter 24 answer key: Correlation vs Causation

## Self-check answers

1. Correlation: two things tend to move together. Causation: one thing actually makes the other happen. Correlation is only a pattern in the data.
2. Hot weather. It raises both ice-cream sales and swimming, so more people are at risk of drowning. (Another valid answer: both rise in holiday months.)
3. Any one of: a random experiment with a comparison group; comparing with a similar group that did not get the change; checking dose and response; checking a believable mechanism; repeating the pattern.

## Practice answers

Labels: P plausible cause, R reverse, T third factor, C coincidence.

### Easy

**Q1 T.** The size of the fire drives both: bigger fires need more engines and cause more damage. Engines do not create damage.

**Q2 T.** Age: older adults have more grey hair and have had more years to save.

**Q3 T.** Town size drives both nests (more land and houses) and births (more people). Storks do not deliver babies.

**Q4 R.** Back pain leads people to visit the physiotherapist. Visits may help or not, but the comparison cannot show that visits cause pain.

**Q5 R.** Profit pays for the parties, so profit comes first. Parties may have small effects, but the stated mechanism points from profit to parties.

**Q6 C.** No known link, no mechanism, a few yearly points, and both can drift upward with city growth. Chance or shared trend.

**Q7 P.** Random assignment makes the groups alike, so the video is the likely cause of fewer early resignations in this group over these three months.

### Medium

**Q8 T.** Opening the newsletter was the customer's own choice, so keen, loyal customers probably open it and also spend more. (A fair test would send it to a random half of customers.)

**Q9 R.** The budget is set from the number of existing employees, so staff numbers decide the budget. The data does not show whether a bigger recruitment budget raises staff numbers.

**Q10 T.** Case severity drives both: severe cases go to large hospitals and severe cases have higher death rates. Compare similar cases only.

**Q11 T.** Population: more people, more shops and more bicycles to steal. Compare per 1,000 people.

**Q12 R.** Output (last year) decided who could choose remote work, so high output came first.

**Q13 T.** Seasonality (year-end shopping) explains the rise: the same rise occurred last year without the offer. The offer may add nothing.

**Q14 C.** Three wins are too few; there is no mechanism by which socks affect bids. Chance.

### Hard

**Q15 (a)** Users: (80 x 800 + 20 x 400) divided by 100 = (64,000 + 8,000) / 100 = 720. Non-users: (20 x 800 + 80 x 400) / 100 = (16,000 + 32,000) / 100 = 480. So users appear to spend 240 more. **(b)** Within each age group the averages are equal (800 vs 800, 400 vs 400): no difference. **(c) T (age).** Most users are under 30 and the under-30s spend more. Response: "The gap comes from the mix of ages; within the same age group the app shows no difference, so this table does not show the app causes more spending."

**Q16 (a)** Any two of: people chose the scheme themselves (self-selection, keen employees would rate higher anyway); high performers may be more likely to join (reverse); no comparison with their ratings before the scheme; no randomisation. **(b)** Example: "Among volunteers, randomly choose half to join the scheme this year and let the other half wait. Compare rating changes between the two groups."

**Q17 P.** Clues: before and after measures, steady dose and response (more hours, more loss), a controlled random lab result, and a known mechanism. Together these point strongly to cause.

**Q18 R.** The first reading assumed meetings lowered output; but low output came first and triggered the meetings. Time order and how the meetings were decided reverse the arrow.

**Q19 C (shared trend).** Both rise over time because of general growth or inflation, not because tea causes subscriptions. Before claiming a link, look for a mechanism, test whether the pattern holds in other places or after removing the time trend (for example by comparing changes year to year), and see whether other sources agree.

**Q20 (a) T.** The data team and their new projects likely raise ratings and also received upgrades first. **(b)** "Employees who received upgrades have higher ratings; this may be partly due to their role and projects, so we cannot yet say the upgrades raise performance." **(c)** Example: "Randomly pick half of eligible staff within the same role to receive upgrades first, and compare rating changes after a quarter."
