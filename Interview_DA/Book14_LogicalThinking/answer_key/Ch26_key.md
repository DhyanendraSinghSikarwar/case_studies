# Chapter 26 answer key: Evaluating Claims, Sources and Evidence

## Self-check answers

1. Any two of: urgency ("today"); "forward to 10 friends"; vague authority ("experts confirm") with no names; an offer that is too good ("free recharge for everyone"); no source or date.
2. The starting and ending numbers (a "50% fall" could be from 4 hairs to 2), and how it was measured: how many people, over how long, compared with what (people using no shampoo or another one). Also who ran the test.
3. The dated HR system report. It is the original record with an owner and a date; a memory is second-hand and may be wrong or out of date.

## Practice answers

**Q1.** Red flags: a too-good offer (free laptop for everyone); a request for bank details; a link to click; "forward to 15 groups"; no official source named. Action: do not click, do not enter details, do not forward. If curious, search for the scheme on the government's own official website (typed in yourself). Verdict: very likely a scam; at best "cannot tell" until an official source confirms it.

**Q2.** It fails WHO (which experts?) and WHAT evidence (no data, no test, no criteria for "best"). Questions: which experts, paid by whom, what was compared, on which criteria (battery, camera, price), and where can I see the test?

**Q3.** Any three good questions, for example: rated by how many customers; where and when (a website, last year, last week); on what scale and compared with which other restaurants; who collected the ratings (the restaurant itself?); what does "No. 1" mean (in this street, in this city)?

**Q4.** (b) HR export, most reliable: the original record, dated, owned by an accountable person. (c) Colleague's memory next: a named person who may be close to the facts, but memory is imperfect and unchecked. (a) Screenshot last: no author, no date, cannot be traced. (If the colleague works in HR and has just seen the report, they might move above memory, but still below the record itself.)

**Q5.** (a) Opinion. (b) Cause claim. (c) Prediction. (d) Fact claim (easy to check by counting floors).

**Q6.**
(a) 10 reviews with average 4.8: sum = 10 x 4.8 = 48.
(b) Five new reviews of 2 stars add 5 x 2 = 10. New sum = 48 + 10 = 58. Average = 58 divided by 15 = 3.8667, about **3.87**.
(c) The 4.8 came from 10 insiders; adding 5 honest ratings dropped it by almost a full point. Small samples move a lot, and ratings written by people connected to the seller are not independent evidence.

**Q7.**
(a) From 5 to 3 is a fall of 2; 2 divided by 5 = 0.4 = 40%. Correct.
(b) Before: 5 per 1,000 = 0.5%. After: 3 per 1,000 = 0.3%. Absolute change = 0.2 percentage points.
(c) 0.2% of 100,000 = 200 fewer failures (2 fewer per 1,000, times 100).
(d) The claim is honest (the number is right) but it sounds bigger than the absolute change. Still ask: how many payments were counted, over what period, whether the mix of customers or time of year was the same, and whether failures were counted the same way before and after.

**Q8.**
(a) 500 divided by 400 = 1.25, a 25% rise. Correct.
(b) A year earlier: 475 divided by 380 = 1.25, also a 25% rise.
(c) Seasonality: sales rise by the same 25% between these two quarters every year, so the logo cannot be credited. The comparison with the same period a year earlier removes the seasonal effect.

**Q9.**
(a) 40 happy divided by 50 replies = 0.8 = 80%.
(b) Response rate = 50 divided by 500 = 10%.
(c) If all 450 non-responders were unhappy: 40 divided by 500 = 8% happy. If all were happy: (40 + 450) divided by 500 = 490 divided by 500 = 98% happy.
(d) The true share could be anywhere from 8% to 98%, so the claim "80% of employees" is not supported. It describes only the 10% who chose to reply. Also ask how the people who replied differ from those who did not.

**Q10.**
(a) None that are independent: the claim traces back to one unnamed "report" that nobody shows. Many repeats equal one source.
(b) The original report: its title, author, date, the sample, what "leave" and "bad experience" meant, and the method. Ideally a primary source you can open yourself.
(c) Verdict: cannot tell yet; not usable in your own report until traced to its origin.

**Q11.**
(a) Sum = 20+22+24+25+26+28+30+35+40+90 = 340. Mean = 340 divided by 10 = **34**. Sorted list is already in order; the middle two are the 5th and 6th values, 26 and 28; median = (26 + 28) divided by 2 = **27**.
(b) Report A quotes the mean (34), pulled up by one very slow delivery of 90 minutes. Report B quotes the typical middle value (27). Both are true; they answer slightly different questions. Without the definitions, they look like a disagreement.
(c) Examples: they may start the clock differently (from order placed versus from pick-up); they may cover different periods or areas; they may include or exclude cancelled or failed deliveries; they may measure different delivery services.

**Q12.**
(a) A: 9 incidents divided by 90,000 hours = 0.0001 per hour = 1.0 per 10,000 hours. B: 6 divided by 40,000 = 0.00015 per hour = 1.5 per 10,000 hours.
(b) Department B has the higher rate (1.5 against 1.0).
(c) The claim compared raw counts, but A worked more than twice the hours of B, so it had more exposure to risk. Question that prevents the mistake: "out of how many hours (or how many people)?" The rate shows B is the less safe department.

**Q13.**
(a) Fraudulent = 1% of 1,000 = 10. Genuine = 990.
(b) The "everything is genuine" tool is right on 990 claims: 990 divided by 1,000 = 99% accuracy, yet it catches no fraud at all.
(c) Conflict of interest: the speaker sells the tool and gains if you believe it. Accuracy alone is a weak figure when fraud is rare. Ask for: how many of the real frauds it caught (out of the 10), how many genuine claims it wrongly flagged, and how it was tested (whose data, which period), ideally by an independent party.

**Q14.** Model answer:
- **Data to ask for:** monthly or quarterly attrition for the firm for at least two years before and after remote work began; headcount per month (so rates, not counts); remote versus office status per employee; reasons for leaving; role, location, tenure.
- **Comparisons:** attrition of remote versus non-remote employees in the same period and similar roles; the firm's trend before the change (was attrition already falling?); external comparison, if available, with similar firms over the same period.
- **Other explanations to test:** job market changes, a different time period (seasonal), changes in who works remotely (perhaps more senior staff chose remote), a hiring freeze, a pay rise at the same time, people who left before the policy.
- **Evidence ladder position:** rung 4 (comparison of groups in existing data). It could show an association; for a stronger test, a pilot where teams are assigned remote or office by chance (rung 5) would be needed.
- **Verdict wording:** "Cannot tell yet; here is the data that would settle it."

**Q15.** Steps:
1. Do not forward and do not click any link inside the message.
2. Identify the claim: a named notice (2026/44) from a named authority (Ministry of Labour) with a stated right (3 holidays).
3. Go to the authority's official website by typing the address yourself (or using a known bookmark) and look for the notice number and date.
4. Check that more than one reliable outlet or your company's HR or legal team confirms it, and that the date is current.
5. Check the wording: does the notice apply to all employees, or to a particular sector or state?
6. Sufficient confirmation: the primary document on the official site (or confirmation by HR from that document), with a date and a number that match.
7. If you cannot confirm it: label it "unverified", tell the sender you could not find it, and do not share. Ask HR. The pressure phrase "share before it is withdrawn" is itself a warning sign.
