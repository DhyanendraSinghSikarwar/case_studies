# Chapter 28: Pólya's 4 Steps: Understand, Plan, Execute, Review

## Concept in plain language

George Pólya was a mathematician who noticed that good problem solvers do not have magic. They follow the same small routine again and again. The routine has four steps.

| Step | The question you ask | What you write down |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Understand | What exactly is being asked? What do I know? | The goal in your own words, the given facts, the units |
| 2. Plan | Have I seen something like this? What method fits? | One sentence naming the method |
| 3. Execute | Now do the plan, one careful line at a time | The working, with each line justified |
| 4. Review | Does the answer fit the question? Can I check it another way? | A check, and a one-line final answer |

**Why four steps and not just "solve it"?** Most wrong answers do not come from weak thinking in step 3. They come from skipping step 1 (answering a slightly different question) or skipping step 4 (never noticing the answer is silly).

### Step 1: Understand

- Restate the problem in your own words. If you cannot, you have not understood it.
- Underline the **unknown** (the thing you must find) and list the **givens** (the facts you are told).
- Check the **units**: minutes or hours, rupees or thousands of rupees.
- Look for **hidden conditions**: "between", "at least", "different", "together".

### Step 2: Plan

A plan is a sentence like "turn two unknowns into one", "list all the cases", "work backward from the deadline", or "draw the order as a timeline". Chapter 29 gives you a toolbox of such strategies. For now, remember one rule: **name the method before you use it.**

### Step 3: Execute

- Write one idea per line.
- Next to every line, write the "why" in a few words.
- If a line feels shaky, stop and recheck it right now. Errors multiply as you go on.

### Step 4: Review

Three quick checks:

1. **Fit:** does the answer answer the question that was asked (units included)?
2. **Reasonable:** is the size sensible (a person cannot be 400 years old; a lunch cannot cost 3 rupees)?
3. **Second route:** can I check by substituting back, by a different method, or by a simple special case?

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts are handed vague questions ("why did sales drop?") and tight deadlines. Writing down the question, the data you have and the method you chose, then checking the result, is the habit that keeps a report from answering the wrong question. The four steps also make your thinking easy for a colleague to read and review.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: A full walk-through (all four steps in detail)

**Problem.** A bakery sells muffins at 35 rupees each and cookies at 20 rupees each. A customer buys 14 items in total and pays exactly 400 rupees. How many muffins and how many cookies?

**Step 1: Understand.**
- Unknowns: number of muffins, number of cookies.
- Givens: muffin 35, cookie 20, 14 items, total 400.
- Why: naming the unknowns lets us write them as letters. Let `m` = muffins, `c` = cookies.
- Hidden condition: counts of items must be whole numbers, 0 or more.

**Step 2: Plan.**
- Two unknowns are hard to handle; one unknown is easy. We know `m + c = 14`, so `c = 14 - m`. Replace `c` and we only have `m` left.
- Why: reducing the number of unknowns is the oldest trick in problem solving.

**Step 3: Execute.**
1. Money rule: 35m + 20c = 400. Why: each muffin adds 35, each cookie adds 20, the sum is what was paid.
2. Replace c by 14 - m: 35m + 20(14 - m) = 400. Why: the item count says c is 14 minus m.
3. Open the bracket: 35m + 280 - 20m = 400. Why: 20 times 14 is 280.
4. Tidy: 15m + 280 = 400. Why: 35m - 20m is 15m.
5. Take 280 from both sides: 15m = 120. Why: to isolate the m term.
6. Divide by 15: m = 8. Why: 120 divided by 15 is 8.
7. Then c = 14 - 8 = 6.

**Step 4: Review.**
- Fit: the question asked for both numbers: 8 muffins and 6 cookies.
- Substitute back: 8 + 6 = 14 items, and 8 x 35 = 280, 6 x 20 = 120, 280 + 120 = 400. Correct.
- Second route (guess and check): try 7 muffins: 245 + 7 x 20 = 385, too low. Try 8: 280 + 120 = 400. Same answer.

**Answer: 8 muffins and 6 cookies.**

### Example 2: The step that catches a trap

**Problem.** A workshop starts at 10:20. It has 3 sessions of 50 minutes each, with a 10-minute break between one session and the next. When does it end?

**1. Understand.** Unknown: the end time. Givens: start 10:20, 3 sessions of 50 minutes, breaks of 10 minutes *between* sessions. Hidden condition: "between" means the breaks are not before the first or after the last session. With 3 sessions there are only 2 gaps.

**2. Plan.** Add up all the minutes, then add them to the start time. Why: the clock moves forward by the total elapsed time.

**3. Execute.**
1. Session time: 3 x 50 = 150 minutes.
2. Break time: 2 x 10 = 20 minutes. Why 2: three sessions have two gaps.
3. Total: 150 + 20 = 170 minutes, which is 2 hours 50 minutes.
4. 10:20 plus 2 hours is 12:20; plus 50 minutes is 13:10.

**4. Review.** If we had wrongly used 3 breaks, we would get 13:20; the review question "how many gaps between 3 things?" catches it. Check by listing the day: 10:20 to 11:10, break to 11:20, 11:20 to 12:10, break to 12:20, 12:20 to 13:10. Matches.

**Answer: 13:10.**

### Example 3: A small logic grid

**Problem.** Asha, Bilal and Chen each handle exactly one different task: payroll, hiring, or training. Asha does not do payroll. Asha does not do hiring. Bilal does not do training. Chen does not do hiring. Who does what?

**1. Understand.** Unknown: the task of each person. Hidden condition: "different" means no two people share a task.

**2. Plan.** Draw a small table of people against tasks and cross out what the clues rule out. Why: a table keeps every possibility in view.

**3. Execute.**

| | payroll | hiring | training |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asha | no | no | ? |
| Bilal | ? | ? | no |
| Chen | ? | no | ? |

1. Asha is not payroll and not hiring, so Asha does training. Why: only one task is left for her.
2. Training is now taken, so Chen cannot do training. Chen is not hiring either, so Chen does payroll.
3. Bilal gets the last task: hiring.

**4. Review.** Check each clue: Asha is not payroll (she is training), Asha not hiring (right), Bilal not training (he has hiring), Chen not hiring (he has payroll). All tasks different. Good.

**Answer: Asha training, Bilal hiring, Chen payroll.**

### Example 4: Planning work that depends on other work

**Problem.** Onboarding a new joiner needs four jobs. Job A (account set-up) takes 2 hours. Job B (laptop set-up) takes 3 hours and can only start after A is finished. Job C (policy reading) takes 4 hours and can be done any time. Job D (final sign-off) takes 1 hour and can only start after B and C are both finished. Two people can work in parallel. What is the shortest total time?

**1. Understand.** Unknown: the shortest finishing time. Givens: durations and "after" rules; two workers.

**2. Plan.** Draw each person's timeline and follow the "after" rules. Why: waiting rules are easiest to see on a time line.

**3. Execute.**
1. Person 1 does A (hour 0 to 2) then B (hour 2 to 5). Why: B needs A first.
2. Person 2 does C (hour 0 to 4) at the same time. Why: C has no waiting rule.
3. D needs B (ends at 5) and C (ends at 4), so D starts at 5.
4. D takes 1 hour: it ends at hour 6.

**4. Review.** Can it be faster? The chain A, then B, then D is 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 hours and must happen one after the other, so no plan can finish before hour 6. The answer 6 is the best possible.

**Answer: 6 hours.**

### Example 5: Counting with a rule

**Problem.** A team has five people: Mina, Noel, Owen, Pia and Quin. A manager picks one as team chair and a different one as deputy. Mina and Noel refuse to serve together (one as chair and the other as deputy). How many different appointments are possible?

**1. Understand.** Unknown: the number of (chair, deputy) pairs. Hidden conditions: chair and deputy are different people; order matters (Mina chair, Noel deputy is different from Noel chair, Mina deputy).

**2. Plan.** Count all pairs ignoring the refusal, then subtract the forbidden ones. Why: it is easier to remove a small bad group than to list the good ones.

**3. Execute.**
1. Chair: 5 choices. Deputy: any of the other 4. All pairs: 5 x 4 = 20.
2. Forbidden pairs: (Mina, Noel) and (Noel, Mina), which is 2.
3. Allowed: 20 - 2 = 18.

**4. Review.** Second route: count by chair. Mina as chair has 3 possible deputies (not herself, not Noel). Noel as chair also 3. Each of Owen, Pia and Quin has 4. Total: 3 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 18. Same.

**Answer: 18.**

## 2 real-life scenarios

All numbers are invented and labelled "illustrative".

### Scenario 1 (HR): a hiring budget

*Illustrative.* A department has a monthly salary budget of 104 (in thousands of rupees). It wants to hire exactly 10 people, spending the whole budget: juniors cost 8 each and seniors cost 14 each. How many of each?

**1. Understand.** Unknowns: juniors `j`, seniors `s`. Givens: j + s = 10, 8j + 14s = 104 (all in thousands). Units: everything is in thousands, so no mixing.

**2. Plan.** Same plan as Example 1: replace s by 10 - j.

**3. Execute.** 8j + 14(10 - j) = 104, so 8j + 140 - 14j = 104, so -6j = -36, so j = 6, and s = 4.

**4. Review.** 6 x 8 = 48 and 4 x 14 = 56, total 104. 6 + 4 = 10. Correct. A plausible answer: both numbers are whole and non-negative.

### Scenario 2 (daily life): when to leave for a flight

*Illustrative.* A flight leaves at 17:40. Check-in closes 60 minutes before departure. The trip to the airport takes 45 minutes, and you need 15 minutes to get a taxi after leaving home. What is the latest time to start from your door?

**1. Understand.** Unknown: the latest leaving time. The key words: "closes before departure", so the real deadline is the check-in close, not the flight time.

**2. Plan.** Work backward from the deadline, subtracting each stage.

**3. Execute.** Deadline: 17:40 minus 60 minutes is 16:40. Minus 45 minutes of travel is 15:55. Minus 15 minutes for the taxi is 15:40.

**4. Review.** Go forward from 15:40: plus 15 is 15:55, plus 45 is 16:40, exactly check-in close. It works. (Most people would leave at the flight time minus travel; Step 1 prevents that.)

## Mistake log

1. **Starting to calculate before reading the question twice.** You then solve a neighbouring problem. Fix: write the unknown in one sentence first.
2. **Ignoring units.** Mixing hours and minutes, or rupees and thousands of rupees. Fix: write the unit next to every number in step 1.
3. **Counting gaps as items (the fence-post slip).** Three sessions have two gaps, not three. Fix: sketch the line in step 1.
4. **Skipping the review because "it feels right".** Feeling is not evidence. Fix: always do one substitution check.
5. **Treating "the answer is a number" as the end.** Answer in the form the question asked (a time, a pair, a name).

## Recap (5 lines)

1. Pólya's four steps are: understand, plan, execute, review.
2. Understanding means naming the unknown, the givens, the units and the hidden conditions.
3. A plan is a named method; a good habit is to name it before using it.
4. Execute one line at a time, and give a reason for every line.
5. Review means: does it fit, is it reasonable, and does a second route agree?
