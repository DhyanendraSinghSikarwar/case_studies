# Chapter 3: AND, OR, NOT and Truth Tables

**What you will learn:** how to combine simple true/false statements with AND, OR and NOT, and how to list every possible case in a truth table so that no case is forgotten.

## Concept in plain language

### Statements and true/false

A **statement** is a sentence that is either true or false, never both. "The invoice is paid" is a statement. In Chapter 2 you practised spotting these. In this chapter we give each simple statement a short name such as **p**, **q** or **r**, so that we can talk about many statements at once. Each one can be **T** (true) or **F** (false).

### The three connecting words

Real rules in offices are built by joining simple statements with three small words. Each word has an exact meaning in logic.

| Word | Reads as | Result is true when |
|---|---|---|
| **NOT p** | the opposite of p | p is false |
| **p AND q** | both together | p is true and q is true (both) |
| **p OR q** | at least one | p is true, or q is true, or both are true |

Some books write these with symbols (NOT as ¬, AND as ∧, OR as ∨). We will use the words, because they are what you type in Excel and SQL.

### The one trap: "OR" in logic is generous

In everyday talk, "tea or coffee?" usually means one of the two, not both. In logic, **OR is inclusive**: it is also true when both parts are true. If a rule says "free delivery if you are a member OR your bill is above 999", a member with a bill above 999 still gets free delivery. When we really mean "one but not both" we say so and call it **exclusive or** (you will build one in the practice).

### What is a truth table?

A **truth table** is a small grid that lists every possible combination of T and F for the simple statements, and shows the result of the rule for each combination. It is a checklist that makes you look at every case, including the awkward ones.

How many rows? Each statement has 2 possible values, so:

| Number of simple statements | Rows needed |
|---|---|
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 8 |
| 4 | 16 |

Each extra statement doubles the rows (2, 4, 8, 16, ...).

**Standard row order** (so that nobody misses a row): start with all T, then change the last column first, like counting backwards.

| p | q |
|---|---|
| T | T |
| T | F |
| F | T |
| F | F |

For 3 statements, the first column is T for four rows then F for four rows; the second is T, T, F, F, T, T, F, F; the third alternates T, F, T, F, ...

### Brackets and the helper-column trick

For a longer rule, do not try to do it in your head. Add one **helper column** for each small piece, then combine the pieces. Brackets show what to do first: in "p AND (q OR r)" work out "q OR r" first. Without brackets, "p AND q OR r" is ambiguous, so always write them.

### Two rules that are very handy (De Morgan's laws)

- NOT (p AND q) means the same as (NOT p) OR (NOT q).
- NOT (p OR q) means the same as (NOT p) AND (NOT q).

Two statements are **equivalent** if their truth-table result columns are identical row by row. You will check these two laws yourself in the practice.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Filters in Excel, SQL and Power BI are exactly AND, OR and NOT rules. A truth table is the safest way to check that a filter selects the rows you really mean, including the rows where two conditions are true together.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: NOT (one statement)

**Statement p:** "The canteen is open." Build the table for NOT p.

1. One statement means 2 rows: p true, p false. (Why: we must cover every case.)
2. NOT flips the value. When p is T, NOT p is F; when p is F, NOT p is T. (Why: NOT means "the opposite".)

| p | NOT p |
|---|---|
| T | F |
| F | T |

**Check:** if the canteen is open, "the canteen is not open" is false. Makes sense.

### Example 2: AND

**Rule:** "Gym entry is allowed if the person is a member (p) AND the fee is paid (q)."

1. Two statements, so 4 rows in standard order. (Why: 2 x 2 = 4 cases.)
2. AND is true only when both are true. (Why: the rule needs both conditions together.)

| p (member) | q (fee paid) | p AND q |
|---|---|---|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | F |
| F | F | F |

**Reading:** only 1 row out of 4 lets the person in. A member who has not paid is refused.

### Example 3: OR (the both-true row)

**Rule:** "Delivery is free if the order is above 500 (p) OR the buyer is a loyalty member (q)." (Illustrative rule.)

1. 4 rows again.
2. OR is false only when both are false. (Why: one true piece is enough.)

| p (order above 500) | q (loyalty member) | p OR q |
|---|---|---|
| T | T | T |
| T | F | T |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | F |

**Reading:** 3 of 4 rows give free delivery. The first row (both true) is true because OR is inclusive; this is the row beginners get wrong.

### Example 4: a helper column (p AND NOT q)

**Rule:** "Salary is processed if attendance is submitted (p) AND there is NOT a pending dispute (q = "a dispute is pending")." This is p AND (NOT q).

1. 4 rows.
2. First compute the helper column NOT q. (Why: one small step at a time prevents slips.)
3. Then combine p with the helper column using AND.

| p | q | NOT q (helper) | p AND NOT q |
|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | F | F |
| T | F | T | T |
| F | T | F | F |
| F | F | T | F |

**Reading:** salary is processed in just one case: attendance submitted and no dispute pending.

### Example 5: three statements

**Rule:** "A travel claim is paid if (there is a receipt (r) AND the manager approved (m)) OR the claim is a small petty-cash claim (c)." This is (r AND m) OR c.

1. Three statements need 8 rows. (Why: 2 x 2 x 2.)
2. Helper column: r AND m first, because it is inside the bracket.
3. Then OR with c.

| r | m | c | r AND m | (r AND m) OR c |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | T | T |
| T | T | F | T | T |
| T | F | T | F | T |
| T | F | F | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | T |
| F | T | F | F | F |
| F | F | T | F | T |
| F | F | F | F | F |

**Reading:** 5 of the 8 rows are paid. Notice a petty-cash claim is paid even with no receipt and no approval: the table makes that visible, which is exactly how you spot a loophole in a rule.

## 2 real-life scenarios

All numbers below are invented and labelled illustrative.

### Scenario 1: Free shipping (commerce)

An online shop's rule (illustrative): "Shipping is free if the order total is at least 999 OR the buyer is a member." Four orders:

| Order | Total | Member? | Total at least 999? | Free shipping? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1,250 | No | T | Yes |
| B | 640 | Yes | F | Yes |
| C | 520 | No | F | No |
| D | 999 | Yes | T | Yes |

Order D shows the "both true" row and the "at least" boundary (a total of exactly 999 counts). Order C is the only row where both pieces are false, so it is the only order that pays for shipping.

### Scenario 2: Early-leaver watch list (HR data)

An HR analyst wants a list (illustrative rule): "department is Sales OR Support, AND tenure is under 12 months." The brackets matter: (Sales OR Support) AND (tenure under 12).

| Employee | Department | Tenure (months) | Sales or Support? | Under 12? | On list? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Sales | 8 | T | T | Yes |
| E2 | Sales | 30 | T | F | No |
| E3 | Support | 5 | T | T | Yes |
| E4 | Support | 40 | T | F | No |
| E5 | Sales | 11 | T | T | Yes |
| E6 | Finance | 3 | F | T | No |

Three employees are on the list. A common mistake is to write "department is Sales AND Support": no employee is in two departments at once, so that filter returns nobody. When people say "and" in English they often mean OR in logic.

## Mistake log

1. **Treating OR as "one but not both".** In logic OR is also true when both parts are true. Use the exclusive form only when the rule says so.
2. **Distributing NOT wrongly.** NOT (p AND q) is not the same as (NOT p) AND (NOT q). It becomes (NOT p) OR (NOT q). Check with a table when unsure.
3. **Forgetting rows.** Missing a row hides a case. Count: 2 statements need 4 rows, 3 need 8, 4 need 16.
4. **Leaving out brackets.** "p AND q OR r" can be read two ways and the two readings are not equivalent. Always bracket.
5. **English "and" meaning OR.** "Students and seniors get a discount" means student OR senior. Translate carefully before you build the table.

## Recap

1. NOT flips true and false; AND needs both true; OR needs at least one true.
2. Logical OR is inclusive: it stays true when both parts are true.
3. A truth table lists every combination: 2 statements give 4 rows, 3 give 8, 4 give 16.
4. Use helper columns and brackets, one small step at a time.
5. Two rules are equivalent when their result columns match row by row (for example De Morgan's laws).
