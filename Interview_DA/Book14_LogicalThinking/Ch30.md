# Chapter 30: Proof Techniques (Direct, Contradiction, Counterexample)

**What you will learn:** how to decide whether a claim is always true or sometimes false, and how to show it with a short, airtight argument (a direct proof, a proof by contradiction, or a single counterexample).

## Concept in plain language

### What is a "claim" and what is a "proof"?

A **claim** (also called a **statement**) is a sentence that is either true or false, such as "the sum of two even numbers is even". (Chapter 2 covered statements.)

A **proof** is an argument that shows a claim is true in **every** possible case, using only facts you already accept and steps that follow one from another. A proof is not "I tried it five times and it worked".

Think of an HR audit. Checking five payslips and finding them correct does not prove *all* payslips are correct. A proof would be a rule or argument that covers every payslip.

### "For all" claims versus "there exists" claims

This is the most useful idea in the chapter.

| Type of claim | Example | What kills it | What proves it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **For all** (every case) | "Every employee in Sales has a target." | **One** counterexample (one Sales employee with no target) | An argument that covers every case |
| **There exists** (at least one case) | "Some employee in Sales exceeded the target." | An argument that none can | **One** example is enough |

So:

- To **disprove** a "for all" claim, you only need **one counterexample** (one case where it fails).
- To **prove** a "for all" claim, one example is never enough. You need a general argument.

### The three techniques

| Technique | Idea | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **Counterexample** | Find one case where the claim fails | You suspect a "for all" claim is false |
| **Direct proof** | Start from what is given, move step by step, arrive at the claim | The claim is "if A then B" and you can go from A to B |
| **Proof by contradiction** | Assume the claim is **false**, follow the consequences, hit something impossible; so the claim must be true | "Direct" is awkward, or the claim says something "cannot" happen |

### A few words you need (all used lightly)

- **Whole number**: 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on (no fractions, no negatives in this chapter).
- **Even number**: a whole number you can write as 2 times some whole number. Written in symbols: 2k, where k is a whole number. Examples: 0, 2, 4, 10.
- **Odd number**: one more than an even number, written 2k + 1. Examples: 1, 3, 9, 15.
- **Multiple of 3**: 3 times some whole number, written 3m.
- **Prime number**: a whole number bigger than 1 whose only whole-number divisors are 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, ...).
- **Letters such as k, m, n**: placeholders for "any whole number". A letter lets one argument cover infinitely many cases at once. That is the whole trick behind a direct proof.

### The three steps of a good proof

1. **Say exactly what the claim is.** (If it is "if A then B", write down A and B separately.)
2. **Decide which technique fits.** Try a few examples first: if one fails, you have a counterexample and you are done.
3. **Write the argument so each line follows from the line before**, and say *why* each time.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts are often told "this is always the case" ("every customer who buys X buys Y", "attrition never rises in Q2"). Knowing that one counterexample ends a "for all" claim, and that many agreeing examples never prove it, stops you from over-trusting a pattern. It also gives you a clean way to say "the data shows one case where this fails".

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: direct proof

**Claim:** The sum of two even numbers is always even.

**Step 1: write what "even" means.** Let the first even number be 2a and the second be 2b, where a and b are whole numbers. *Why:* every even number can be written as 2 times something, and using letters means we cover every even number, not just 4 and 6.

**Step 2: add them.** 2a + 2b. *Why:* that is exactly "the sum of the two numbers".

**Step 3: pull out the common 2.** 2a + 2b = 2(a + b). *Why:* both parts contain a 2, so we can take it outside brackets.

**Step 4: read the result.** a + b is a whole number (a whole number plus a whole number), so 2(a + b) is 2 times a whole number, which is the definition of even. *Why:* we have landed on the definition of "even", so the claim is proved for **every** pair of even numbers.

**Conclusion:** True, proved directly.

### Example 2: direct proof (a different shape)

**Claim:** The sum of any two consecutive whole numbers is odd. (Consecutive means one right after the other, like 7 and 8.)

**Step 1: name the numbers.** Call the smaller one n, so the next is n + 1. *Why:* one letter is enough because the second number is fixed once the first is chosen.

**Step 2: add.** n + (n + 1) = 2n + 1. *Why:* n plus n is 2n, and the 1 stays.

**Step 3: compare with "odd".** An odd number is 2k + 1. Here k = n, so 2n + 1 has exactly that shape. *Why:* matching the definition is how a direct proof finishes.

**Conclusion:** True. Quick sanity check, not part of the proof: 7 + 8 = 15 (odd), 20 + 21 = 41 (odd).

### Example 3: disprove with a counterexample

**Claim:** "For every whole number n, the number n² + n + 11 is prime." (n² means n times n.)

**Step 1: test small cases.** *Why:* if the claim is false, an early case may show it.

| n | n² + n + 11 | Prime? |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 11 | yes |
| 1 | 13 | yes |
| 2 | 17 | yes |
| 3 | 23 | yes |
| 4 | 31 | yes |
| 5 | 41 | yes |
| 6 | 53 | yes |
| 7 | 67 | yes |
| 8 | 83 | yes |
| 9 | 101 | yes |
| 10 | 121 | **no**, 121 = 11 times 11 |

**Step 2: stop at the first failure.** n = 10 gives 100 + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11 × 11, which is not prime. *Why:* one failing case is enough to kill a "for all" claim.

**Conclusion:** False. n = 10 is the counterexample. **Lesson:** ten cases in a row worked, and the claim was still false. Many agreeing examples do not make a proof.

### Example 4: proof by contradiction

**Claim:** Among any 13 employees, at least two were born in the same calendar month.

**Step 1: assume the opposite.** Suppose the claim is false: no two of the 13 share a birth month. *Why:* a proof by contradiction always begins by assuming the claim fails.

**Step 2: follow the consequence.** If no two share a month, each of the 13 people has a *different* month. *Why:* that is what "no two share" means.

**Step 3: count the months.** There are only 12 months in a year, so we can fit at most 12 different months. *Why:* we are checking whether 13 different months is possible.

**Step 4: spot the impossibility.** We need 13 different months but only 12 exist. This is impossible. *Why:* a contradiction means our starting assumption must have been wrong.

**Conclusion:** The assumption was false, so the claim is true: at least two of the 13 share a birth month.

### Example 5: proof by contradiction (on a number claim)

**Claim:** If n² is even, then n is even. (Here n is a whole number.)

**Step 1: assume the opposite of the conclusion.** Suppose n² is even **but** n is odd. *Why:* we keep the given (n² is even) and assume the "then" part fails (n is not even, so odd).

**Step 2: write n as odd.** n = 2k + 1. *Why:* that is the definition of odd.

**Step 3: square it.** n² = (2k + 1) × (2k + 1) = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k² + 2k) + 1. *Why:* we expand the brackets and then pull out a 2 so we can see the shape.

**Step 4: compare with "odd".** 2(2k² + 2k) + 1 is "2 times something, plus 1", so n² is odd. *Why:* it matches the definition of odd.

**Step 5: spot the clash.** We were told n² is even, yet we showed it is odd. A number cannot be both. *Why:* that is the contradiction.

**Conclusion:** The assumption "n is odd" must be false, so n is even. Claim proved.

## 2 real-life scenarios

*All numbers below are illustrative (invented).*

### Scenario 1: the "every Sales employee" claim (data, HR)

A manager says: **"Every employee in the Sales team completed the induction training."** You look at the training record for the Sales team:

| Employee | Team | Induction completed |
|---|---|---|
| Anita | Sales | Yes |
| Bharat | Sales | Yes |
| Chetan | Sales | No |
| Divya | Sales | Yes |

The claim is a "for all" claim. Chetan is a Sales employee who has not completed induction, so **one counterexample** ends it. You do not need to look at anyone else. Your report says: "The claim is false: Chetan (Sales) has no induction record." Notice what you would **not** say: "75% of Sales completed it, so the claim is mostly true". That is a different, weaker statement.

If the table had been the *whole* team and every row said Yes, then checking all four rows **would** be a proof, because the list is finite and you covered every case.

### Scenario 2: the weekly-off roster (commerce, daily life)

A shop has 8 staff members. Each person is given exactly one weekly day off, from 7 possible days (Monday to Sunday). The owner asks: **"Must at least two staff share the same day off?"**

Use contradiction. Suppose nobody shares a day off. Then the 8 people have 8 *different* days off. But a week has only 7 days. That is impossible. So at least two staff must share a day off, whatever roster the owner chooses. This is useful: it tells the owner that clashes are unavoidable with 8 staff and 7 days, so the rule should be to manage clashes, not to ban them.

## Mistake log

1. **Treating examples as a proof.** "It worked for 2, 4 and 6, so it is always true" is a guess, not a proof (see Example 3: ten cases, still false).
2. **Looking for a counterexample to a "there exists" claim.** To prove "some such case exists", one example is enough; no need to prove anything general.
3. **Assuming the wrong opposite in a contradiction proof.** The opposite of "n is even" is "n is odd", not "n is negative". The opposite of "at least two share a month" is "no two share a month", not "everyone shares a month". Always write the exact negation first.
4. **Skipping the "why" in a direct proof.** Writing 2a + 2b = 2(a + b) without saying "so it has the form 2 times something, which is even" leaves the proof unfinished.
5. **Using a counterexample that does not meet the conditions.** If the claim is about whole numbers, -3 or 2.5 is not a valid counterexample.

## Recap

1. A proof covers every case; checking some examples never proves a "for all" claim.
2. One counterexample is enough to disprove a "for all" claim.
3. A direct proof starts from the definitions (even = 2k, odd = 2k + 1) and walks to the claim.
4. A proof by contradiction assumes the claim fails and shows that leads to something impossible.
5. Always try small cases first: they often reveal the counterexample quickly.
