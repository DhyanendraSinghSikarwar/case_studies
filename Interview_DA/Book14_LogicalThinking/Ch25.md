## Concept in plain language

### What is a bias?

Your brain makes thousands of quick decisions a day. To save effort it uses **shortcuts** (rules of thumb). Most of the time they work. Sometimes they push you in the same wrong direction again and again. A **cognitive bias** is exactly that: a **predictable, repeating tilt** in how we judge, caused by a mental shortcut.

Two things to remember:

- A bias is not stupidity. Clever people have all of these biases.
- A bias is not a lie. You honestly believe what you conclude. That is why biases are dangerous: they feel like common sense.

### The common biases (our working list)

| Bias | Plain meaning | Tiny example | A simple antidote |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | You look for, notice and remember facts that agree with what you already believe, and skip the rest | Only counting the days your favourite bus was on time | Actively search for one fact that would prove you wrong |
| **Anchoring** | The first number you hear pulls your later estimate towards it | A shirt "was ₹4,000, now ₹2,400" feels cheap even if it normally sells at ₹2,300 | Decide your own number before you see theirs |
| **Survivorship bias** | You study only the winners or survivors and forget the ones who dropped out of view | Judging gyms by members who stayed, not by those who quit | Ask: who is missing from this data? |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | You keep going because of money or effort already spent and gone, not because of what lies ahead | Finishing a bad film because the ticket was expensive | Only count future costs and future benefits |
| **Availability bias** | You think something is common because an example comes to mind easily (a vivid or recent story) | Fearing one rare event you heard about last week | Look up the actual count or rate |
| **Halo effect** | One good trait makes you rate everything else about a person or product higher | Rating a smart-looking candidate as also punctual | Score each trait separately |
| **Recency bias** | You give too much weight to the latest events | Rating a whole year on the last two weeks | Look at the full period, then the latest |
| **Framing effect** | The same facts feel different when worded differently | "90% passed" feels better than "10% failed" | Rewrite the claim in the opposite wording |
| **Hindsight and outcome bias** | After you know the result, you feel you "knew it all along", and you judge a decision only by its result | "That plan was obviously going to fail" | Judge the decision by what was known at that time |
| **Base-rate neglect** | You ignore how common something is overall and focus on a test result or a story | Treating a "fraud alert" as proof of fraud even when fraud is very rare | Ask: out of how many cases? Use counts |
| **Overconfidence** | You feel surer than your record justifies | Saying "I am certain" and being right 6 times out of 10 | Write your guess with a range, check later |

### Reading a case in three steps

1. **Find the decision or belief.** What is the person about to conclude?
2. **Find the shortcut.** What did they notice, ignore, or get pulled towards?
3. **Name the bias, then repair it.** Say what extra information or rule would remove the tilt.

> **Why this matters for data work.** Data does not interpret itself. Analysts choose which data to pull, which comparison to show, and which story to tell, so every bias above can sneak into a report. A named bias gives you a specific question to ask instead of a vague "something feels off".

## 5 solved examples

### Solved example 1: confirmation bias in hiring (illustrative numbers)

*Meena, a recruiter, believes "graduates of College X are our best hires". She looks through the hires from College X and points to 4 who did very well. Is she right?*

1. **Find the belief.** "College X hires are better."
2. **Spot the shortcut.** She only looked at the successes from College X. That is searching for evidence that agrees with her.
3. **Ask what would prove her wrong.** We need all hires, from both groups, and the same success test for each.
4. **Check the full picture.** Suppose College X gave 10 hires (4 did very well) and other colleges gave 30 hires (12 did very well).
   - College X: 4 out of 10 = 40%.
   - Others: 12 out of 30 = 40%.
5. **Conclude.** The rates are equal. Her belief was not supported. The "why" of the steps: a belief only becomes a finding when you also look at the cases that could have disagreed with it.

### Solved example 2: anchoring in a shop

*A jacket tag reads "Original price ₹4,000, now ₹2,400". The same jacket usually sells in other shops for ₹2,300. Is it a bargain?*

1. **Find the decision.** Buy or not.
2. **Spot the anchor.** ₹4,000 is the first number you see. It pulls your sense of "fair price" upwards.
3. **Compute the claimed discount.** 1 minus 2,400 divided by 4,000 = 1 minus 0.6 = 0.4, so "40% off".
4. **Compare to a number you trust.** The usual price is ₹2,300, so you would pay ₹2,400 minus ₹2,300 = ₹100 more than normal.
5. **Conclude.** Not a bargain. The "why": a discount is only real compared with a fair price, and the shop chose the comparison number, not you.

### Solved example 3: survivorship bias at a gym

*A gym advertises: "Members who stayed a year lost an average of 8 kg." Of 200 people who joined this year, 50 are still members. Why is the advert weak?*

1. **Find the claim.** The gym's programme leads to an 8 kg loss.
2. **Ask who is in the data.** Only the people who stayed.
3. **Count who is missing.** 200 joined, 50 stayed: 50 divided by 200 = 25%. So 150 people (75%) are not in the average.
4. **Think about why they left.** Many probably left because it did not work for them. The ones who stayed are likely the ones it worked for.
5. **Conclude.** The 8 kg describes a filtered group, not a typical joiner. To judge the programme you need the results of all 200, including those who left.

### Solved example 4: sunk cost in a software project (illustrative numbers)

*A company has already spent ₹12 lakh building an internal tool. To finish it needs another ₹4 lakh and is expected to bring benefits worth ₹3 lakh. A ready-made tool costs ₹2 lakh and would bring the same ₹3 lakh benefit. What should it do?*

1. **Separate past from future.** The ₹12 lakh is already spent. It is the same whichever option is chosen, so it must not enter the comparison.
2. **Net value of finishing.** Future benefit ₹3 lakh minus future cost ₹4 lakh = minus ₹1 lakh.
3. **Net value of buying.** ₹3 lakh minus ₹2 lakh = plus ₹1 lakh.
4. **Compare.** Buying is ₹2 lakh better than finishing.
5. **Conclude.** Buy the ready-made tool. The "why": "we have come too far to stop" is about the past; a good decision is about what each option gives from now on.

### Solved example 5: framing effect

*Offer A says: "Of 80 trainees, 72 got certified." Offer B says: "8 of 80 trainees failed to get certified." Which training provider is better?*

1. **Rewrite both in the same wording.** A: 72 out of 80 certified = 72 divided by 80 = 0.9 = 90%.
2. **B in the same wording.** 8 failed means 80 minus 8 = 72 certified, also 90%.
3. **Compare.** Identical results.
4. **Notice the feeling.** A sounds more positive, B sounds more worrying, yet the facts are the same.
5. **Conclude.** The providers are equal on this fact. The "why": always translate a claim into the opposite wording before reacting to its tone.

## 2 real-life scenarios (illustrative numbers)

### Scenario 1: the interview scorecard (halo effect)

An interviewer is impressed by a candidate's polished speaking. She gives 5 out of 5 for "overall" and says "clearly a strong all-rounder". The role needs problem solving and technical skill as well.

- She separates the scores: communication 5, technical skill 3, problem solving 3.
- She uses the weights the team agreed in advance: technical 40%, communication 20%, problem solving 40%.
- Weighted score = 0.4 x 3 + 0.2 x 5 + 0.4 x 3 = 1.2 + 1.0 + 1.2 = 3.4 out of 5.
- The "overall 5" came from one shining trait spreading to the rest (halo). The 3.4 is the fairer number.

Lesson: score traits one at a time, with weights fixed before you meet the candidate.

### Scenario 2: "resignations always follow appraisals" (availability bias)

A manager says, "People always resign right after appraisal week. I have seen it three times." The three cases are fresh in her memory.

- She asks HR for the data. In the last year there were 40 resignations, and 9 of them happened within 30 days after appraisal results.
- 9 divided by 40 = 0.225, which is 22.5%.
- That is more than a random one-month share might suggest (a month is about 1 out of 12, roughly 8%), so the idea has some support, but it is far from "always": 31 of the 40 resigned at other times.
- Memory made the vivid cases look like the rule.

Lesson: when a story comes to mind easily, ask "how many out of how many?"

## Mistake log

- **Calling every error "bias".** A bias is a repeating tilt from a mental shortcut. A simple arithmetic slip or a missing fact is not one.
- **Naming the bias and stopping.** The label is only the start. The useful part is the repair: what data or rule would remove the tilt.
- **Thinking "I am not biased, only others are".** Everyone is. The habit to build is checking your own conclusions, especially the ones you like.
- **Mixing up sunk cost and normal planning.** Looking at past spending to learn is fine. Letting it decide what to do next is the fallacy.
- **Assuming a biased conclusion must be false.** A biased route can still land on a true statement. A bias means the evidence is not yet trustworthy, not that the claim is wrong.

## Recap

1. A cognitive bias is a repeating tilt in judgment caused by a mental shortcut; everyone has them.
2. Confirmation, anchoring, survivorship, sunk cost, availability, halo, recency, framing, hindsight and base-rate neglect are the ones to know by name.
3. Read a case in three steps: find the belief, find the shortcut, name the bias and repair it.
4. Each bias has a question that defuses it: who is missing, what is the future cost, out of how many, what is the opposite wording.
5. Judge decisions by what was known at the time, and judge claims by all the evidence, not the pieces that please you.
