# Chapter 5: Quantifiers and Negation

**What you will learn:** how the small words "all", "some" and "none" control what a sentence claims, and how to write the exact opposite (the negation) of any such sentence.

## Concept in plain language

### What is a quantifier?

A **quantifier** is a word that tells you *how many* items a sentence is talking about. It does not describe the items; it counts them, roughly.

| Quantifier | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **All** (every, each, any) | Every single one, no exceptions | "All employees have an ID card." |
| **Some** (at least one) | One or more; could even be all | "Some employees work from home." |
| **None** (no) | Zero; not even one | "No employee works on Sunday." |

Two small but important rules:

- In logic, **"some" means "at least one"**. It does not mean "not all". "Some employees are present" is still true if every employee is present.
- **"All" is broken by a single exception.** One employee without an ID card makes "All employees have an ID card" false. That single exception has a name: a **counterexample**.

### What is a negation?

The **negation** of a statement is a new statement that is **false exactly when the original is true, and true exactly when the original is false**. They can never both be true and never both be false.

This is stricter than an "opposite". Many people write the *opposite* by flipping the word, and that is where mistakes happen.

Test: "All employees are present."

- Opposite (wrong): "No employee is present." If 8 of 10 are present, both sentences are false. A real negation can never leave both false.
- Negation (right): "At least one employee is not present." If 8 of 10 are present, this is true and the original is false. If all 10 are present, the original is true and this is false. They always disagree.

### The four negation rules

| Original | Negation | Plain reason |
|---|---|---|
| **All** A are B | **Some** A are **not** B | One exception is enough to break "all". |
| **No** A is B | **Some** A **is** B | One example is enough to break "none". |
| **Some** A are B | **No** A is B | "At least one" fails only when there are zero. |
| **Some** A are **not** B | **All** A are B | "At least one exception" fails only when there are no exceptions. |

Memory trick: **negation swaps the quantifier and negates the property.** All becomes Some-not, Some becomes None. The quantifier flips ("all" to "some", "some" to "none") and the property flips with it where needed.

### Negating "and", "or" and conditions inside the statement

When the property itself contains "and" or "or", negate it too. The rule (called De Morgan's rule, after a 19th century logician) is:

| Property | Negated property |
|---|---|
| X **and** Y | **not X or not Y** (at least one of them fails) |
| X **or** Y | **not X and not Y** (both fail) |
| If X then Y | X **and not** Y (X happens but Y does not) |

Example: "Every employee has a laptop **and** an ID card." One employee with no laptop, or no ID card, breaks it. So the negation is "Some employee has no laptop **or** has no ID card."

### Counting words

| Original | Negation |
|---|---|
| At least 3 | At most 2 (that is, fewer than 3) |
| At most 3 | At least 4 (more than 3) |
| Exactly 1 | None, or two or more |

### Nested quantifiers (a quantifier inside a quantifier)

Some sentences have two layers: "**Every** department has **at least one** employee who ..." To negate, flip the layers **from the outside in**: "all" becomes "some" and "some" becomes "none".

"Every department has at least one employee earning above 50,000."
- Outside layer "every department" becomes "some department".
- Inside layer "at least one employee earns above 50,000" becomes "no employee earns above 50,000".
- Negation: "**Some department has no employee earning above 50,000.**"

### A small oddity: empty groups

"All unicorns in the office are purple" is true in logic if there are no unicorns, because no one can show an exception. This is called being **vacuously true**. Remember it when a group might be empty; you will meet it once in the practice questions.

## Why this matters for data work

> Checking a claim in data means looking for the one record that breaks it. "All rows have a customer ID" is refuted by one blank; "some rows are duplicates" is confirmed by one duplicate. Knowing the correct negation tells you exactly what to search for, which is how data checks, filters and audits are written.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Negating "all"

**Statement:** "All the laptops in the store room are tested."

1. The claim is about *every* laptop. **Why:** "All" leaves no exceptions.
2. To make it false, one untested laptop is enough. **Why:** a single counterexample breaks "all".
3. Negation: **"Some laptop in the store room is not tested."** **Why:** rule 1 of the table.
4. Quick check with a store room of 3 laptops: if all three are tested, the original is true and the negation false; if two are tested, the original is false and the negation true. They always disagree.

### Example 2: Negating "none"

**Statement:** "No applicant submitted the form late."

1. The claim says the number of late applicants is zero. **Why:** "No" means zero.
2. To make it false, at least one applicant must have been late.
3. Negation: **"Some applicant submitted the form late."** **Why:** rule 2.
4. Common slip to avoid: "All applicants submitted late." That is far too strong; one late applicant is enough.

### Example 3: Negating "some"

**Statement:** "Some stalls sold out."

1. "Some" means at least one stall sold out.
2. This fails only if not a single stall sold out.
3. Negation: **"No stall sold out"** (every stall still has stock). **Why:** rule 3.
4. Common slip to avoid: "Some stalls did not sell out." Both can be true at once (stall A sold out, stall B did not), so it is not a negation.

### Example 4: Negating a property with "and"

**Statement:** "Every employee in the team has completed the training and signed the policy."

1. Outside layer: "every" becomes "some employee".
2. Inside property: "completed the training **and** signed the policy" becomes "has **not** completed the training **or** has **not** signed the policy". **Why:** to fail an "and", it is enough that one part fails.
3. Negation: **"Some employee in the team has not completed the training or has not signed the policy."**
4. Check with a team of 2: Employee A did both; Employee B did the training only. Original: false (B fails). Negation: true (B has not signed). Good.

### Example 5: Nested quantifiers

**Statement:** "Every department has at least one employee earning above 50,000."

1. Layer 1 (outer): "every department". Layer 2 (inner): "at least one employee earns above 50,000".
2. Flip the outer layer: "some department".
3. Flip the inner layer: "no employee earns above 50,000", that is, everyone there earns 50,000 or less.
4. Negation: **"Some department has no employee earning above 50,000."**
5. Check with 2 departments: Sales has salaries 40,000 and 60,000; Admin has 30,000 and 45,000. Original: false (Admin has nobody above 50,000). Negation: true. Good.

## 2 real-life scenarios

All numbers are **illustrative** (invented for teaching).

### Scenario 1: A payroll check (data and HR)

A payroll officer says: "Every payslip this month shows the correct tax code." You have 120 payslips (illustrative).

- To **prove** the claim you must check all 120. Checking 30 and finding no error proves nothing about the other 90.
- To **disprove** it you need only one wrong tax code. The negation is "Some payslip shows a wrong tax code."
- In a spreadsheet this is a filter for rows where the tax code is **not equal** to the expected code. If the filter returns even one row, the claim is false. If it returns zero rows, the claim holds.

### Scenario 2: A shop owner's claim (commerce)

A shop owner says: "Some of our products sell out within a day." Your sales table (illustrative) has 40 products.

- To **prove** this you need only one product that sold out in a day.
- To **disprove** it you must check all 40 and find that none sold out in a day: "No product sells out within a day."
- Notice the mirror image of Scenario 1: with "some", one example proves it and you need every record to disprove it; with "all", one example disproves it and you need every record to prove it.

## Mistake log

1. **Writing the opposite instead of the negation.** "All present" is not negated by "None present". Ask: can both be false at once? If yes, it is not a negation.
2. **Reading "some" as "not all".** In logic "some" means "at least one", so it is compatible with "all".
3. **Forgetting to negate the inside.** The negation of "All are X and Y" needs "not X **or** not Y"; changing "and" to "or" is the step people skip.
4. **Flipping only one layer of a nested statement.** Change "every" to "some" on the outside, and "some" to "none" inside.
5. **Believing a sample proves "all".** Checking some of the items can disprove "all" but can never prove it; only checking every item can.

## Recap

1. A quantifier (all, some, none) says how many items a sentence is about; "some" means at least one.
2. The negation is false exactly when the original is true; it is not the "opposite" word.
3. All becomes some-not; none becomes some; some becomes none; some-not becomes all.
4. Inside the statement, "and" becomes "or" when negated, and nested layers are flipped from the outside in.
5. One counterexample kills "all"; one example proves "some"; use this to decide what to search for in data.
