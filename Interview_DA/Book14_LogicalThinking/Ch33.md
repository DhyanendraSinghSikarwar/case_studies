## Concept in plain language

### What is a Fermi problem?

A **Fermi problem** is a question where you do not know the exact answer and cannot easily look it up, but you can still get a useful rough answer by reasoning. The name comes from a physicist who liked such questions. You do not need to remember the person; you only need the method.

An **estimate** is a rough answer that is close enough to make a decision. It is not a guess pulled from the air, and it is not an exact number. It is a number you can explain, step by step.

Typical Fermi questions:

- How many chai cups are sold in a big city in one day?
- How many sheets of paper does our office use in a year?
- How many delivery riders do we need for 6,000 orders a day?

### The big idea: break it down

You may know nothing about "chai cups sold in a city". But you may have a feeling about smaller pieces:

- How many people live in the city (roughly)?
- What share of them buy tea outside the home?
- How many cups does one such person buy in a day?

Each small piece is easier to guess than the whole thing. Then you **multiply** the pieces together.

### The six steps

| Step | What you do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Pin down the question**: what exactly is counted, where, and over what time? | "Cups of tea sold at stalls and cafes, in one city, in one day" |
| 2 | **Break it into factors** you can guess | people x share who buy x cups each |
| 3 | **Write each guess as a round, friendly number** and label it an assumption | 8,000,000 people; 30% buy; 2 cups each |
| 4 | **Calculate**, keeping units next to every number | 8,000,000 x 0.30 x 2 = 4,800,000 cups per day |
| 5 | **Sanity-check** with a second path or a common-sense limit | Count stalls instead of people |
| 6 | **Give a range** and say which guess matters most | "Roughly 3 to 5 million, driven mostly by the share who buy" |

### Useful tools

- **Round friendly numbers.** Use 8,000,000 rather than 7,843,219. Use 2 rather than 1.87. The point is speed and clarity.
- **Units.** Always write the unit (people, cups per person per day, rupees per hour). If the units on your answer are not the units the question asks for, something is wrong.
- **Order of magnitude.** This means roughly "how many zeros": thousands, lakhs, millions. Getting the order of magnitude right (not off by 10 times) is the main goal of a Fermi estimate.
- **Top-down and bottom-up.** Top-down starts from a big group and takes shares (city population, then share who drink tea). Bottom-up starts from the sellers or units and multiplies (number of stalls, then cups per stall). Doing both is the best sanity check.
- **Low and high.** Give a low guess and a high guess for the shakiest factor. This shows how sure you are.
- **Errors can offset.** Some of your guesses will be too high and some too low, so they often partly cancel. This is a reason the method is useful, not a promise that it is always accurate.

### What a good estimate is NOT

- It is not a made-up number with no steps.
- It is not false precision. "4,812,306 cups" is a bad sign: your inputs were not that exact.
- It is not a fact about the real world unless you checked your inputs. In this chapter every input number is invented or assumed and is labelled **illustrative**.

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Analysts are often asked "roughly how big is this?" before any data exists, or must judge whether a reported number is believable. Estimating lets you plan, spot numbers that are far off (often a sign of a data or unit error), and explain your thinking. Estimation does not replace real data; it tells you what real data should look like.

## 5 solved examples

All numbers are **illustrative** assumptions, not verified facts.

### Example 1: Chai cups in a large city per day (two paths)

**Question:** Roughly how many cups of tea are sold at stalls and cafes in a large city in one day?

**Step 1: pin down.** Count cups sold (not made at home), one day, one city. Assume the city has 8,000,000 people.

**Step 2: top-down path.**

- Adults (assume 70% of people): 8,000,000 x 0.70 = 5,600,000 adults. *Why:* children mostly do not buy tea outside.
- Share of adults who buy at least one cup out on a typical day (assume 30%): 5,600,000 x 0.30 = 1,680,000 buyers. *Why:* not everyone does.
- Cups per buyer per day (assume 2): 1,680,000 x 2 = **3,360,000 cups**.

**Step 3: bottom-up path (the check).**

- Stalls and cafes: assume 1 per 400 people. 8,000,000 / 400 = 20,000 sellers. *Why:* this uses a different kind of knowledge (how many shops you see).
- Cups per seller per day (assume 150): 20,000 x 150 = **3,000,000 cups**.

**Step 4: compare.** 3,360,000 and 3,000,000 differ by about 12% (3,360,000 / 3,000,000 = 1.12). Two independent paths agree, which raises our confidence.

**Answer:** about 3 million cups a day (range roughly 2.5 to 4 million). The share of adults who buy is the shakiest guess.

### Example 2: Office drinking water per year

**Question:** An office has 500 staff. How many 20-litre water bottles does it use in a year?

- Litres per person per day drunk at work (assume 1.5). *Why:* we need a daily rate per person first.
- Working days a year (assume 230). *Why:* nobody drinks office water on holidays.
- Total litres: 500 x 1.5 x 230 = 172,500 litres. *Why:* people x litres per person per day x days.
- Bottles: 172,500 / 20 = **8,625 bottles**. *Why:* convert litres to the unit asked (bottles).

**Answer:** about 8,600 bottles a year, roughly 9,000 if we round up.

### Example 3: Interview hours needed for hiring

**Question:** A company must hire 120 people. How many interviewer person-hours does that take?

- Assume 80% of offers are accepted. Offers needed: 120 / 0.80 = 150. *Why:* some people say no, so we must make more offers than hires.
- Assume 3 finalists are interviewed for each offer made: 150 x 3 = 450 finalists. *Why:* the funnel is wider at the top.
- Assume each finalist has 3 interviews of 1 hour, each with 2 interviewers: 450 x 3 x 1 x 2 = **2,700 person-hours**. *Why:* every hour of interview uses two people's time.

**Answer:** about 2,700 interviewer person-hours (1,350 clock hours of interviews). *Note:* this ignores screening calls and scheduling, so the real figure would be higher.

### Example 4: Reading time for a book (using a range)

**Question:** How long to read a 300-page book?

- Words per page (assume 300): 300 x 300 = 90,000 words.
- Reading speed (assume between 200 and 300 words per minute). *Why:* people differ, so we give a range rather than one number.
- Slow reader: 90,000 / 200 = 450 minutes = 7.5 hours.
- Fast reader: 90,000 / 300 = 300 minutes = 5 hours.

**Answer:** about 5 to 7.5 hours; "around 6 hours" is a fair single number.

### Example 5: Sanity-checking a claimed number

**Claim (invented):** "Our 80-person team sends 4,000,000 emails a year."

- Assume each person sends 40 emails a day and works 230 days: 80 x 40 x 230 = 736,000 emails. *Why:* build the expected figure from small, believable parts.
- The claim implies 4,000,000 / 80 / 230 = about 217 emails per person per day. *Why:* turning the total back into a per-person rate makes it easy to judge.
- 217 is about 5 times our assumed 40.

**Answer:** the claim is about 5 times higher than a reasonable estimate. It might include automatic emails or the whole department, or it might be a unit error. Ask where the number came from before using it.

## 2 real-life scenarios

All numbers are **illustrative**.

### Scenario 1 (commerce): how many delivery riders?

A grocery delivery start-up expects 6,000 orders a day. One rider can complete about 12 deliveries a day.

- Riders if demand were even: 6,000 / 12 = 500 riders.
- Orders bunch up at meal times and some riders are absent, so add a 30% buffer: 500 x 1.30 = **650 riders**.

*Lesson:* the base calculation is easy; the real thinking is deciding what extra allowance is reasonable. Say your buffer out loud so others can challenge it.

### Scenario 2 (data): will this table fit in a spreadsheet?

An HR team wants to load a table of 2,000,000 rows and 12 columns into a spreadsheet. (From memory, unverified: a current Excel sheet holds up to 1,048,576 rows. Check your own version.)

- Rows versus the limit: 2,000,000 / 1,048,576 = about 1.9. The table is almost twice the row limit, so it will not fit on one sheet.
- Rough size: assume 8 bytes per cell (a byte is a tiny unit of storage). 2,000,000 x 12 x 8 = 192,000,000 bytes, about 192 MB; doubling for overhead gives about 400 MB.

*Lesson:* a two-line estimate told the team to use a database or SQL instead (see Book 2), before anyone wasted an hour trying to open the file.

## Mistake log

1. **Skipping step 1.** Not fixing what is counted (cups sold or made, per day or per year) produces an answer to the wrong question.
2. **Dropping units.** Mixing hours with minutes or per-day with per-year numbers gives answers off by 10 times or more.
3. **False precision.** Reporting 3,361,742 when the inputs are rough. Round to one or two meaningful digits.
4. **Only one path.** A single path hides a bad assumption; a second path exposes it.
5. **Hiding the assumptions.** An estimate nobody can challenge is useless. State each assumption so others can change it.

## Recap

1. A Fermi estimate breaks a big unknown into small guesses and multiplies them.
2. First fix what is counted, where, and over what time.
3. Use round numbers, keep units, and avoid false precision.
4. Check with a second path or a common-sense limit.
5. Give a range and name the guess that matters most.
