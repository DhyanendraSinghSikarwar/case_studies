# Chapter 20: Clocks and Calendars

**What you will learn:** how to find the angle between clock hands, when hands meet or point apart, how fast and slow clocks drift, and how to jump across dates and weekdays (due dates, financial years) without counting every day.

## Concept in plain language

### Part 1: The clock face is a circle of 360 degrees
A **degree** (written °) is a small unit for measuring a turn. A full circle is 360°. A clock face is a circle with 12 numbers, so each number is 30° away from the next one (360 divided by 12).

Two hands move at different speeds:

| Hand | Full circle takes | Moves in 1 minute |
|---|---|---|
| Minute hand | 60 minutes | 6° (360 divided by 60) |
| Hour hand | 12 hours = 720 minutes | 0.5° (360 divided by 720) |

So the minute hand gains on the hour hand by 6 - 0.5 = **5.5° every minute**. This single number solves almost every clock problem.

### Part 2: The angle formula
At H hours and M minutes (use the hour on a 12-hour dial):
- The hour hand sits at 30 × H + 0.5 × M degrees, measured from the 12.
- The minute hand sits at 6 × M degrees from the 12.
- The gap is the difference of the two. Written short:

**Angle = | 30 × H - 5.5 × M |**

The bars | | mean "ignore the minus sign". If the answer is more than 180°, subtract it from 360°, because we always quote the smaller angle between the hands.

Useful special cases, all found from the same formula:
- Hands **overlap** (angle 0): 30 × H = 5.5 × M.
- Hands are **opposite** (angle 180°): the difference is 180.
- Hands form a **right angle** (angle 90°): the difference is 90 or 270.

### Part 3: Fast and slow clocks
A clock that **gains** 3 minutes every hour shows 63 minutes for every true 60. A clock that **loses** shows fewer. The trick is a ratio: compare "minutes shown" to "true minutes", then scale.

### Part 4: Calendars and the 7-day wrap
Weekdays repeat every 7 days. So to find a weekday far ahead, you only need the **remainder** after dividing the number of days by 7. (This is the "odd days" idea: the days left over after removing complete weeks.)

- 7 days later: same weekday. 8 days later: one weekday forward. 15 days later: 15 = 14 + 1, so one weekday forward.
- Going backwards, move back by the remainder.

### Part 5: Leap years
A normal year has 365 days (52 weeks and 1 extra day). A **leap year** has 366 days (52 weeks and 2 extra days), because February gets a 29th day. The rule used in the common (Gregorian) calendar:
1. A year divisible by 4 is a leap year (2028).
2. Except a century year (ending in 00), which is a leap year only if divisible by 400. So 2000 was a leap year; 1900 and 2100 are not.

Month lengths: 31 days in Jan, Mar, May, Jul, Aug, Oct, Dec; 30 days in Apr, Jun, Sep, Nov; February 28 or 29.

### Part 6: Financial years
Many companies count a **financial year** (also "fiscal year") for accounts and tax. A common convention in India is 1 April to 31 March, named for the two calendar years it touches (April 2026 to March 2027 is "FY 2026-27"). Other companies simply use January to December. Always check which one a question means. In this chapter we say it each time.

> **Why this matters for data work:** Dates are in almost every table an analyst opens: invoice dates, joining dates, due dates, month-end reports. Knowing how weekdays, leap years and financial years behave helps you spot a wrong date, build a correct "30 days later" rule, and group rows into the right quarter.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Angle at 4:20
**Question:** What is the angle between the hands at 4:20?
1. H = 4, M = 20. The hour hand is at 30 × 4 + 0.5 × 20 = 120 + 10 = 130°. Why: the hour hand has also crept forward a little because 20 minutes have passed.
2. The minute hand is at 6 × 20 = 120°. Why: each minute is 6°.
3. The gap is 130 - 120 = **10°**. Why: the formula |30 × 4 - 5.5 × 20| = |120 - 110| = 10 gives the same.
4. It is less than 180°, so we keep it.

**Answer: 10°.**

### Example 2: Angle at 7:45
**Question:** What is the angle between the hands at 7:45?
1. Hour hand: 30 × 7 + 0.5 × 45 = 210 + 22.5 = 232.5°.
2. Minute hand: 6 × 45 = 270°.
3. Gap: 270 - 232.5 = **37.5°**. Why: both hands are measured from the same starting point (12), so the difference is the gap.

**Answer: 37.5°.** Check with the formula: |210 - 247.5| = 37.5.

### Example 3: When do the hands overlap between 8 and 9?
**Question:** At what time after 8:00 do the two hands first lie on top of each other?
1. At 8:00 the hour hand is 240° ahead of the minute hand (8 × 30).
2. The minute hand gains 5.5° every minute. Why: it moves 6° while the hour hand moves 0.5°.
3. It must gain all 240°. Time needed = 240 divided by 5.5 = 43.636... minutes, which is 43 and 7/11 minutes. Why: dividing the gap to close by the closing speed.
4. 43 and 7/11 minutes is under 60, so it happens in the 8 o'clock hour.

**Answer: 8:43 and 7/11 minutes (about 8:43:38).**

### Example 4: Weekday of a later date
**Question:** 7 November 2026 is a Saturday. What day is 25 December 2026?
1. Days from 7 Nov to 25 Dec: the rest of November is 30 - 7 = 23 days, plus 25 days in December, total 48 days. Why: count forward to the end of November, then add December's days.
2. 48 divided by 7 is 6 weeks (42 days) with remainder 6. Why: full weeks bring us back to Saturday; only the remainder moves the weekday.
3. Saturday plus 6 days: Sunday (1), Monday (2), Tuesday (3), Wednesday (4), Thursday (5), **Friday (6)**.

**Answer: Friday.**

### Example 5: A fast clock
**Question:** A clock gains 6 minutes every hour. It is set right at 7:00 am. Later it shows 12:30 pm. What is the true time?
1. Shown time passed: 7:00 to 12:30 = 5 hours 30 minutes = 330 minutes.
2. In every true 60 minutes the clock shows 66. Why: it gains 6 each hour.
3. So true minutes = 330 × 60 / 66 = 300 minutes = 5 hours. Why: scale the shown time back down by the ratio 60 to 66.
4. True time = 7:00 am + 5 hours = **12:00 noon**.

**Answer: 12:00 noon.** Check: in 5 true hours the clock gains 5 × 6 = 30 minutes, so it shows 12:30. It matches.

## 2 real-life scenarios (all numbers illustrative)

### Scenario 1: A supplier invoice due date (commerce)
A supplier invoice is dated **12 December 2026** with payment terms "net 45 days" (pay within 45 days of the invoice date). When is it due?
1. Days left in December after the 12th: 31 - 12 = 19. Why: counting from the invoice date.
2. Remaining days to add: 45 - 19 = 26, so the date moves into January 2027: **26 January 2027**.
3. Weekday: 12 Dec 2026 to 26 Jan 2027 is 45 days. 45 = 6 weeks (42 days) + 3. 12 December 2026 is a Saturday, so the due date is Saturday + 3 = **Tuesday**.

Finance would pay on Tuesday 26 January 2027. If the rule were "net 45" and the due date fell on a weekend, the policy would need to say whether to pay earlier or later. Spotting that in advance is the value of a weekday check.

### Scenario 2: A probation end date and its financial year (HR)
An employee joins on **18 November 2026** with a 90-day probation. When does it end, which weekday is it, and which financial year (April to March) does it fall in?
1. From 18 Nov: days left in November = 12, December = 31 (total 43), January = 31 (total 74). Remaining 90 - 74 = 16 days, so the date is **16 February 2027**.
2. Weekday: 18 November 2026 is a Wednesday. 90 = 12 weeks (84 days) + 6. Wednesday + 6 = **Tuesday**.
3. Financial year: 16 February 2027 is before 1 April 2027, so it belongs to **FY 2026-27**, in its last quarter (January to March).

If your company uses a calendar-year basis instead, the same date is simply "Q1 2027". Always state which convention you use.

## Mistake log
1. **Forgetting that the hour hand moves too.** At 4:20 the hour hand is not exactly on 4; it has moved 10° past it. Use 30 × H + 0.5 × M, not just 30 × H.
2. **Quoting an angle above 180°.** If the formula gives 234°, the angle between the hands is 360 - 234 = 126°.
3. **Counting both end dates.** "From 10 March to 25 April" is a difference of dates; decide first whether the start day counts, and say so.
4. **Dividing by 7 and using the quotient.** For weekdays, only the remainder matters.
5. **Treating every century year as a leap year.** 1900 and 2100 are not; 2000 was.

## Recap
1. A clock face is 360°; the minute hand moves 6° a minute, the hour hand 0.5°, so the gap changes by 5.5° a minute.
2. Angle between hands = | 30 × H - 5.5 × M |, then take the smaller side (never above 180°).
3. Overlap, opposite and right angles all come from setting that gap to 0, 180 or 90 (or 270).
4. Fast and slow clocks are a ratio problem: shown minutes against true minutes.
5. Weekdays repeat every 7 days (use the remainder); leap years are divisible by 4, except centuries not divisible by 400.
