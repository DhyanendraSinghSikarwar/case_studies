# Chapter 27: Misleading Charts and Statistics

**What you will learn:** how a chart can be drawn so that true numbers give a false impression, which parts of a chart to read before you read the picture, and how to ask for the missing context (zero line, denominator, time window) that repairs it.

## Self-check (3 questions)

1. A bar chart's vertical axis starts at 94 instead of 0. Bars show 96 and 98. Does the second bar look about the same as the first, or much taller?
2. A pie chart has slices labelled 45%, 40% and 33%. What is wrong?
3. A newspaper says "average salary here is ₹76,000", yet most people earn about ₹36,000. How is that possible?

## Concept in plain language

### A chart is an argument made with pictures

A chart is not just data. The person who drew it chose **what to show, what to leave out, how to scale it and what to say in the title**. None of that is wrong in itself. It becomes misleading when the picture suggests something the numbers do not support.

The habit to build: **read the frame before you read the picture.**

### The checklist: read these first

| Part of the chart | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Does it state a conclusion ("Sales soar!") or just describe? |
| **Axis labels and units** | What is measured, in what unit (₹, %, count, per 1,000)? |
| **Scale and baseline** | Does a bar chart start at zero? Are the steps equal? |
| **Time window** | Why does it start and stop where it does? |
| **Denominator** | Is it a count or a rate? Out of how many? |
| **Groups and categories** | Are groups the same size or width? Are any left out? |
| **Source** | Who made the data, and when? |

### Common tricks (and honest fixes)

| Trick | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Truncated axis** | Cutting off the bottom of a bar chart makes small differences look huge | Start bars at zero, or print the true values on the bars |
| **Cherry-picked window** | Showing only a rising stretch hides a longer fall | Show the full period and say why a shorter one was used |
| **Picture sizes** | Making an icon taller and wider multiplies its area, so a doubling looks like four times | Repeat equal-sized icons, one icon per fixed amount |
| **Pie that does not add up** | Slices that sum to more or less than 100% | Check the sum; use a bar chart for multiple answers |
| **Unequal bins in a histogram** | Bars of equal width for bands of different size distort the shape | Make width proportional, and plot count per unit of band width |
| **Dual axes** | Two lines on two different scales can be made to touch or cross as the drawer likes | Show two charts, or label both axes clearly and index to 100 |
| **Mean hiding the middle** | One very large value drags the average up | Show the median and the spread too |
| **Counts instead of rates** | Large groups look "worse" simply because they are large | Show a rate (per 100, per 1,000) |
| **Percent of a tiny base** | "Up 300%" can be 2 to 8 | Show the actual numbers |
| **Cumulative line** | A running total always rises, even when each day is falling | Plot the daily values too |
| **Log scale unexplained** | Equal steps mean multiplying, not adding | State "log scale" and read it as ratios |
| **Reversed or unlabeled axis** | Up can mean "smaller" | Always read the axis numbers |
| **3D effects and decoration** | Perspective makes front bars look bigger | Use plain 2D |
| **Two lines that move together** | Suggest one causes the other | See Chapter 24; look for other explanations |

A fair note: **line charts do not always need to start at zero**. For example, a chart of body temperature could sensibly run from 36 to 38 degrees. What matters is that the axis is clearly labelled and that the scale suits what the reader needs to see. For **bar charts**, length encodes the amount, so a zero baseline is the safe rule.

### Two helpful arithmetic ideas (light tools)

- **Linear versus area.** If every side of a square is multiplied by 2, the area is multiplied by 2 x 2 = 4. For 3, by 3 x 3 = 9.
- **Rate.** Rate = count divided by size of the group (or time, or hours). Compare rates, not raw counts, across groups of different size.

> **Why this matters for data work.** Charts are the main way an analyst's work reaches decision makers. A chart that is accurate but easy to misread causes the same harm as one that is wrong. Knowing these tricks helps you criticise charts you receive and draw honest ones yourself.

## 5 solved examples

All data are invented for practice. Charts are described in words.

### Solved example 1: the truncated bar chart

*A bar chart compares attendance: Team North 96%, Team South 98%. The vertical axis runs from 94% to 98%. How do the bars compare, and what is true?*

1. **Read the axis first.** It starts at 94, not 0.
2. **Measure what the eye sees.** Bar height above the baseline: North = 96 minus 94 = 2 units; South = 98 minus 94 = 4 units.
3. **Compare the pictures.** 4 divided by 2 = 2, so South's bar looks twice as tall.
4. **Compare the truth.** 98 divided by 96 = 1.0208, so South is about 2.1% higher.
5. **Conclude.** The picture shows "double", the data show "about 2% more". The "why": a bar's length is read as the amount itself, so cutting the bottom off exaggerates the gap. Fix: start at zero, or label the bars with the actual numbers.

### Solved example 2: the cherry-picked window

*A slide titled "Orders are growing!" shows a line for October, November and December: 410, 430 and 455 orders. Month by month for the whole year the orders were: Jan 600, Feb 580, Mar 560, Apr 540, May 520, Jun 500, Jul 480, Aug 460, Sep 420, Oct 410, Nov 430, Dec 455.*

1. **Ask why the window is only three months.** Nothing explains it.
2. **Compute the shown growth.** From October to December: (455 minus 410) divided by 410 = 45 divided by 410 = 0.1098, about +11.0%.
3. **Compute the full-year change.** From January to December: (455 minus 600) divided by 600 = minus 145 divided by 600 = minus 0.2417, about minus 24.2%.
4. **Compare.** The three months are real, but they are a small recovery inside a year-long fall.
5. **Conclude.** "Growing" is true for the last three months only. The "why": the start and end dates of a chart are choices; ask what the line looks like if you go back further.

### Solved example 3: doubling that looks like quadrupling

*A bar-free chart shows revenue as coin pictures. Last year's coin is 2 cm tall and 2 cm wide. This year's revenue is exactly twice last year's, so the designer draws this year's coin 4 cm tall and 4 cm wide.*

1. **What did the designer intend?** "Revenue doubled", so height doubled and width doubled.
2. **What does the eye read?** The area of the coin, not just its height.
3. **Compute the areas.** Last year: 2 x 2 = 4 square cm. This year: 4 x 4 = 16 square cm.
4. **Compare.** 16 divided by 4 = 4, so the coin looks four times as big.
5. **Conclude.** The chart visually says "four times" for a doubling. The "why": area grows with the square of the side. Fix: use one icon per fixed amount (for example one coin per ₹10 lakh), and draw two coins this year against one last year.

### Solved example 4: the pie that adds to 118%

*A pie chart shows how employees commute: Bus 45%, Metro 40%, Car 33%. How should you read it?*

1. **Add the slices.** 45 + 40 + 33 = 118.
2. **Compare with 100.** A pie must total 100%. This one totals 18 points too many.
3. **Look for the reason.** Probably the survey allowed multiple answers (some people use both bus and metro), so each percentage is "share of people who use this", not "share of all trips".
4. **Decide what is safe to say.** 45% of employees use the bus; 40% use metro; 33% use a car. The categories overlap.
5. **Conclude.** A pie chart is the wrong shape here; use a bar chart with the note "multiple answers allowed". The "why": a pie promises that the pieces are parts of one whole.

### Solved example 5: unequal bins

*A histogram of monthly salaries (₹ thousand) shows three bars of equal width: 0 to 20 has 40 people, 20 to 40 has 30 people, 40 to 100 has 30 people.*

1. **Notice the band widths.** They are 20, 20 and 60 thousand wide, though the bars are drawn the same width.
2. **Compute people per ₹1 thousand of band width.** 40 divided by 20 = 2.0; 30 divided by 20 = 1.5; 30 divided by 60 = 0.5.
3. **Compare the shapes.** The drawn counts (40, 30, 30) look nearly flat for the second and third bars. The densities (2.0, 1.5, 0.5) fall steeply.
4. **Meaning.** The 40 to 100 band is a long, thin tail; people are spread thinly across it.
5. **Conclude.** With unequal bands, plot density (count per unit width) or use equal-width bins. The "why": in a histogram, the area of a bar, not just its height, represents the count.

## 2 real-life scenarios (illustrative numbers)

### Scenario 1: the cumulative sales line

A retail dashboard shows a line labelled "Sales this week" that climbs every day. The daily sales (₹ thousand) were Monday 100, Tuesday 90, Wednesday 80, Thursday 70, Friday 60.

- Running totals: 100, 190 (100 + 90), 270, 340, 400.
- The chart of running totals goes up every day, so it looks like growth.
- The daily figures fell every day, by ₹10 thousand each day, a fall of 100 to 60, which is 40%.
- Fix: plot the daily values (or both), and label the cumulative line as "running total".

Lesson: a running total can only go up if there are any sales at all, so it cannot show decline.

### Scenario 2: attrition counts versus rates

An HR chart of "Leavers by department" shows Sales 24, Support 15, Tech 10, so Sales looks worst. Headcounts are Sales 120, Support 50 and Tech 200.

- Sales: 24 divided by 120 = 0.20, or 20%.
- Support: 15 divided by 50 = 0.30, or 30%.
- Tech: 10 divided by 200 = 0.05, or 5%.
- By count: Sales, Support, Tech. By rate: Support, Sales, Tech.
- The department that loses the largest share of its people is Support, which the count chart ranked second.

Lesson: when groups differ in size, compare rates, and show the denominators.

## Mistake log

- **Reading the picture before the axis.** Always read the baseline, units and time window first.
- **Believing a zero baseline is required everywhere.** It is the safe rule for bars. For lines, a labelled non-zero axis can be honest.
- **Comparing counts across groups of different size.** Convert to a rate before concluding.
- **Trusting percentages on tiny numbers.** Ask for the raw counts behind "up 300%".
- **Assuming the chart's author lied.** Most misleading charts come from carelessness or default settings. Ask for context rather than accusing.

## Recap

1. A chart is an argument: read title, axes, units, baseline, window, denominator and source before the picture.
2. For bar charts a zero baseline is the safe rule; truncation makes small gaps look large.
3. Pictures scale by area: doubling both sides quadruples the apparent size.
4. Prefer rates to counts, median alongside mean, and daily values alongside running totals.
5. When in doubt, ask for the numbers behind the picture and redraw it plainly.
