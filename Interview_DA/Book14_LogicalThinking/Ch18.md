# Chapter 18: Puzzles with Floors, Boxes, Days, Months and Schedules

**What you will learn:** how to turn a pile of clues about floors, boxes, weekdays, months and work schedules into one table, fill it by elimination, and prove the answer is the only one.

## Concept in plain language

### What kind of puzzle is this?

All the puzzles in this chapter have the same skeleton:

- There is a small set of **things** (people, departments, boxes, meetings).
- There is a small set of **places** (floors, shelves, weekdays, months, time slots).
- Each thing gets exactly one place and each place is used by exactly one thing.
- You get a list of **clues**. Each clue is true, and together they point to **one** arrangement.

Your job is to find that arrangement. You do not need any maths. You need patience and a tidy way of writing things down.

### The six-step method

1. **List the things and the places.** Write the places in a row or column (floor 1 to floor 5, Monday to Friday) so you can see the order.
2. **Read all the clues once** and underline the exact words: "directly above", "somewhere above", "next to", "not next to", "before", "the day after".
3. **Draw a grid or a strip.** Rows are things, columns are places; or one strip of empty boxes in order.
4. **Place the fixed facts first** (a clue that names an exact place), then the strongest relationships ("directly above" ties two things together).
5. **Eliminate.** Cross out what a clue forbids. When a thing has only one place left, write it in. When a place has only one thing left, write that in too.
6. **If you are stuck, try cases.** Pick a place for one thing, follow the clues, and see whether you hit a contradiction. Then try the other place. Finally, **check every clue** against your answer.

### Words that trip people up

| Clue wording | Meaning | Example (floors 1 to 5, floor 1 lowest) |
|---|---|---|
| A is **directly above** B | A is exactly one floor higher than B | B on 2, A on 3 |
| A is **above** B | A is on a higher floor, any distance | B on 2, A on 3, 4 or 5 |
| A and B are **adjacent** (next to each other) | They differ by exactly one floor, either order | 2 and 3, or 3 and 2 |
| A and B are **not adjacent** | They do not differ by exactly one floor (they may differ by two or more) | 1 and 3 is allowed |
| A is **exactly two floors above** B | A is two floors higher, so one floor sits between | B on 1, A on 3 |
| "A is not on floor 3" | A may be anywhere except floor 3 | |

"Directly above" is stronger than "above". "Above" does not tell you how far.

### The same idea for days and months

- **Weekdays repeat every 7 days.** To find the weekday after N days, divide N by 7 and look only at the remainder. For example 10 days is one full week plus 3 extra days, so you move 3 weekdays forward.
- **Months have different lengths**: 31 days for January, March, May, July, August, October and December; 30 days for April, June, September and November; February has 28 days (29 in a leap year). A month with 31 days is 4 weeks plus 3 days, so the same date moves 3 weekdays forward from one month to the next; a 30-day month moves it 2 weekdays; a 28-day February moves it 0.
- Month names also form a circle: after December comes January. Counting "7 months after April" means counting on from May, one at a time.

### Scheduling puzzles

Some clues are about **time**: how long a task takes and which task must wait for another.

- A task that **needs** another cannot start until that other task has finished.
- Two tasks that do not need each other can run **at the same time** if two different people do them.
- The longest chain of waiting tasks is called the **critical path**. No amount of extra helpers can finish faster than that chain.
- Total work divided by the number of helpers (rounded up) is another lower limit on the finishing time.

### Assignment puzzles ("who does which task")

When each person can do only some tasks, start with the person or the task that has **only one option**. Lock it in, remove it from the others, and repeat. This is exactly the elimination idea again.

### Does the puzzle have exactly one answer?

A good puzzle has **one** answer. After you find an arrangement, ask: "Could another arrangement also satisfy every clue?" If you used elimination only (no guessing), you already know it is unique. If you guessed, you must have shown that the other guesses fail.

---

## Why this matters for data work

A set of clues that must all be true at once is the same shape as a set of rules in a data check or a filter: you keep only the rows that satisfy every condition, and you ask whether exactly one row is left. Scheduling puzzles also train you to see which step in a process has to wait for which, which is how you think about a data pipeline or a month-end report.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Floors (four departments)

**Problem.** Four departments (Finance, Legal, Marketing, Stores) occupy floors 1 to 4 of a building, one per floor, floor 1 the lowest. (1) Marketing is on the top floor. (2) Finance is directly above Stores. (3) Stores is on a higher floor than Legal. Who is on each floor?

**Step 1: Draw the strip.**

```
Floor 4: ?
Floor 3: ?
Floor 2: ?
Floor 1: ?
```

**Step 2: Place the fixed fact.** Clue 1 names an exact place: Marketing is on floor 4. (Why first: an exact fact needs no thinking and removes one thing and one floor.)

**Step 3: Use the strongest relationship.** Clue 2 ties Finance and Stores together as a block "Finance on top of Stores". Only three floors (1, 2, 3) are left, so the block fits at floors 2 and 3, or at floors 1 and 2.

**Step 4: Use the remaining clue.** Clue 3 says Stores is above Legal, so Legal must sit below Stores. If the block were on floors 1 and 2, Stores would be on floor 1 and nothing could be below it. So the block is on floors 2 and 3: Stores on 2, Finance on 3. Legal takes the only floor left, floor 1.

**Step 5: Check.**

| Floor | Department | Clue check |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Marketing | Clue 1: top floor, yes |
| 3 | Finance | Clue 2: directly above Stores (floor 2), yes |
| 2 | Stores | Clue 3: above Legal (floor 1), yes |
| 1 | Legal | |

**Answer.** Legal 1, Stores 2, Finance 3, Marketing 4.

### Example 2: Boxes (a grid)

**Problem.** A red, a blue and a green box hold a pen, a cup and a clip (one item per box). (1) The pen is not in the red box. (2) The clip is in neither the red nor the blue box. Which item is in each box?

**Step 1: Grid.** Rows are items, columns are boxes. Write X for "cannot be there".

| | Red | Blue | Green |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pen | X (clue 1) | | |
| Cup | | | |
| Clip | X (clue 2) | X (clue 2) | |

**Step 2: Lock the only option.** The clip row has only Green left, so the clip is in the green box. Cross out the rest of the Green column (the pen and the cup cannot be in green).

**Step 3: Continue.** The pen row now has only Blue left (red crossed by clue 1, green crossed because the clip is there). So the pen is in the blue box. The cup takes the last box, red.

**Answer.** Red box: cup. Blue box: pen. Green box: clip. Every clue holds: the pen is not red, and the clip is green.

### Example 3: Days and months

**Problem.** 5 March is a Saturday. What weekday is 28 March? What weekday is 5 April in the same year?

**Step 1: Count the gap.** From 5 March to 28 March is 28 - 5 = 23 days. (Why subtract: the gap between two dates in the same month is just the difference of the day numbers.)

**Step 2: Keep only the extra days.** 23 divided by 7 is 3 full weeks with remainder 2. Only the remainder changes the weekday: Saturday plus 2 weekdays is Sunday, then Monday. So 28 March is a **Monday**.

**Step 3: Next month.** March has 31 days, so 5 April is 31 days after 5 March. 31 divided by 7 is 4 weeks with remainder 3. Saturday plus 3 weekdays is Sunday, Monday, Tuesday. So 5 April is a **Tuesday**.

**Check.** 28 March is a Monday, so 4 April is also a Monday (28 + 7 = 35, which is 4 April), and 5 April is Monday plus 8 days, which is Tuesday. It agrees.

### Example 4: Scheduling with two people

**Problem.** A month-end job has three tasks: A counts cash (30 minutes), B sorts receipts (20 minutes), C writes the bank slip (10 minutes, and it needs A to be finished first). Two people can work together. What is the least total time? What if one person does everything?

**Step 1: One person.** 30 + 20 + 10 = 60 minutes. (Why: with one worker the tasks happen one after another.)

**Step 2: Find the waiting chain.** Only C waits (for A). The chain A then C takes 30 + 10 = 40 minutes. This is the critical path, so no team can beat 40 minutes.

**Step 3: Try to reach 40.** Person 1 does A (0 to 30) then C (30 to 40). Person 2 does B (0 to 20) and then is free. Everything finishes at minute 40.

**Answer.** Two people need at least 40 minutes (and 40 is reachable); one person needs 60.

### Example 5: A shift puzzle with an "if" clue

**Problem.** Gita, Hari and Ivy work the Morning, Afternoon and Night shifts, one each. (1) Hari is not on Night. (2) Ivy is not on Afternoon. (3) If Gita is on Morning, then Ivy is on Afternoon. (4) Gita is not on Night. Who works which shift?

**Step 1: List what each can do.**

| | Morning | Afternoon | Night |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gita | ? | ? | X (clue 4) |
| Hari | ? | ? | X (clue 1) |
| Ivy | ? | X (clue 2) | ? |

**Step 2: Night.** Gita and Hari are both barred from Night, so Ivy works Night.

**Step 3: Use the "if" clue.** Clue 3 says: if Gita is on Morning, Ivy would be on Afternoon. But Ivy is on Night, so the "then" part is false. An "if" clue whose "then" is false forces its "if" part to be false too. So Gita is **not** on Morning.

**Step 4: Finish.** Gita can only be on Afternoon, and Hari takes Morning.

**Answer.** Gita Afternoon, Hari Morning, Ivy Night. Check: Hari not Night, Ivy not Afternoon, Gita is not on Morning so clue 3 asks for nothing, Gita not Night. All hold, and elimination left no other option.

## 2 real-life scenarios

All numbers below are invented and labelled illustrative.

### Scenario 1 (commerce): packing an order in a small warehouse (illustrative)

An online shop packs one order in five steps (illustrative times):

| Step | Time | Must wait for |
|---|---|---|
| Pick the items | 20 min | nothing |
| Print the label | 10 min | nothing |
| Pack the box | 15 min | Pick |
| Seal the box | 5 min | Pack and Label |
| Hand over to the courier | 10 min | Seal |

With one worker the order takes 20 + 10 + 15 + 5 + 10 = 60 minutes. With two workers, the longest waiting chain is Pick (20) then Pack (15) then Seal (5) then Hand over (10) = 50 minutes. One worker picks and packs while the other prints the label early, so the label never delays anything. A third worker cannot do better than 50. So the second worker saves 10 minutes and the third saves nothing. The lesson: find the critical path first, then decide how many people are worth adding.

### Scenario 2 (HR and daily life): finding payday (illustrative)

A company pays salary on the last working day (Monday to Friday) of each month. In a month with 30 days, the 1st falls on a Saturday. Which date is payday?

- The 1st is Saturday, so the 8th, 15th, 22nd and 29th are Saturdays too.
- The 29th is Saturday and the 30th is Sunday, so neither is a working day.
- The 28th is the Friday just before, so payday is the **28th**.

The step that matters is "count weeks from a known date": jumping by 7s lands on the same weekday.

## Mistake log

1. **Treating "above" as "directly above".** "Above" allows any gap; "directly above" means exactly one floor. Reread the clue word by word.
2. **Treating "not adjacent" as "far apart".** Two things that are not adjacent can still be two floors apart. It only rules out neighbours.
3. **Counting days or months with the start date included.** "10 days from today" means move 10 steps forward from today, so today is step 0. For months, "7 months after April" starts counting at May.
4. **Stopping at the first arrangement that seems to work.** Check every clue, including the ones you used first, and ask whether another arrangement could also work.
5. **Reading an "if" clue backwards.** "If X then Y" does not say "if Y then X". It only gives information when X is true, or when Y is false (then X must be false).

## Recap

1. List the things and the places first, then draw a strip or a grid.
2. Place exact facts first, then tie related things together, then eliminate.
3. "Directly above" is one step; "above" is any gap; "not adjacent" only forbids neighbours.
4. Weekdays repeat every 7 days, so use the remainder; month lengths shift weekdays by 3, 2 or 0.
5. For schedules, the longest waiting chain (critical path) and total work divided by workers set the time limits.
