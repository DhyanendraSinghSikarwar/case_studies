# Appendix F: Glossary of Logic Terms

This glossary lists the technical terms taught in Book 14, in alphabetical order. Each entry gives a plain-language meaning, a tiny invented example where it helps, and the chapter where the term is first taught, written as (Ch N). All examples are invented and illustrative.

How to use it: look a term up when a chapter uses a word you have forgotten, then go back to the chapter for the worked examples. Where a chapter uses a word in a narrower way than everyday speech (for example, "argument" is a set of reasons, not a quarrel), the entry follows the chapter and says so.

Note: the entries are drawn from the chapters present in the book folder (Ch 1 to Ch 15, Ch 17, Ch 18 and Ch 20 to Ch 34). Terms taught only in Ch 16 and Ch 19, which were not in the folder when this appendix was written, are not listed.

## Easy to confuse

| Pair | The difference |
|---|---|
| Valid and true | Valid is about the shape of the reasoning (conclusion must be true if premises are true); true is about facts. A valid argument can have false premises. |
| Valid and sound | Sound means valid and all premises actually true. |
| Converse and contrapositive | Converse swaps p and q only; the contrapositive swaps and negates both and always means the same as the original. |
| Inverse and contrapositive | Inverse negates both without swapping and is not equivalent to the original; it matches the converse. |
| Deduction and induction | Deduction claims a guarantee; induction claims the conclusion is likely. |
| Induction (Ch 1) and mathematical induction (Ch 31) | Different things: the first is reasoning from cases to a likely general claim; the second is a proof method with a base case and an inductive step. |
| Correlation and causation | Correlation is moving together; causation is one thing making the other happen. |
| Distance travelled and displacement | Travelled adds every leg; displacement is the straight line from start to end. |
| Standard competition and dense ranking | Standard skips the number after a tie; dense does not. |
| Opposite and negation | A negation is false exactly when the original is true; a flipped word may leave both false. |

## A

- **Above** (Ch 18): One thing is on a higher floor or place than another, at any distance; it is weaker than "directly above". Example: B on floor 2, A on floor 4 means A is above B.
- **Add-the-two-before** (Ch 9): A series where each term is the sum of the two terms before it (Fibonacci type). Example: 2, 3, 5, 8, 13.
- **Ad hominem** (Ch 23): A fallacy that attacks the person instead of the argument. Example: "Her report is wrong because she is new here."
- **Adjacent** (Ch 18): Next to each other, differing by exactly one place, in either order. Example: Floors 3 and 4 are adjacent.
- **Algorithm** (Ch 32): A set of exact, ordered steps that, followed in order, solves a problem and ends. Example: Look at each sale, keep the biggest seen so far, then stop.
- **All (universal quantifier)** (Ch 5): A quantifier meaning every single one, with no exceptions; one exception makes it false. Example: "All staff have an ID card" is false if one person lacks one.
- **Analogy** (Ch 8): A pair of items with a relationship that is copied onto a second pair, written A : B :: C : D. Example: Pilot : aircraft :: driver : car.
- **Anchor (logic grid)** (Ch 10): The group (usually people) that every other group is tied to in a puzzle with three or more categories. Example: Uma is in Payroll, Payroll works nights, so Uma works nights.
- **Anchoring** (Ch 25): A bias where the first number you hear pulls your later estimate towards it. Example: A shirt marked "was 4,000, now 2,400" feels cheap even if it normally sells for 2,300.
- **And (conjunction)** (Ch 3): "p AND q" is true only when both p and q are true. Example: "Member AND bill above 999" needs both.
- **Anecdote** (Ch 26): A single story used as evidence; it is the weakest rung of the evidence ladder. Example: "My cousin quit and got a raise, so quitting pays."
- **Angle formula (clock)** (Ch 20): The angle between the clock hands at H hours and M minutes is the absolute value of 30 x H minus 5.5 x M, and if it is above 180 degrees you subtract it from 360. Example: At 3:00 the angle is 90 degrees.
- **Anticlockwise** (Ch 13): Turning the opposite way to clock hands, for example north to west to south to east. Example: Facing north and turning a quarter anticlockwise, you face west.
- **Appeal to emotion** (Ch 23): A fallacy that uses pity, fear or guilt instead of evidence. Example: "Think of the sad puppy, now buy our product."
- **Appeal to false authority** (Ch 23): A fallacy that leans on someone famous or senior who is not an expert on the topic at hand. Example: "A film star says this loan is the best."
- **Appeal to ignorance** (Ch 23): A fallacy that says a claim is true because nobody has disproved it (or false because nobody has proved it). Example: "Nobody proved it is unsafe, so it is safe."
- **Appeal to tradition** (Ch 23): A fallacy that says something is right because it has always been done that way. Example: "We have always done it, so we must continue."
- **Argument** (Ch 1): A small piece of text where some statements (premises) are offered as reasons for another statement (the conclusion); here it does not mean a quarrel. Example: "The warehouse floods every monsoon, so we should move the stock upstairs."
- **Arithmetic slip** (Ch 34): A bug where a calculation on its own step is simply wrong. Example: 90 divided by 12 written as 8.5 instead of 7.5.
- **Assumption (statement-assumption)** (Ch 21): Something the speaker takes for granted without saying it, and without which the statement would make no sense; the chapter checks it with the negation test. Example: "Please bring your ID card" assumes employees have ID cards.
- **Availability bias** (Ch 25): A bias where you think something is common because an example comes to mind easily. Example: Fearing one rare event you heard about last week.
- **Average of averages** (Ch 34): A bug where group averages are averaged without weighting by group size. Example: Averaging a team of 2 and a team of 20 as if they counted equally.

## B

- **Bandwagon** (Ch 23): A fallacy that says something must be right because many people do or believe it. Example: "Everyone is switching, so it must be good."
- **Base (percentage base)** (Ch 34): The starting number a percentage is taken of; using the wrong base is a common bug. Example: 10% of the new price is not 10% of the old price.
- **Base case** (Ch 31): The first step of an induction proof: check the claim directly for the first value, usually n = 1. Example: Show 1 = 1 x 2 / 2 before proving a sum rule.
- **Baseline (chart)** (Ch 27): The value where a chart's bars or line start; for bars it should normally be zero. Example: A bar chart starting at 90 makes small gaps look huge.
- **Base-rate neglect** (Ch 25): A bias where you ignore how common something is overall and focus on a test result or a story. Example: Treating a "fraud alert" as proof of fraud when fraud is very rare.
- **Blood relation** (Ch 14): A puzzle type where family facts are given in words and you must name how two people are related by drawing the family. Example: "Lila is the mother of Omar; how is Lila's husband related to Omar?"
- **Boundary value (edge value)** (Ch 11): The exact number named in a rule, which you should test because it often falls on the surprising side. Example: For "more than 500", the value 500 takes the No arrow.
- **Brackets** (Ch 3): Symbols that show which part of a logic rule to work out first. Example: In "p AND (q OR r)", work out "q OR r" first.
- **Bug** (Ch 34): A mistake in a program or, here, in a step of a written solution. Example: A wrong unit in step 2.

## C

- **Calendar remainder (odd days)** (Ch 20): The days left over after removing complete weeks, used to find a weekday far ahead. Example: 15 days is 2 weeks and 1 odd day, so one weekday forward.
- **Casework** (Ch 29): A strategy that splits a problem into separate, non-overlapping cases that cover everything, then handles each. Example: Split by how many 50-rupee notes are used: 0, 1 or 2.
- **Causation** (Ch 24): One thing actually makes the other happen, so changing the first changes the second. Example: A random coupon test raised next-week purchases.
- **Cause claim** (Ch 26): A claim that one thing made another happen; it needs a fair comparison. Example: "The new roster cut errors."
- **Cell** (Ch 10): One box in a table or matrix, found by reading a row and a column together. Example: The Payroll row and Contract column cell.
- **Chain (chained comparison)** (Ch 15): The fact that comparisons link up: if A is taller than B and B is taller than C, then A is taller than C. Example: A > B > C.
- **Chain (if-then shape)** (Ch 6): A valid shape: if P then Q, if Q then R, so if P then R. Example: If late then fined; if fined then upset; so if late then upset.
- **Check digit** (Ch 17): A last digit calculated from the earlier digits by a fixed rule, so a mistyped digit is caught. Example: The units digit of the sum of the first five digits.
- **Cherry-picked window** (Ch 27): A chart trick that shows only a rising stretch and hides a longer fall. Example: Three good months of a bad year.
- **Circular reasoning** (Ch 23): A fallacy where the conclusion is already hidden inside the premise. Example: "It is fair because it is just."
- **Claim** (Ch 26): A statement that says something is true, not a fact until it is checked; in Ch 30 it is also a statement to be proved. Example: "The office has 120 employees."
- **Classification** (Ch 8): Sorting things into groups using a rule you can check on each item. Example: Sorting expenses into travel, food and supplies.
- **Clockwise** (Ch 13): Turning the way clock hands move, for example north to east to south to west. Example: Facing north and a quarter turn clockwise, you face east.
- **Clue** (Ch 18): A true fact in a puzzle; together all clues point to one arrangement. Example: "Omar is not on floor 3."
- **Clue words** (Ch 1): Words that often signal a conclusion (therefore, so, hence) or a premise (because, since, given that). Example: "Rohan is late because the train was delayed" has the conclusion first.
- **Code** (Ch 17): A rule that changes information (the plain word) into another form (the coded word). Example: Moving every letter forward 2 places.
- **Code and coded word** (Ch 17): A code is a rule (the key) that changes information, the plain word, into another form, the coded word, and decoding runs the rule backwards. Example: Shift every letter forward 2 places: CAT becomes ECV.
- **Cognitive bias** (Ch 25): A predictable, repeating tilt in thinking caused by a mental shortcut; it is not stupidity and not lying. Example: Rating a whole year on the last two weeks.
- **Coincidence** (Ch 24): A no-real-link explanation for two things moving together, by chance or because both drift upward over time. Example: Two unrelated numbers rose over the same years.
- **Collapse trick** (Ch 14): Spotting that a middle part of a relation chain is a person you already have, so the drawing shrinks. Example: "My mother's only daughter" is me, if I am a woman.
- **Collision (code)** (Ch 17): Two different plain things producing the same coded result, so the code cannot be decoded safely. Example: Using only initials, two people both become "RK".
- **Column total** (Ch 10): The sum of the cells going down one column of a table. Example: Total contract staff across all teams.
- **Comparison group** (Ch 24): A fair group to compare against, so a before-and-after change is not fooled by seasons or trends. Example: Compare trained staff with similar untrained staff.
- **Compass directions** (Ch 13): North, East, South and West, with in-between NE, SE, SW, NW; on a map north is up and east is right. Example: Clockwise from N: NE, E, SE.
- **Complement** (Ch 7): Everything in the universe that is not in a set; think "not A". Example: If A is customers who bought a phone, A' is customers who did not.
- **Conclusion (argument)** (Ch 1): The claim that the premises are meant to support. Example: "So we should move the stock upstairs."
- **Conclusion (statement-conclusion type)** (Ch 21): In this chapter, something that must be true because of the given statement alone; if it might be true but is not guaranteed, it does not follow. Example: From "All dogs are animals" you cannot conclude "All animals are dogs".
- **Condition (if-then)** (Ch 4): The part after "if" in an if-then rule; textbooks call it the hypothesis or antecedent. Example: "Shipment is delayed" in "If delayed, send an apology email".
- **Condition (pseudocode)** (Ch 32): A yes/no test in pseudocode that decides whether some steps run. Example: IF number > largest THEN ...
- **Confirmation bias** (Ch 25): A bias where you look for, notice and remember facts that agree with what you already believe. Example: Counting only the days your favourite bus was on time.
- **Confounder (third factor)** (Ch 24): A hidden factor that affects both things being compared and can fake a link between them. Example: Hot weather drives both ice-cream sales and swimming accidents.
- **Context** (Ch 2): The place and time facts a sentence needs before it can be called true or false. Example: "It is raining" needs a place and a time.
- **Contradiction, proof by** (Ch 30): A proof that assumes the claim is false, follows the consequences to something impossible, and so concludes the claim is true. Example: Assume the biggest odd number exists, then show a bigger one.
- **Contrapositive** (Ch 4): The rule built from "If p then q" by swapping and negating both parts: "If not q then not p"; it always means the same as the original. Example: If no apology email, then the shipment was not delayed.
- **Controlled test** (Ch 26): The strongest rung of evidence: people assigned by chance to the options being compared. Example: Random coupons.
- **Converse** (Ch 4): The rule built from "If p then q" by swapping only: "If q then p"; it does not always mean the same as the original. Example: If the customer got an apology email, the shipment was delayed (not guaranteed).
- **Converse error** (Ch 34): A bug that treats "if A then B" as if it said "if B then A". Example: "All sales staff get an allowance, Meera gets one, so she is in sales."
- **Coordinate** (Ch 13): An address on a grid written as two numbers (across, up). Example: Walking 5 east then 3 north gives (5, 3).
- **Correlation** (Ch 24): Two things tend to move together; it is a pattern in the data, not proof that one causes the other. Example: Higher ad spend and higher sales in the same months.
- **Counterexample** (Ch 4): One case where the condition is true and the result false, which breaks an if-then rule; more widely, one case that breaks an "all" claim. Example: One Sales employee with no target breaks "Every Sales employee has a target".
- **Counterexample method** (Ch 6): Showing an argument is invalid by building a situation where every premise is true and the conclusion is false. Example: Premises "Some A are B; some B are C" with A and C kept apart.
- **Counting rule for three sets** (Ch 7): At least one = A + B + C - (A and B) - (A and C) - (B and C) + (all three), because people in the centre are added three times, removed three times, then added back once (often called inclusion and exclusion). Example: 20 + 15 + 12 - 6 - 5 - 4 + 2 = 34 people in at least one group.
- **Counting rule for two sets** (Ch 7): At least one = A + B - both, because the overlap is counted twice and must be subtracted once; it is often called inclusion and exclusion. Example: 60 + 50 - 20 = 90 people.
- **Counts instead of rates** (Ch 27): A chart trick where large groups look worse only because they are large. Example: More complaints from a big branch; compare complaints per 1,000 customers.
- **Course of action** (Ch 21): A proposed step to fix a problem, which should be followed only if it is relevant, practicable, proportionate and causes no new harm. Example: "Re-check the invoice totals" for a billing error.
- **Critical path** (Ch 18): The longest chain of tasks that must wait for each other, which limits how fast the whole job can finish. Example: A 3-step chain of 2 days each needs at least 6 days, however many helpers.
- **Cubes** (Ch 9): A number family: a number multiplied by itself three times. Example: 1, 8, 27, 64.
- **Cumulative line** (Ch 27): A running total that always rises, even when each day is falling. Example: Total sales to date keeps climbing while daily sales shrink.
- **Cycle** (Ch 9): A pattern that repeats after a fixed length; use division with remainder to find a far term. Example: Circle, square, square repeating has length 3.

## D

- **Data sufficiency** (Ch 22): Judging whether the facts given are enough to settle a question with exactly one answer, before calculating. Example: "Is the team bigger than 10?" given only the average age: not sufficient.
- **Debugging** (Ch 34): Finding and fixing a mistake, here in the steps of a reasoning chain. Example: Reading a solution line by line to find the wrong line.
- **Decision (diamond)** (Ch 11): A flowchart shape that asks a yes/no question, with one labelled arrow out for each answer. Example: "Total at least 999?"
- **Decision tree** (Ch 11): A flowchart that only branches forward, with no loops, ending in outcomes called leaves. Example: A bonus rule: tenure question, then rating question, then outcome.
- **Decoding** (Ch 17): Running a code rule backwards to recover the plain word. Example: ECV back to CAT by moving each letter back 2.
- **Deduction** (Ch 1): Reasoning where the author claims that if the premises are true the conclusion cannot be false, a guarantee. Example: All sales staff get a phone allowance; Meera is in sales; so Meera gets one.
- **Degree** (Ch 13): A unit for measuring turns; a full circle is 360, a half turn 180, a quarter turn 90, and each compass step 45. Example: North to east is 90 degrees clockwise.
- **De Morgan's laws** (Ch 3): Two rules: NOT (p AND q) equals (NOT p) OR (NOT q), and NOT (p OR q) equals (NOT p) AND (NOT q). Example: Not (a car or a bike) means no car and no bike.
- **Denominator** (Ch 26): The "out of how many" number behind a count or a percentage; without it a claim is hard to judge. Example: "Most complaints came from branch A" means little until you know branch A's size.
- **Dense ranking** (Ch 15): A tie method where tied people share a rank and no number is skipped. Example: Scores 90, 80, 80, 70 get ranks 1, 2, 2, 3.
- **Denying the condition** (Ch 6): An invalid shape: if P then Q, not P, so not Q; Q may happen anyway. Example: If it rains the road is wet; it did not rain; so the road is dry (a sprinkler may have run).
- **Denying the result (modus tollens)** (Ch 6): A valid shape: if P then Q, not Q, so not P. Example: If paid then receipt sent; no receipt; so not paid.
- **Direction (analogy)** (Ch 8): The order of a relationship, which must be kept the same on both sides of an analogy. Example: Page : book is not the same as book : page.
- **Directly above** (Ch 18): Exactly one floor higher than another; stronger than "above". Example: B on 2, A on 3.
- **Direct proof** (Ch 30): A proof that starts from what is given and moves step by step, each line following from the last, to the claim. Example: Write even numbers as 2k and 2m, add them to get 2(k + m).
- **Displacement (distance from the start)** (Ch 13): The straight-line distance from start to end, which can be much smaller than the distance travelled. Example: 5 km north then 5 km south: travelled 10 km, displacement 0.
- **Distance travelled** (Ch 13): The sum of every leg of a journey, like a vehicle's odometer. Example: 5 km east plus 3 km north is 8 km travelled.
- **Dose and response** (Ch 24): A steady pattern in which more of the cause goes with more of the effect, which supports a causal story. Example: More training hours, higher scores, at every level.
- **Double counting** (Ch 34): A bug where the same thing is counted twice, or an ordered pair as two. Example: Adding people who attended both trainings to each training's total and not removing the overlap.
- **Dual axes** (Ch 27): A chart with two lines on two different scales, which can be made to touch or cross as the drawer likes. Example: Show two charts, or index both lines to 100.

## E

- **Edge case** (Ch 32): An odd input such as an empty list, a single item or a tie that an algorithm must still handle. Example: Finding the largest value in an empty list.
- **Element (member)** (Ch 7): One item inside a set. Example: Asha is an element of "employees who joined this year".
- **Elimination** (Ch 29): A strategy of crossing out options that break a clue until one is left. Example: Cross out every candidate number whose last digit breaks a clue.
- **Encoding** (Ch 17): Applying the code rule to turn a plain word into a coded word. Example: Shifting every letter forward 2 places.
- **Equivalent** (Ch 3): Two statements are equivalent if their truth-table result columns are identical row by row. Example: "If p then q" and its contrapositive.
- **Estimate** (Ch 33): A rough answer that is close enough to make a decision and that you can explain step by step. Example: About 4,800,000 cups a day, with the steps shown.
- **Even number** (Ch 30): A whole number you can write as 2 times a whole number, 2k. Example: 0, 2, 4, 10.
- **Evidence** (Ch 26): What you can point to that supports a claim. Example: A dated HR export showing headcount.
- **Evidence ladder** (Ch 26): Ranking evidence from weakest (a single story) through expert opinion, survey and group comparison to a controlled test. Example: A random test beats an anecdote.
- **Exclusive or** (Ch 3): An OR meaning one but not both; you must say so, because logic's plain OR includes both. Example: "Tea or coffee, not both".
- **Exists (there exists)** (Ch 30): A claim that at least one case has the property; one example proves it. Example: "Some employee in Sales exceeded the target."

## F

- **Fair experiment** (Ch 24): Splitting people at random into groups, changing one thing for one group only, and comparing. Example: Give a coupon to a random half of customers.
- **Fallacy** (Ch 23): A pattern of bad reasoning where the reasons look like support but do not; it does not prove the conclusion false. Example: "Two rude riders, so all riders are rude."
- **False cause** (Ch 23): A fallacy that assumes A caused B just because B came after A. Example: "I wore my lucky shirt, then we won the bid."
- **False dilemma** (Ch 23): A fallacy that offers only two options when more exist. Example: "Either we cut salaries or we shut down."
- **False precision** (Ch 33): Giving a very exact number when the inputs were rough. Example: "4,812,306 cups" from guessed inputs.
- **Family tree** (Ch 14): A drawing of a family where each person is a labelled point and lines show parent, sibling or spouse links. Example: Piotr and Lila above Omar and Quincy.
- **Fencepost error (off-by-one)** (Ch 28): Miscounting by one when counting items or gaps; three sessions have two gaps, not three. Example: A 10 m fence with posts every 2 m needs 6 posts, not 5 and not 10.
- **Fermi problem** (Ch 33): A question with no easy exact answer that you solve by breaking it into guessable factors. Example: How many chai cups are sold in a city in a day?
- **Financial year** (Ch 20): A twelve-month accounting period; a common Indian convention is 1 April to 31 March, named for the two years it touches. Example: April 2026 to March 2027 is FY 2026-27.
- **Find patterns** (Ch 29): A strategy of solving small cases, writing results in a row, spotting the rule and checking it on one more case. Example: Table of results for 1, 2, 3 and 4 rows.
- **Finite** (Ch 32): A property of an algorithm: it ends after a limited number of steps. Example: It says STOP or runs out of items.
- **First faulty step** (Ch 34): The earliest incorrect step in a solution; it is the root cause, and later steps only carry the damage. Example: Step 2 is wrong, so steps 3 to 5 inherit the error.
- **Five-question routine** (Ch 26): WHO says it, WHAT evidence, HOW measured, WHAT is missing, CAN I CHECK elsewhere. Example: Apply to a forwarded message.
- **Flawed solution** (Ch 34): A worked answer that looks reasonable but contains a mistake. Example: A tidy five-line answer with a wrong base.
- **Flowchart** (Ch 11): A picture of a process: boxes for actions, diamonds for questions, arrows for order, starting at Start and stopping at End. Example: A returns process with a 7-day check.
- **Follows (certain)** (Ch 12): A verdict meaning the conclusion is true in every picture that obeys the statements. Example: "All X are Y; all Y are Z" gives "All X are Z".
- **For all (universal claim)** (Ch 30): A claim about every case; one counterexample kills it and only a general argument proves it. Example: "Every payslip is correct."
- **Framing effect** (Ch 25): A bias where the same facts feel different when worded differently. Example: "90% passed" feels better than "10% failed".

## G

- **Gap (difference)** (Ch 9): What you get by subtracting one term from the next. Example: In 6, 11, 16 the gaps are 5, 5.
- **Givens** (Ch 28): The facts you are told in a problem. Example: "The team has 12 people."
- **Grand total** (Ch 10): The corner total of a table, equal to both the sum of the row totals and the sum of the column totals. Example: If the two sums disagree, a number is wrong.
- **Group (syllogism)** (Ch 12): A collection of things drawn as a circle in a Venn picture. Example: "Invoices".

## H

- **Halo effect** (Ch 25): A bias where one good trait makes you rate everything else about a person or product higher. Example: Rating a smart-looking candidate as also punctual.
- **Hasty generalisation** (Ch 23): A fallacy that draws a broad rule from too few or unrepresentative cases. Example: "Two rude riders, so all riders are rude."
- **Helper column** (Ch 3): An extra column in a truth table for one small piece of a long rule. Example: A column for "q OR r".
- **Hidden conditions** (Ch 28): Easily missed words such as "between", "at least", "different" or "together" that change a problem. Example: "At least 3" includes 3.
- **Hindsight and outcome bias** (Ch 25): A bias where, after the result is known, you feel you knew it all along and judge a decision only by its result. Example: "That plan was obviously going to fail."
- **Hour hand and minute hand speeds** (Ch 20): The minute hand moves 6 degrees a minute and the hour hand 0.5, so the minute hand gains 5.5 degrees a minute. Example: The key number for clock problems.

## I

- **If and only if (iff)** (Ch 4): A rule that works in both directions: "If p then q" and "If q then p"; true when p and q are both true or both false. Example: A coupon works if and only if the cart total is at least 500.
- **If-then rule (conditional)** (Ch 4): A rule saying that whenever the first thing happens the second must follow; it is broken only when the condition is true and the result false. Example: If a shipment is delayed, the customer gets an apology email.
- **Implicit assumption** (Ch 21): An assumption the statement needs but does not say; it fails the negation test if flipping it makes the statement pointless. Example: "Employees do not have ID cards" makes "Bring your ID" silly.
- **Inclusive or** (Ch 3): The logic meaning of OR: true when either part is true or both are. Example: A member whose bill is above 999 still gets free delivery.
- **Induction** (Ch 1): Reasoning where the premises make the conclusion likely but a surprise is still possible, usually going from observed cases to a general or future case; it is not the same as mathematical induction (Ch 31). Example: The last five Mondays were busy, so this Monday will probably be busy.
- **Inductive hypothesis** (Ch 31): The assumption, in an induction step, that the claim is true for some value k. Example: Assume the sum rule works for k.
- **Inductive step** (Ch 31): The second part of an induction proof: assuming the claim for k, show it must be true for k + 1. Example: The sum up to k + 1 is the sum up to k plus the next term.
- **Input** (Ch 32): What a procedure starts with. Example: A list called sales.
- **Intersection** (Ch 7): The elements in both sets (A and B together). Example: Customers who bought both a phone and a case.
- **Invalid** (Ch 6): An argument is invalid if all premises could be true while the conclusion is false. Example: If P then Q; Q; so P.
- **Inverse** (Ch 4): The rule built from "If p then q" by negating both parts without swapping: "If not p then not q"; it means the same as the converse, not the original. Example: If the shipment is not delayed, no email is sent (not guaranteed).

## K

- **Key (code)** (Ch 17): The one rule that never changes inside a code question; your job is to find it. Example: "Shift every letter by 2."

## L

- **Last one standing** (Ch 10): A grid rule: if a row has crosses everywhere but one cell, that cell must be a tick. Example: One cell left means it is the answer.
- **Leaf** (Ch 11): A final outcome at the end of a branch in a decision tree. Example: "Bonus approved".
- **Leap year** (Ch 20): A 366-day year: divisible by 4, except century years, which need divisibility by 400. Example: 2028 is a leap year; 2100 is not.
- **Linear versus area** (Ch 27): If every side of a picture is multiplied by 2, the area is multiplied by 4, so doubling an icon's height and width looks like four times. Example: A twice-as-tall, twice-as-wide icon.
- **Loaded question** (Ch 23): A fallacy using a question with an unfair assumption built in. Example: "Have you stopped cheating on timesheets?"
- **Logic** (Ch 1): The study of good reasoning: do the reasons given really support the claim?. Example: "The road is jammed, so I will take the metro."
- **Logic grid** (Ch 10): A table of yes and no cells (ticks and crosses) used to solve who-goes-with-what puzzles. Example: People as rows, teams as columns.
- **Log scale** (Ch 27): An axis where equal steps mean multiplying, not adding; it must be stated. Example: Steps of 1, 10, 100, 1,000.
- **Loop** (Ch 11): A flow that goes back to an earlier step; in pseudocode, steps repeated for each item or while a test holds. Example: "Ask for the password again."

## M

- **Make-a-sentence method** (Ch 8): Finding the relationship in the first pair, stating it in a sentence, and reusing the same sentence for the second pair. Example: "A pilot flies an aircraft; a driver drives a car."
- **Maternal** (Ch 14): On the mother's side. Example: A maternal uncle is my mother's brother.
- **Mathematical induction** (Ch 31): A method to prove a claim for every whole number from a start value, using a base case and an inductive step; it is a proof method, not the guess-from-cases reasoning of Ch 1. Example: Prove a sum rule for all n.
- **Matrix** (Ch 10): A table where both the rows and the columns name things, so each cell answers a question about one row and one column. Example: Teams by employment type.
- **Mean hiding the middle** (Ch 27): A chart trick where one very large value drags the average up, so the median and spread should also be shown. Example: Nine salaries with one huge one.
- **MECE** (Ch 8): A grouping scheme where there is no overlap and nothing is left out. Example: Employment types: permanent, contract, intern, other.
- **Misread condition** (Ch 34): A bug that uses a number or rule the problem did not state. Example: Using 30 days when the problem said 28.
- **Modus ponens (affirm the condition)** (Ch 6): A valid shape: if P then Q, P, so Q. Example: If late then fined; late; so fined.
- **Multiple of 3** (Ch 30): A whole number that is 3 times some whole number, written 3m. Example: 3, 6, 9.
- **Multiplier (ratio)** (Ch 9): What you get by dividing one term by the one before it. Example: In 5, 15, 45 the multiplier is 3.

## N

- **Negation** (Ch 5): A statement that is false exactly when the original is true and true exactly when the original is false; it is stricter than an "opposite". Example: The negation of "All are present" is "At least one is not present".
- **Negation test** (Ch 21): Flipping a proposed assumption to its opposite and checking whether the statement becomes pointless. Example: "Employees do not have ID cards" breaks "Bring your ID".
- **Negative clue** (Ch 10): A clue saying something is not so, which is one cross in a grid and still a real clue. Example: "Naveen is not in Payroll."
- **Nested quantifiers** (Ch 5): A quantifier inside a quantifier, negated from the outside in, flipping each layer. Example: The negation of "Every department has at least one earner above 50,000" is "Some department has none".
- **No (none)** (Ch 5): A quantifier meaning zero; one example breaks it. Example: "No employee works on Sunday."
- **Not (negation word)** (Ch 3): "NOT p" is true when p is false. Example: NOT "invoice paid".
- **Not adjacent** (Ch 18): Not differing by exactly one place; they may differ by two or more. Example: Floors 1 and 3 are allowed.
- **Not sufficient** (Ch 22): Facts that leave more than one possible answer to a question. Example: Two situations obey the facts but give different answers.

## O

- **Odd number** (Ch 30): One more than an even number, written 2k + 1. Example: 1, 3, 9, 15.
- **Odd one out** (Ch 8): A list where one item does not follow the rule the rest share. Example: Water, tea, juice, chair, milk.
- **Only (word in relations)** (Ch 14): A word that makes a description point to exactly one person. Example: "My father's only brother".
- **Only if** (Ch 4): A wording where "p only if q" means "If p then q"; it points to what is required. Example: You may enter only if you have a pass means if you entered, you had a pass.
- **Open sentence** (Ch 2): A sentence with a blank that can change its truth, so it is not true or false until the blank is filled in. Example: "x + 4 = 10", or "salary above 50,000" before saying whose.
- **Opinion or value** (Ch 26): A claim of taste that cannot be true or false by itself; ask for the criteria. Example: "This is the best canteen."
- **Or (disjunction)** (Ch 3): "p OR q" is true when p is true, q is true, or both are. Example: Member OR bill above 999.
- **Order of magnitude** (Ch 33): Roughly how many zeros a number has; the main goal of a Fermi estimate. Example: Thousands versus millions.
- **Original (if-then)** (Ch 4): The starting rule "If p then q" from which converse, inverse and contrapositive are built. Example: If delayed, then apology email.
- **Output** (Ch 32): What a procedure must end with. Example: The largest sale.
- **Overconfidence** (Ch 25): A bias where you feel surer than your record justifies. Example: Saying "I am certain" and being right 6 times in 10.
- **Overlap (clock hands)** (Ch 20): When both hands point the same way, so the angle is 0. Example: Just after 12:00 and again about 1:05.

## P

- **Paternal** (Ch 14): On the father's side. Example: A paternal grandmother is my father's mother.
- **Pattern series** (Ch 9): A series made from shapes or tiles, solved by counting what changes at each step. Example: Tiles per step: 4, 7, 10.
- **Percentage points** (Ch 26): The difference between two percentages, not a percent of a percent. Example: From 8% to 10% is up 2 percentage points.
- **Percent of a tiny base** (Ch 27): A chart trick where a large percentage is a tiny real change. Example: "Up 300%" can be 2 to 8.
- **Pie that does not add up** (Ch 27): A pie whose slices sum to more or less than 100 percent. Example: Multiple-answer survey shown as a pie.
- **Plausible cause** (Ch 24): The story that A really does cause B, supported by evidence such as a fair experiment. Example: Random coupons raised purchases.
- **Polya's four steps** (Ch 28): A problem-solving routine: Understand, Plan, Execute, Review. Example: Read twice, name a method, work, check.
- **Position (series and letters)** (Ch 9): The place of a term or letter in an ordered list, where A is 1 and Z is 26. Example: C is position 3.
- **Practicable (course of action)** (Ch 21): A check that the people concerned can really carry out the step. Example: "Interview all 5,000 staff by tomorrow" is not practicable.
- **Prediction** (Ch 26): A claim about the future, which nobody can verify now; look at the reasoning and past accuracy. Example: "Sales will double next year."
- **Premise** (Ch 1): A reason or piece of evidence offered to support a conclusion. Example: "The warehouse floods every monsoon."
- **Prime number** (Ch 9): A whole number bigger than 1 divisible only by 1 and itself. Example: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11.
- **Proof** (Ch 30): An argument showing a claim is true in every possible case, using accepted facts and steps that follow one from another; trying five cases is not a proof. Example: Showing that any two even numbers add to an even number.
- **Proportionate** (Ch 21): A check that a step is not extreme. Example: "Close the office" for a late delivery is not.
- **Pseudocode** (Ch 32): Steps written exactly enough to read like code but in plain English, tied to no real programming language. Example: SET total TO 0.

## Q

- **Quantifier** (Ch 5): A word that says roughly how many items a sentence is about, such as all, some or none. Example: "Some employees work from home."

## R

- **Random assignment** (Ch 24): Splitting people into groups by chance so the groups are alike in everything else. Example: Draw names from a bowl.
- **Rank** (Ch 15): A place number in a list sorted by something; it tells order, not the gap. Example: Rank 3 of 10 by sales.
- **Rank from top plus rank from bottom** (Ch 15): The fact that rank from top + rank from bottom = total people + 1, because the person is counted in both. Example: 4th from top among 10 is 7th from bottom.
- **Rate** (Ch 27): A count divided by the size of the group, time or hours; compare rates, not raw counts, across groups of different size. Example: Complaints per 1,000 customers.
- **Recency bias** (Ch 25): A bias where you give too much weight to the latest events. Example: Rating a whole year on the last two weeks.
- **Red flags (messages)** (Ch 26): Warning signs in a forwarded message such as urgency, no author or date, vague "experts say" and requests to forward or to share money or ID details. Example: "Today only, forward to 10 friends."
- **Red herring** (Ch 23): A fallacy that changes the subject to something irrelevant. Example: "Why worry about delays? We won an award."
- **Relationship words** (Ch 14): Kinship words built from parent, child, sibling and spouse: grandparent (a parent's parent), uncle or aunt (a parent's sibling), cousin (a parent's sibling's child), nephew or niece (a sibling's child), parent-in-law (a spouse's parent), sibling-in-law (a spouse's sibling or a sibling's spouse) and son or daughter-in-law (a child's spouse). Example: My father's sister's son is my cousin.
- **Relative versus absolute change** (Ch 26): A relative change is a percent of the starting number; an absolute change is the actual difference; ask for both. Example: "Down 50%" can be from 2 in 1,000 to 1 in 1,000.
- **Relevant (course of action)** (Ch 21): A check that the step deals with the stated problem. Example: Fixing the payroll bug for a payroll error.
- **Remainder** (Ch 9): What is left after dividing, used for weekdays and cycles. Example: 10 divided by 7 leaves 3.
- **Reversal (letter code)** (Ch 17): A code rule that reverses the word or the alphabet (A = Z, B = Y). Example: CAT reversed is TAC.
- **Reverse cause** (Ch 24): An explanation where B actually causes A, the reverse of what you assumed. Example: Poor sleep leads to sleeping pills, not the other way.
- **Reversed or unlabeled axis** (Ch 27): An axis where up can mean smaller or the numbers are missing. Example: Read axis numbers first.
- **Reversible code** (Ch 17): A code where each coded word comes from only one plain word, so it can be decoded. Example: A one-to-one letter swap.
- **Right angle** (Ch 20): A 90 degree angle between two lines or hands. Example: Clock hands at 3:00.
- **Row total** (Ch 10): The sum of the cells across one row of a table. Example: Total staff in Payroll.
- **Rule (series)** (Ch 9): The pattern that generates every term in a series; the simplest rule that fits every term is the answer. Example: Add 3 each month.

## S

- **Sample size** (Ch 26): How many cases a claim rests on; "nine out of ten" can be ten people. Example: 9 of 10 is not 900 of 1,000.
- **Sanity check** (Ch 33): Checking an estimate with a second path or a common-sense limit. Example: Count stalls instead of people.
- **Self-selection** (Ch 24): A trap where people choose their own group, so the groups differ from the start. Example: Volunteers for training are already keen.
- **Series** (Ch 9): A list of terms in a fixed order, where each follows a rule. Example: 20, 23, 26, 29.
- **Set** (Ch 7): A group of things with a clear rule for "in" or "not in". Example: Customers who bought a phone.
- **Shadows** (Ch 13): A shadow falls away from the sun, so morning shadows fall west and evening shadows fall east. Example: Morning shadow on your left while facing north: sun is east.
- **Shift codes (constant, progressive, alternating)** (Ch 17): Letter codes where each letter moves the same number of places (constant), a growing number (progressive, +1, +2, +3) or a number that takes turns (alternating, +2, -1, +2). Example: CAT with +2 becomes ECV; with +1, +2, +3 becomes DCW.
- **Sibling** (Ch 14): Someone who has the same two parents as you; the word does not tell gender. Example: My brother or sister.
- **Simplify** (Ch 29): A strategy of solving the same problem with smaller numbers first, then spotting what changes. Example: Try 3 rows instead of 200.
- **Slippery slope** (Ch 23): A fallacy claiming one small step will inevitably lead to disaster, with no evidence for the chain. Example: "One late day and soon nobody will work."
- **Some (existential quantifier)** (Ch 5): A quantifier meaning at least one, which could even be all; it does not mean "not all". Example: "Some staff are present" is true even if all are.
- **Sound** (Ch 6): An argument is sound if it is valid and all its premises are actually true, which guarantees a true conclusion. Example: All birds can fly; a penguin is a bird; so a penguin can fly. The shape is valid but a premise is false, so it is unsound.
- **Source** (Ch 26): Where a claim comes from; closer to the original dated record is better, and many repeats are not many sources. Example: An HR export beats a forward.
- **Spouse** (Ch 14): The person you are married to; the word does not tell gender. Example: Husband or wife.
- **Squares** (Ch 9): A number family: a number multiplied by itself. Example: 1, 4, 9, 16.
- **Standard competition ranking** (Ch 15): A tie method where tied people share a rank and the next rank number is skipped. Example: Scores 90, 80, 80, 70 get ranks 1, 2, 2, 4.
- **Statement** (Ch 2): A sentence that makes a claim that can be settled as either true or false, not both; false and unknown-to-us claims are still statements. Example: "A week has eight days" is a false statement.
- **Statement (given facts)** (Ch 12): In syllogism and statement-assumption chapters, the facts you are given before judging an item; do not confuse it with the logic meaning in Ch 2. Example: "All invoices are checked."
- **Straw man** (Ch 23): A fallacy that twists someone's view into a weaker version and then attacks that. Example: "You want longer breaks? So you want nobody to work."
- **Subset** (Ch 7): A set whose every element is also in another set. Example: Course passers inside course attendees.
- **Sufficient** (Ch 22): Facts that leave exactly one possible answer; for a yes/no question, always yes or always no. Example: "Definitely no" is sufficient.
- **Sunk cost fallacy** (Ch 25): A bias where you keep going because of money or effort already spent and gone, not what lies ahead. Example: Finishing a bad film because the ticket was expensive.
- **Survey** (Ch 26): A set of answers from people asked; check who answered and how many. Example: A poll shared in one company.
- **Survivorship bias** (Ch 25): A bias where you study only the winners or survivors and forget those who dropped out of view. Example: Judging gyms by members who stayed.
- **Syllogism** (Ch 12): A tiny argument made of statements and a conclusion whose job is to decide if the conclusion has to be true from the statements alone. Example: All X are Y; all Y are Z; so all X are Z.

## T

- **Table** (Ch 10): Information arranged in rows (across) and columns (down). Example: Staff by team.
- **Term (series)** (Ch 9): One item in a series. Example: The third term of 6, 11, 16 is 16.
- **Three-D effects and decoration** (Ch 27): Perspective and decoration that make front bars look bigger and distract from the data. Example: Use plain 2D.
- **Time order** (Ch 24): The idea that a cause must come before its effect. Example: The coupon was sent before the purchase.
- **Time window (chart)** (Ch 27): The start and stop dates of a chart; ask why it begins and ends where it does. Example: A three-month window in a year of decline.
- **Top-down and bottom-up** (Ch 33): Top-down starts from a big group and takes shares; bottom-up starts from units like stalls and multiplies; doing both gives a check. Example: City population first, or number of stalls first.
- **Trace** (Ch 32): Walking through steps on a tiny example, writing down every variable after each step. Example: Track "largest" as you go.
- **Triangular numbers** (Ch 9): A number family whose gaps grow by 1 each time. Example: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15.
- **Truncated axis** (Ch 27): A bar chart axis that does not start at zero, making small differences look huge. Example: Bars starting at 90.
- **Truth table** (Ch 3): A grid listing every combination of T and F for the simple statements and the result of a rule for each; n statements need 2 to the power n rows. Example: Two statements need 4 rows.
- **Truth value** (Ch 2): True or false, the single value a statement has even if we cannot see which. Example: "A week has seven days": true.
- **Tu quoque** (Ch 23): A fallacy that dismisses advice by pointing out that the speaker does not follow it. Example: "You tell me to save, but you spend too."

## U

- **Unequal bins in a histogram** (Ch 27): Equal-width bars drawn for bands of different size, distorting the shape. Example: Plot count per unit of band width.
- **Union** (Ch 7): The elements in A, or B, or both; think "at least one". Example: Customers who bought a phone or a case.
- **Unit mismatch** (Ch 34): A bug that mixes units such as minutes with hours or decimal time with clock time. Example: 2 h 30 min written as 2.30 hours.
- **Universe** (Ch 7): The full collection being discussed; people in no group still count. Example: All 100 customers.
- **Unknown (problem)** (Ch 28): The thing you must find in a problem. Example: "How many pens?"
- **Unless** (Ch 4): "p unless q" means "If not q then p". Example: "Free delivery unless the bill is small" means if the bill is not small then delivery is free.
- **Unsound** (Ch 6): An argument is unsound if it is invalid, or valid with at least one false premise. Example: Valid shape, false premise.
- **Unsupported step** (Ch 34): A bug that draws a link the facts do not give. Example: "Sales rose, so the new roster worked."

## V

- **Vacuously true** (Ch 4): A rule or claim that is true only because its condition never happens or the group is empty. Example: "All unicorns in the office are purple".
- **Valid** (Ch 6): An argument is valid if the conclusion must be true whenever all premises are true; this is about the shape, not whether the facts are true. Example: All cats are bicycles; Tom is a cat; so Tom is a bicycle.
- **Variable** (Ch 32): A named box that holds a value. Example: SET total TO 0.
- **Venn diagram** (Ch 7): A picture of overlapping circles in a rectangle showing the regions of two or three sets. Example: Four regions for two sets.
- **Venn method (syllogisms)** (Ch 12): Drawing one circle per group, drawing each statement loosely, testing the conclusion, then trying to break it. Example: Redraw the loose parts another way.
- **Verdict (claims)** (Ch 26): One of three honest outcomes: supported, not supported, or cannot tell yet. Example: "Cannot tell yet: need the denominator."

## W

- **Whole number** (Ch 30): 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on in this book, with no fractions or negatives. Example: 7.
- **Work backward** (Ch 29): A strategy that starts at the end and undoes each step with its opposite. Example: Spent half: multiply what is left by 2.
- **Wrap-around** (Ch 9): After Z the alphabet starts again at A, so position 27 is A. Example: Three places after Y is B.
- **Wrong operation** (Ch 34): A bug that adds when you should multiply, or similar. Example: Adding rates instead of multiplying by hours.
