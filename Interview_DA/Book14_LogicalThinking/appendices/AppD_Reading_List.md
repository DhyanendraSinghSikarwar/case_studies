# Appendix D: Reading list

**What you will find here:** three books that connect to this book, what each one is about in plain words, which chapters of Book 14 each one goes with, and how a beginner can use each one.

> **Important: from memory, unverified.** Everything below is written from memory. Before you search for or buy any of these, check the exact title, the author, the edition and whether it is available to you (in a library, in a shop, or online). Different editions and translations may have slightly different titles or contents. We state no publication years, prices, sales figures, quotes or claims about what "studies show". We also make no claim that reading any of these books will make you better at anything.

This book does not require any of them. Our book is complete on its own. These are optional, for when you want a second voice.

## How to use this list (our suggestion)

- You do not need to read any of these cover to cover. Pick the parts that match the chapter you are studying.
- Read a little, then stop and close the book. Try to say the main idea in your own words, in one or two sentences.
- Read after you finish the matching chapters in this book, not before. The chapter gives you the basics first, and the book then adds a different angle.
- Keep notes in your error log (Chapter 37) when a book helps you see a mistake you made.

All reading suggestions in this appendix are **our suggestion, not research**.

## 1. *How to Solve It* (George Polya)

- **Title and author (from memory, unverified):** *How to Solve It*, by George Polya (the name is often written with an accent, Pólya). Check the title and the edition.
- **What it is about, in plain words:** It is a book about how to approach a problem when you do not know how to start. It describes a way of working: first make sure you understand the problem, then make a plan, then carry out the plan, then look back at your work. It also lists helpful questions and ideas you can ask yourself when you are stuck. The examples are mostly maths problems, but the method is about thinking in general.
- **Book 14 chapters it connects to:** Chapter 28 (Polya's 4 steps: understand, plan, execute, review) and Chapter 29 (strategies such as working backward, simplifying, finding patterns, casework and elimination).
- **How a beginner can use it (our suggestion):**
  1. Finish Chapter 28 in this book first, then read the parts of the book about the four steps.
  2. When you are stuck on a practice question in Chapter 29, open the book and look for the idea that fits what you are doing, for example "try a simpler version".
  3. You do not need to follow its maths examples in detail. Focus on the way of asking questions.
  4. Do not try to read it in one go. A page or two a day is fine.

## 2. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Daniel Kahneman)

- **Title and author (from memory, unverified):** *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, by Daniel Kahneman. Check the title and the edition.
- **What it is about, in plain words:** It is about how people think and decide. It describes two broad ways the mind works: a quick, automatic way and a slower, more careful way. The book then looks at the kinds of mistakes people make because of the quick way, such as being swayed by a first number they hear or by a vivid story. It is written for general readers and explains many ideas through short examples.
- **Book 14 chapters it connects to:** Chapters 23 to 27 (Part 4: Critical Thinking). The closest links are Chapter 25 (cognitive biases such as anchoring) and Chapter 24 (correlation versus causation), with background for Chapters 23, 26 and 27.
- **How a beginner can use it (our suggestion):**
  1. Read selected chapters only. Choose the ones whose titles match a topic you just studied, for example anchoring after Chapter 25.
  2. Treat it as a long book. It may feel heavy, so give yourself permission to skip parts and come back later.
  3. After each section, write one line: "Where might this happen in my own work, for example in hiring or in reading a report?"
  4. If a part uses terms you do not know, skip it and return after finishing more of this book.

## 3. *The Art of Thinking Clearly* (Rolf Dobelli)

- **Title and author (from memory, unverified):** *The Art of Thinking Clearly*, by Rolf Dobelli. Check the title, the translation and the edition.
- **What it is about, in plain words:** It is a collection of many very short pieces. Each piece is about one common thinking error, for example following the crowd or sticking with a bad decision because you have already spent money on it. The pieces are short and can be read in any order.
- **Book 14 chapters it connects to:** Chapters 23 to 27 (Part 4: Critical Thinking), most closely Chapter 23 (logical fallacies such as bandwagon), Chapter 25 (cognitive biases such as sunk cost and survivorship) and Chapter 27 (misleading charts and statistics).
- **How a beginner can use it (our suggestion):**
  1. Read one or two short pieces at a time, for example while having tea.
  2. Pick the pieces that match the names you learned in Chapters 23 and 25.
  3. After each piece, check: does the book's name for the error match the name used in this book? Names for the same idea can differ. If they differ, trust the explanation, not the label.
  4. Do not try to memorise the whole list. Choose three or four errors you often make and watch for them.

## How to read critically

A book on thinking is still a set of claims, and any claim can be wrong, incomplete, or true only in some situations. Use these checks with every book, including the three above and this one.

- **Separate the idea from the story.** An interesting story is not proof. Ask: what exactly is being claimed, and what would show it is wrong?
- **Look for the evidence.** Does the author show how they know this, or only tell you? Is the evidence something you can check?
- **Watch for "studies show".** If a book says this, ask: which study, who did it, and can I find it myself? We have not checked the claims in these books and we do not repeat them here.
- **Check for over-generalising.** An example of a mistake does not mean everyone makes it every time.
- **Notice your own bias.** If a claim feels good to you, that is the time to check it more carefully (Chapter 25).
- **Note the date and the edition.** Ideas can be updated, challenged or corrected after a book is printed.
- **Compare two sources.** If one book makes a surprising claim, look for a second, independent source before you accept it.

**Reminder: Chapter 26 teaches claim evaluation.** Use the same checks from Chapter 26 (who says it, what is the evidence, is it checkable, are there other explanations) on the claims in these books. A well-known author or a popular book does not make a claim automatically true.
