## Concept in plain language

### What is a set?

A **set** is just a group of things that we can clearly tell apart from things outside the group. The things inside are called **elements** (or members).

- "Employees who joined this year" is a set. Each employee is an element.
- "Customers who bought a phone" is a set.
- A good set has a clear rule: for any person you can say "in" or "not in", with no argument.

We write a set with curly brackets: `{Asha, Bilal, Chen}`.

### The universe

The **universe** is the full collection we are talking about. If we study 100 customers, the universe is those 100 customers. Every set we make is a part of the universe. Always ask: "How big is the universe?" because the people who are in no group at all still count.

### Two sets and their regions

Draw two overlapping circles, A and B, inside a rectangle (the universe). The picture has **four regions**:

| Region | Plain words | Name |
|---|---|---|
| Only A | in A, not in B | A minus B |
| Both | in A and in B | intersection (written A ∩ B) |
| Only B | in B, not in A | B minus A |
| Neither | outside both circles | complement of the union |

Two words you will meet again and again:

- **Union** (written A ∪ B): in A, or in B, or in both. Think "at least one".
- **Intersection** (written A ∩ B): in A and also in B. Think "both".
- **Complement** (written A' ): everything in the universe that is not in A. Think "not A".

### The counting rule for two sets

If you add the size of A and the size of B, the people in the overlap get counted twice. So you subtract the overlap once:

**At least one = A + B - both**

Then: **Neither = Total - at least one**.

### Counting regions, not circles

A circle's total (say "60 bought A") includes the overlap. The most common beginner error is to treat "60 bought A" as "60 bought only A". To find **only A**, take the circle total and remove the overlap:

**Only A = A - both**

### Subsets

If every element of one set is also in another, the first is a **subset** of the second. Example: everyone who passed the advanced course is also someone who attended it. The passers are a subset of the attendees. In a picture, the small circle sits fully inside the big one, and "only the small one" is empty.

### Three sets

Three overlapping circles give **eight regions** (seven inside the circles, one outside):

| Region | Plain words |
|---|---|
| Only A, only B, only C | in exactly one circle |
| A and B only, A and C only, B and C only | in exactly two circles |
| All three | in the centre where all circles meet |
| Neither (outside) | in none of the circles |

The counting rule for three sets:

**At least one = A + B + C - (A and B) - (A and C) - (B and C) + (all three)**

Why the "+ all three" at the end? People in the centre are added three times (once per circle), then removed three times (once per pair). So they vanish completely, and we add them back once.

Useful helpers (S means "the sum of the three circle totals"):

- Only A = A - (A and B) - (A and C) + (all three). The same pattern works for B and C.
- Exactly two = (each pair total minus all three), added up.
- S = (exactly one) + 2 x (exactly two) + 3 x (exactly three). This works because a person in two circles is counted twice in S, and in three circles, three times.

### The golden method

1. Say what the universe is and how big it is.
2. Name the sets (A, B, C).
3. Start from the **centre** (the "all" or "both" region) and work outwards.
4. Fill every region with a number; check that the regions add up to the total.
5. Read the answer from the right regions.

### De Morgan's rules (two handy equalities)

- Not (A or B) is the same as (not A) and (not B).
- Not (A and B) is the same as (not A) or (not B).

Example: "Not (a car or a bike)" means "no car and no bike".

---

> **Why this matters for data work**
> Almost every report has overlapping groups: customers who bought product A and product B, employees who completed two trainings, users seen on app and on website. If you do not know how to count the overlap once, totals come out too big. A Venn picture also helps you check whether a number in a report is even possible.

## 5 solved examples

### Example 1: Listing the regions (two sets, named elements)

Resume screening passed (R) = {Ira, Joel, Kavya, Leena}. Skills test passed (T) = {Kavya, Leena, Mohan}. Find R ∪ T, R ∩ T and the people who passed only the resume screening.

1. **Union:** collect every name that appears in either set, writing each name once. Why: union means "at least one", and a person is one person even if they appear twice. Result: {Ira, Joel, Kavya, Leena, Mohan}, which is 5 people.
2. **Intersection:** find names that appear in both lists. Why: intersection means "both". Result: {Kavya, Leena}.
3. **Only resume:** take R and remove anyone also in T. Why: "only" means we cross out the overlap. Result: {Ira, Joel}.

Check: only R (2) + both (2) + only T (1) = 5, matching the union.

### Example 2: Counting "at least one" and "neither" (illustrative numbers)

An online store looked at 200 orders. 120 had gift wrap, 90 chose express shipping, and 50 had both.

1. Universe = 200 orders. A = gift wrap (120), B = express (90), both = 50.
2. **At least one:** 120 + 90 - 50 = 160. Why: the 50 orders with both were counted in each total, so subtract them once.
3. **Neither:** 200 - 160 = 40.
4. **Only gift wrap:** 120 - 50 = 70. **Only express:** 90 - 50 = 40.
5. Check: 70 + 50 + 40 + 40 = 200.

### Example 3: Finding the overlap when "neither" is given (illustrative)

Of 75 employees, 48 enrolled in first-aid training, 33 enrolled in fire-safety training, and 12 enrolled in neither. How many enrolled in both?

1. People in at least one course = 75 - 12 = 63. Why: take away the people in no circle first.
2. Rearrange the counting rule: both = A + B - (at least one) = 48 + 33 - 63 = 18.
3. Check: only first-aid = 48 - 18 = 30; only fire-safety = 33 - 18 = 15; 30 + 15 + 18 + 12 = 75.

Why this works: the sum 48 + 33 = 81 is bigger than the 63 real people, and the extra 18 is exactly the double-counted overlap.

### Example 4: Reading a Venn from clues (illustrative)

In a survey of 60 visitors, 35 used the app, 20 used the website, and 8 used both. How many used only the app, only the website, and neither?

1. Only app = 35 - 8 = 27. Why: the 35 includes the 8 who used both.
2. Only website = 20 - 8 = 12.
3. At least one = 27 + 8 + 12 = 47.
4. Neither = 60 - 47 = 13.

Do not write the 35 in the "only app" region. The circle total is not the region.

### Example 5: Three sets (illustrative)

Among 100 employees, 40 use the cab service (C), 35 use the meal card (M) and 30 use the gym (G). Also 12 use C and M, 10 use C and G, 9 use M and G, and 4 use all three. How many use none, and how many use exactly two?

1. Start in the centre: all three = 4.
2. **Pairs only:** C and M only = 12 - 4 = 8; C and G only = 10 - 4 = 6; M and G only = 9 - 4 = 5. Why: each pair total includes the centre people.
3. **Singles only:** only C = 40 - 8 - 6 - 4 = 22; only M = 35 - 8 - 5 - 4 = 18; only G = 30 - 6 - 5 - 4 = 15. Why: remove everything else in that circle.
4. At least one = 22 + 18 + 15 + 8 + 6 + 5 + 4 = 78. Check with the formula: 40 + 35 + 30 - 12 - 10 - 9 + 4 = 78.
5. None = 100 - 78 = 22.
6. Exactly two = 8 + 6 + 5 = 19.

## 2 real-life scenarios

### Scenario 1 (commerce): shirts and shoes (illustrative numbers)

A shop recorded 500 shoppers on a sale day. 300 bought shirts, 220 bought shoes, 120 bought both.

- At least one item: 300 + 220 - 120 = 400.
- Bought nothing: 500 - 400 = 100.
- Only shirts: 300 - 120 = 180; only shoes: 220 - 120 = 100.

A manager who adds 300 + 220 = 520 buyers has more "buyers" than shoppers (500). The Venn picture shows why: 120 people were counted twice.

### Scenario 2 (data and HR): a training audit that does not add up (illustrative numbers)

An HR report says: 150 employees in the company, 90 completed Module A, 70 completed Module B, 30 completed both. A line in the report claims "everyone completed at least one module".

- At least one = 90 + 70 - 30 = 130.
- That leaves 150 - 130 = 20 employees with no module at all.
- So the claim "everyone completed at least one" is false for these numbers. The Venn check caught a data problem in two lines of arithmetic.

## Mistake log

1. **Treating a circle total as "only".** "60 bought A" includes the people who also bought B. Always subtract the overlap to get "only A".
2. **Forgetting the "neither" region.** The universe is bigger than the circles. Always subtract "at least one" from the total.
3. **Adding without subtracting the overlap.** A + B counts the overlap twice; this makes totals larger than the real population.
4. **Three sets: forgetting to add back the centre.** After subtracting the three pair totals, the all-three people have been removed once too often; add them back once.
5. **Mixing "exactly two" with "at least two".** "Exactly two" leaves out the centre; "at least two" includes it.

## Recap (5 lines)

1. A set is a clearly defined group; the universe is everyone we are talking about.
2. Two sets: at least one = A + B - both; neither = total - at least one.
3. A circle total is not the "only" region: only A = A - both.
4. Three sets: add the singles, subtract the pairs, add back the centre; fill the diagram from the centre outwards.
5. Always check that your regions add up to the total, and use a Venn sketch to test whether a reported number is even possible.
