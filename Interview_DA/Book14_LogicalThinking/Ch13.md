# Chapter 13: Direction and Distance

**What you will learn:** how to turn a "walk 5 km north, turn right..." story into a small map, so you can say where someone ends up, how far they are from the start, and which way they are facing.

## Concept in plain language

### The compass

A compass has four main directions: **North (N), East (E), South (S), West (W)**. Between them sit four in-between directions: **North-East (NE), South-East (SE), South-West (SW), North-West (NW)**.

On a normal map, **north is up, east is right, south is down, west is left**. Always draw it that way.

Going round like a clock (clockwise): N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW, back to N. Each step is a turn of **45 degrees**. A quarter turn is **90 degrees**, a half turn is **180 degrees**.

| Turn | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Clockwise (cw) | The way clock hands move: N to E to S to W |
| Anticlockwise (acw) | The opposite way: N to W to S to E |

### Left and right depend on where you face

"Turn right" does not mean "go east". It means "turn a quarter turn clockwise from the way you are facing now".

| You face | Turn left, you face | Turn right, you face | Behind you |
|---|---|---|---|
| North | West | East | South |
| East | North | South | West |
| South | East | West | North |
| West | South | North | East |

Tip: draw a small arrow for "the way I face" at every step. Never trust memory for left and right.

### Turning a story into a map (coordinates)

A **coordinate** is just an address on a grid: two numbers, (across, up). We use:

- East is plus across, West is minus across.
- North is plus up, South is minus up.

Start at (0, 0). Walking 5 km east takes you to (5, 0). Then 3 km north takes you to (5, 3). Every leg changes only one of the two numbers.

### Two different distances

- **Distance travelled:** add up every leg (the odometer of a vehicle).
- **Distance from the start (displacement):** the straight line from start to end. It can be much smaller. Walk 5 km north and 5 km south: travelled 10 km, but you are back at the start (0 km).

### Finding the straight-line distance

If you end up some distance across and some distance up, the straight line is the long side of a right-angled triangle. The rule (Pythagoras) says: straight line = square root of (across squared plus up squared).

You do not need to calculate this each time. A few friendly triples appear again and again:

| Across | Up | Straight line |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 6 | 8 | 10 |
| 5 | 12 | 13 |
| 8 | 15 | 17 |
| 20 | 21 | 29 |

Multiples work too: 3, 4, 5 doubled gives 6, 8, 10; tripled gives 9, 12, 15.

### Which side of the start are you on?

Look at the signs of (across, up):

| Across | Up | You are to the |
|---|---|---|
| plus | plus | North-East |
| minus | plus | North-West |
| plus | minus | South-East |
| minus | minus | South-West |

If one number is zero you are exactly (due) north, south, east or west.

### Shadows (a classic trick)

The sun rises in the east and sets in the west. A shadow always falls **away from the sun**.

- Morning: sun in the east, so shadows fall **west**.
- Evening: sun in the west, so shadows fall **east**.

If you know where a shadow falls and which side of you it is on, you can work out which way you face.

> **Why this matters for data work:** Many data tasks need a mental map: where points sit relative to each other, which way a change goes, and how far apart two things really are. Distance between two locations is also a column you will meet in logistics and store data. Practising "draw it, then calculate" builds the habit of turning a messy description into a clean picture before solving.

## Five solved examples

### Example 1: Turning by degrees (easy)

*Raju faces south-east and turns 90 degrees clockwise. Which way does he face now?*

1. Clockwise order is N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW. Why: we need a fixed order to count steps along.
2. A 90 degree turn is two steps of 45 degrees. Why: each step in the list is 45 degrees.
3. From SE, one step cw is S, two steps cw is SW.

**Answer: South-West.**

### Example 2: Straight legs and the straight line (easy)

*A picker starts at the dock, walks 21 m north and then 20 m east. How far is she from the dock, and on which side?*

1. Start at (0, 0). Why: we measure everything from the dock.
2. 21 m north: (0, 21). 20 m east: (20, 21). Why: north changes "up", east changes "across".
3. Straight line: across 20, up 21, which is the friendly triple 20, 21, 29. So 29 m. Why: the long side of a right-angled triangle.
4. Across is plus and up is plus, so north-east.

**Answer: 29 m, north-east of the dock.**

### Example 3: Left and right turns (medium)

*Meera faces west. She walks 16 km, turns left and walks 30 km. How far is she from where she began, and on which side?*

1. Start (0, 0), facing west. Walking west 16 km gives (-16, 0). Why: west is minus across.
2. Facing west, a left turn means facing south (from the table). Why: left of west is south.
3. Walking south 30 km gives (-16, -30). Why: south is minus up.
4. Straight line: 16 and 30 are double of 8 and 15, so the line is 2 times 17, which is 34 km.
5. Across minus, up minus: south-west.

**Answer: 34 km, south-west of the start.**

### Example 4: A shadow (medium)

*One morning, after sunrise, Ria stands so that her shadow falls directly behind her. Which way does she face?*

1. In the morning the sun is in the east. Why: sunrise is in the east.
2. The shadow falls away from the sun, so it falls west.
3. The shadow is behind her, so behind is west.
4. If behind is west, then in front is east (the opposite).

**Answer: East.** She is looking straight at the sun.

### Example 5: Travelled versus from the start (medium)

*A guard walks 18 m east, then 14 m north, then 18 m west. How far did he walk, and how far is he from the start?*

1. Distance travelled: 18 + 14 + 18 = 50 m. Why: add every leg.
2. Position: (18, 0), then (18, 14), then (0, 14). Why: the east and west legs cancel each other.
3. He is at (0, 14): exactly 14 m north of the start. Why: across is zero, so there is no sideways gap.

**Answer: walked 50 m, but only 14 m from the start (due north).** Notice how different the two distances are.

## Two real-life scenarios

*All numbers below are illustrative.*

### Scenario 1: The delivery rider's fuel claim

A rider leaves a store, goes 3 km west, 4 km north, then 3 km east to hand over a parcel. His claim sheet says "10 km travelled".

- Travelled: 3 + 4 + 3 = 10 km (what the fuel claim should use).
- Position: (-3, 0), then (-3, 4), then (0, 4). He ends 4 km due north of the store.

Lesson: a claim needs distance travelled. A "how far is the customer" question needs the straight line (4 km). Check which one the question asks for.

### Scenario 2: The recruiter's branch visit

An HR recruiter leaves head office and drives 15 km south and then 20 km east to a branch. Her manager asks, "How far apart are head office and the branch, and where is the branch?"

- Position: (0, -15), then (20, -15).
- Straight line: across 20, up 15 (a multiple of 3, 4, 5, five times over), so 25 km.
- Across plus, up minus: the branch is south-east of head office.

In a dataset, each office could be a row with an "across" and an "up" value; the same two-step thinking (draw, then measure) gives the distance column.

## Mistake log

1. **Treating "right" as "east".** Right and left depend on the way you face. Draw the arrow every time.
2. **Mixing up clockwise and anticlockwise.** Clockwise from north goes to east. Say the order N, E, S, W out loud to check.
3. **Adding legs when asked for distance from the start** (or the other way round). Read the question: "how far did he walk" adds; "how far is he from the start" is the straight line.
4. **Forgetting a sign.** West and south are minus. Keeping a small (across, up) table avoids this.
5. **Describing the wrong end of the line.** "A is north-east of B" and "B is south-west of A" are both true but opposite. Check who the question puts first.

## Recap

1. Draw a map with north up and east right, and track (across, up) from (0, 0).
2. Left and right depend on the way you face; each quarter turn is 90 degrees, each compass step is 45.
3. Distance travelled adds all legs; distance from the start is the straight line.
4. Use friendly triples (3, 4, 5; 5, 12, 13; 8, 15, 17; 20, 21, 29) and their multiples.
5. Shadows fall away from the sun: west in the morning, east in the evening.
