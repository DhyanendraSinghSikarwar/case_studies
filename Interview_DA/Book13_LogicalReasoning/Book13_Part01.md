### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Which day-to-day analyst skills is a logical reasoning test trying to measure, and which important analyst skills does it fail to measure?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Vendor test pages list Data Analyst as a target role for logical reasoning tests (vendor claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand why the gate exists and can talk about reasoning as a work skill, not a trick.
**Say this in the interview:** "The test is a quick proxy for careful thinking: reading rules exactly, spotting what follows and what does not, and working under time. It cannot show business sense, communication, SQL (the language for querying databases) or Excel skill, or how I handle messy real data."
**Answer:** **It measures: precise reading, deduction from given rules, pattern spotting, structured working and accuracy under time. It does not measure: domain knowledge, tool skills (SQL, Excel, BI), data cleaning on messy data, asking good questions, storytelling and stakeholder handling.**
**Check:** Not numeric; the skill list is our reading, with the vendor claim unverified.
**Explain simply:**
- Deduction means reaching a conclusion that must be true from the given facts. Analysts do this when they check whether a report's claim really follows from the data.
- Pattern spotting maps to noticing trends, outliers and broken rules in a dataset.
- A test has fixed facts and one right answer; real work has missing facts, so a test cannot see how you ask for them.
- Employers use it because it is cheap to run for many applicants (our reading, not a sourced claim).
**Worked mini-example:** Rule "all orders above 5,000 need approval". Deduction: an order of 7,000 needs approval. Real work adds: is the 5,000 in rupees or thousands, and who approves? The test checks the first, the job needs both.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does a high score prove you are a good analyst?* No, it only shows one slice of ability. 2) *How do you show the missing skills?* Through projects, SQL tests and clear explanations in the interview.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying the test is "just IQ" or "useless", or claiming it measures everything an analyst does.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a section, a correct answer earns 1 mark and a wrong answer loses 0.25 marks, and every question has 4 options. What is the expected score of a blind guess, and of a guess after you have confidently eliminated two options?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn a marking scheme into a guess-or-skip decision.
**Say this in the interview:** "Expected value is probability right times the reward minus probability wrong times the penalty. Blind: 0.25 times 1 minus 0.75 times 0.25 is 0.0625. After eliminating two: 0.5 minus 0.125 is 0.375."
**Answer:** **Blind guess: +0.0625 marks per question (1/16). After eliminating two options: +0.375 marks.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Expected value (EV) is the average result if you repeated the same situation many times.
2. Blind guess: chance right 1/4, wrong 3/4. EV = (1/4)(1) - (3/4)(1/4) = 0.25 - 0.1875 = 0.0625.
3. Two options removed: chance right 1/2. EV = 0.5 - 0.5(0.25) = 0.375.
**Check:** verified by script (exact fractions): 1/16 and 3/8.
**Explain simply:**
- A tiny positive EV means guessing helps only slightly; skipping scores 0.
- Each real elimination raises the odds, so confident elimination is worth more than raw luck.
- Caveat: 0.0625 is an average; one question is still a gamble.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Penalty 0.5, blind guess?* EV = 0.25 - 0.375 = -0.125, so do not blind guess (script-verified). 2) *Eliminate one option (3 left) at 0.25 penalty?* EV = 1/3 - (2/3)(0.25) = 0.1667, worth guessing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to multiply the penalty by the probability of being wrong, or treating "eliminated" as certain when you only half-eliminated.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Why should a beginner build accuracy before speed in reasoning practice, and how do you sequence untimed, loosely timed and strictly timed practice?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Advice to skip early and classify errors appears on Q&A sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a sensible learning method.
**Say this in the interview:** "Speed built on wrong methods only produces fast wrong answers. I first solve untimed until the method is right, then add a loose clock, then a strict one."
**Answer:** **Our suggested sequence: (1) untimed, aim for correct method and about 90% accuracy; (2) loosely timed, about 1.5 times the target time; (3) strictly timed at the real pace, then full mocks.** (The 90% and 1.5 times are our rules of thumb, not research.)
**Check:** 72 x 1.5 = 108 s (script). The 90% and 1.5 times figures are our rules of thumb.
**Explain simply:**
- Accuracy first means each wrong answer is a lesson about method; under a clock you only learn panic.
- Speed mostly comes from recognising patterns you have already practised, so it follows accuracy.
- Move to the next stage only when the current one feels routine.
**Worked mini-example:** Target 72 s per question. Stage 2 allows about 108 s (1.5 x 72). Stage 3 uses 72 s.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When do you return to untimed?* When one topic keeps failing in mocks. 2) *How often to take a full mock?* Our suggestion: weekly near the test date.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Doing only timed tests from day one, or staying untimed forever.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A section has 25 questions in 30 minutes, so the plan is 72 seconds per question. The first 5 questions took 9 minutes in total. What average time per question is left for the remaining questions, and by what percentage is it lower than the original plan?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Quick time-budget arithmetic and awareness that overspending early squeezes later questions.
**Say this in the interview:** "30 minus 9 leaves 21 minutes for 20 questions, which is 63 seconds each. That is 9 seconds below 72, so 12.5 percent lower."
**Answer:** **63 seconds per question, which is 12.5% lower than 72 seconds.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Remaining time = 30 - 9 = 21 minutes = 1,260 seconds.
2. Remaining questions = 25 - 5 = 20.
3. 1,260 / 20 = 63 seconds.
4. Drop = (72 - 63) / 72 = 9/72 = 0.125 = 12.5%.
**Check:** verified by script: 63.0 s and 0.125.
**Explain simply:**
- The first 5 questions used 108 s each on average (540 / 5), well above 72.
- Percentage lower = (old - new) / old.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the first 5 took 6 minutes?* 24 min for 20 questions = 72 s, exactly on plan (script-verified). 2) *What do you do?* Skip the slowest questions early.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing 21 minutes by 25, or computing the percentage against 63 instead of 72.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Candidate A attempts 15 questions and gets 60% of them right. Candidate B attempts 10 questions and gets 90% right. Each correct answer is worth 1 mark. Who scores higher (a) with no negative marking, and (b) with a penalty of 1/3 mark per wrong answer?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you see that accuracy and attempts trade off differently under different rules.
**Say this in the interview:** "A gets 9 right and 6 wrong; B gets 9 right and 1 wrong. With no penalty they tie at 9. With a one-third penalty A scores 7 and B scores about 8.67, so B wins."
**Answer:** **(a) Tie at 9 marks each. (b) B scores higher: B about 8.67 versus A 7.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. A: 15 x 0.6 = 9 right, 6 wrong. B: 10 x 0.9 = 9 right, 1 wrong.
2. (a) No penalty: A = 9, B = 9.
3. (b) A = 9 - 6/3 = 9 - 2 = 7. B = 9 - 1/3 = 8.67.
**Check:** verified by script with exact fractions (7 and 26/3).
**Explain simply:**
- Attempting more helps only if the extra answers are right often enough.
- Here A's 5 extra attempts added no net right answers but 5 extra wrong ones.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *A with a penalty of 0 and 12 right of 15?* A would lead (12 versus 9). 2) *At what penalty does B overtake A?* Any positive penalty, since B has fewer wrong answers.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying A wins because 15 is more than 10, or taking 60% of 10.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A test gives +4 for a correct answer and -1 for a wrong one. At what probability of being right does attempting a question break even, and does a blind guess on a 4-option question pay?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Setting up a break-even probability.
**Say this in the interview:** "Break even where 4p equals 1 minus p times 1, so p is 0.2. A blind guess has p of 0.25, above 0.2, so it pays slightly: +0.25 marks."
**Answer:** **Break-even probability = 1/5 = 20%. A blind guess (25%) pays: expected +0.25 marks per question.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. EV = 4p - 1(1 - p) = 5p - 1.
2. Set EV = 0, so p = 1/5.
3. Blind guess, 4 options: p = 0.25, EV = 5(0.25) - 1 = 0.25.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Break-even p = penalty / (reward + penalty) = 1/5. You need to be right at least once in 5 tries.
**Check:** verified by script: EV(0.25) = 1/4, EV(0.2) = 0.
**Explain simply:**
- Break-even is the chance at which guessing neither gains nor loses on average.
- Above it, attempt; below it, skip.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *+3 and -1?* Break-even 1/4, so a blind 4-option guess is exactly zero. 2) *+1 and -0.25?* 0.25/1.25 = 0.2, same as here.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Putting reward over (reward + penalty), which gives 0.8 instead of 0.2; the penalty goes on top.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Which words in a reasoning question stem (for example "only", "at least", "at most", "must be", "could be", "not necessarily") most often cause misreading, and what routine do you use to read a stem without losing marks?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Careful reading, the commonest source of lost marks.
**Say this in the interview:** "Words that set strength or limits flip the answer. I read the question line first, underline the limiting words, then read the data, and re-read the question before answering."
**Answer:** **Risky words: only, at least, at most, exactly, must be (always true), could be (possible), not necessarily (not forced), except, not, all, some, no.**
**Check:** Worked example enumerated by script: with 2 to 5 tall people, at least 1 is always tall, all 5 is possible, and exactly 2 is not forced.
**Explain simply:**
- Must be true means true in every possible case; could be true means true in at least one case.
- At least 3 means 3 or more; at most 3 means 3 or fewer.
- "Not necessarily true" means some case exists where it is false.
**Worked mini-example:** Fact: "at least 2 of 5 people are tall". Must be true: at least 1 is tall. Could be true: all 5 are tall. Not necessarily true: exactly 2 are tall.
**Our suggested routine:** (1) read the question first, (2) circle limiting words, (3) read the data, (4) solve, (5) re-read the question and tick the answer to it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Where do you lose most marks?* On EXCEPT and NOT items, so circle them. 2) *What if "some" appears?* Treat it as at least one.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "could be" with a "must be" test, or missing a word like "not".

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Rank these question types by how quickly most candidates can score on them (syllogism, simple coding-decoding, linear seating with 5 people, circular seating with 8 people and 6 conditions, 3-attribute grid puzzle), and explain how the ranking decides your order of attempt.

**What the interviewer is checking:** Triage: doing quick wins first.
**Say this in the interview:** "Quick types first: coding-decoding, syllogism, linear seating; then the circular seating; the grid puzzle last because it takes longest and one slip ruins it."
**Answer:** **Our suggested ranking, fastest to slowest: (1) simple coding-decoding, (2) syllogism, (3) linear seating with 5 people, (4) circular seating with 8 people and 6 conditions, (5) 3-attribute grid puzzle.** This is a judgement, not measured data; individual candidates differ, so check your own mock timings.
**Check:** 5! = 120 and 7! = 5,040 verified by script; the ranking itself is our judgement.
**Explain simply:**
- Fewer conditions and fewer positions to fill mean faster solving and fewer chances of error.

- One puzzle often supplies several questions, so judge it by marks per minute.
**Worked mini-example:** A 5-person linear seating has 5! = 120 arrangements, but 6 clues cut it to a few in a minute or two. An 8-person circle has 7! = 5,040 distinct arrangements, which is why clues must be used cleverly.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 After a timed mock test, how do you classify each lost mark into reading errors, concept gaps, calculation slips and time-pressure errors, and what is the fix for each type?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). The idea of classifying errors by failure mode appears on Q&A sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you learn systematically from mistakes.
**Say this in the interview:** "For each lost mark I redo the question untimed. If I get it right and I misread, it is a reading error; if I slip in arithmetic, calculation; if I could not start, a concept gap; if I was right but ran out of time, time pressure."
**Answer:** **Fixes (our suggestions): reading error, circle limiting words and re-read the question; concept gap, relearn the rule and do 5 similar items; calculation slip, write steps and estimate before computing; time-pressure error, set a per-question cap and skip then return.**
**Check:** Example tally 4 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 10 (script). The fixes are our suggestions.
**Explain simply:**
- Tag each lost mark with one label; a tally shows your largest leak.
- Count unattempted questions separately: they are time-pressure losses, not concept gaps.
**Worked mini-example:** A mock shows 10 marks lost: 4 reading, 3 concept, 1 calculation, 2 time. Fix reading first, since it is the largest and the cheapest to repair.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How often?* After every full mock. 2) *What if two labels fit?* Pick the first cause that would have stopped the loss.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Just reading the solution and moving on, with no tag and no fix.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do the common reasoning topics (syllogisms, conditional statements, ranking, series, critical reasoning) map to tasks an analyst does at work, such as filtering data, checking a join or challenging a conclusion in a report?

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In a 25-minute section of 20 questions, you plan 8 easy questions at 45 seconds, 8 medium questions at 80 seconds and skip the 4 hard ones in the first pass. How much time is left for each hard question if you share the remaining time equally?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Time-budget arithmetic.
**Say this in the interview:** "Easy takes 360 seconds, medium 640, total 1,000. The section is 1,500 seconds, so 500 remain, and over 4 hard questions that is 125 seconds each."
**Answer:** **125 seconds per hard question (about 2 minutes 5 seconds).**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Total time = 25 x 60 = 1,500 s.
2. Easy = 8 x 45 = 360 s; medium = 8 x 80 = 640 s; together 1,000 s.
3. Left = 1,500 - 1,000 = 500 s.
4. 500 / 4 = 125 s.
**Check:** verified by script: 125.0.
**Explain simply:**
- This assumes the first pass goes exactly to plan; keep a few seconds spare in practice.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If you do one of the hard ones in the first pass in 45 s, leaving 3?* Then 1,500 - 1,045 = 455 s is shared by 3, about 152 s each (script-verified). 2) *If the first pass overruns by 100 s?* 400 / 4 = 100 s each.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to convert 25 minutes to seconds, or spending the whole remainder on a single hard question.

### Q18 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A test deducts 1/3 mark for each wrong answer and every question has 4 options. What is the expected value of a blind guess, and what is the minimum number of options you must eliminate before guessing becomes worthwhile?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** The classic "fair penalty" idea.
**Say this in the interview:** "A blind guess is 1/4 minus 3/4 times 1/3, which is exactly zero. Eliminating one option gives 1/3 minus 2/3 times 1/3, which is 1/9 above zero, so one elimination is enough."
**Answer:** **Blind guess: expected value 0. Minimum eliminations for a positive expected value: 1 (EV = +1/9 = +0.111 marks); with 2 eliminated, +1/3.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Blind: EV = (1/4)(1) - (3/4)(1/3) = 1/4 - 1/4 = 0.
2. One option removed: p = 1/3. EV = 1/3 - (2/3)(1/3) = 3/9 - 2/9 = 1/9.
3. Two removed: p = 1/2. EV = 1/2 - (1/2)(1/3) = 1/3.
**Check:** verified by script (exact fractions): 0, 1/9, 1/3.
**Explain simply:**
- With penalty 1/(options - 1) the scheme is designed so pure guessing averages zero.
- "Worthwhile" here means a positive average; the gain from one elimination is small, so only eliminate when truly sure.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Penalty 1/2 with 4 options?* Blind EV = -1/8, so skip; one elimination: 1/3 - (2/3)(1/2) = 0, break-even. 2) *5 options and penalty 1/4?* Blind EV = 1/5 - (4/5)(1/4) = 0.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying a blind guess "has a 25% chance, so it is fine".

### Q36 ⭐ 🟡 In a week of practice you solved 40 puzzles. 28 were right at the first attempt, 8 more were right after a second look and 4 stayed wrong. What are your first-attempt accuracy and your final accuracy, and what does the gap between them tell you?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Percentage basics and reading error patterns.
**Say this in the interview:** "First attempt is 28 out of 40, which is 70 percent; final is 36 out of 40, which is 90 percent. The 20-point gap means many errors are slips I can fix by checking."
**Answer:** **First-attempt accuracy 70%; final accuracy 90%; gap 20 percentage points.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. First attempt: 28 / 40 = 0.70 = 70%.
2. Final: (28 + 8) / 40 = 36 / 40 = 0.90 = 90%.
3. Gap: 90 - 70 = 20 percentage points (8 puzzles).
4. Meaning: 8 puzzles were within your ability but failed due to slips (misreads, rushed steps), while 4 are real knowledge gaps.
5. In a timed test you do not always get a second look, so train a fast self-check to turn those 8 into first-time wins.
**Check:** Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Percentage points measure the difference between two percentages; 90% minus 70% is 20 points, not "20 percent".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Next step?* Log the cause of each of the 12 non-first-time results. 2) *If the 4 are one topic?* Study that topic.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reporting only final accuracy; ignoring the gap.

### Q37 ⭐ 🟡 When a puzzle can be solved either by brute-force enumeration of cases or by one clever deduction, how do you decide which to use under time pressure?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Practical judgement between safe and fast methods.
**Say this in the interview:** "I look for a clue that cuts the cases sharply. If I see one in a few seconds, I use it. If not, and the cases are few, I enumerate carefully."
**Answer:** **Take the deduction if you spot it within about 20 to 30 seconds (our suggestion); otherwise enumerate if the case count is small (say under about 6); if both are poor, skip and return later.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Scan clues for a fixed anchor (a known position, a unique value).
2. Estimate cases: 3 people have 6 orders, 4 have 24, 5 have 120.
3. If the count is small, enumeration is safe and mechanical.
4. If large and no deduction appears, leave it for later.
5. After a clever deduction, still verify the answer against all clues.
**Check:** Orderings counted as n!: 3! = 6, 4! = 24, 5! = 120 (hand-checked).
**Explain simply:**
- Brute force means trying every possibility; it is reliable but slow when the list is long.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Interview, not a test?* Say the clever idea aloud; if unsure, enumerate aloud. 2) *Both give different answers?* One has an error; recheck.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Hunting for a clever trick for minutes; enumerating without a clear scheme.

### Q38 ⭐ 🟡 With no negative marking and 20 questions, Candidate Y attempts 16 questions at 75% accuracy and leaves 4 blank, while Candidate X attempts all 20 at 55% accuracy. If Y guesses the 4 blanks at random on 4-option questions, what is Y's expected score, and who wins?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Expected value and the rule "never leave blanks without a penalty".
**Say this in the interview:** "Y gets 12 from the 16 plus about 1 from guessing, so 13. X gets 11. Y wins, and Y should always guess the blanks."
**Answer:** **Y's expected score is 13 (of 20); X's is 11. Y wins by 2 marks on average.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Y attempted: 16 x 0.75 = 12.
2. Y guesses: 4 x (1/4) = 1.
3. Y total: 12 + 1 = 13.
4. X: 20 x 0.55 = 11.
5. Without negative marking, a guess never costs anything, so blanks are always worse.
**Check:** Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Expected score is an average over many repeats; on a single test Y could get fewer than X by luck.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Without guessing, Y?* 12 versus 11, still ahead. 2) *With -1/3 penalty?* A pure guess has EV 0, so blanks cost nothing but gain nothing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating the guesses as guaranteed; ignoring that the marking scheme drives the decision.

### Q39 ⭐ 🟡 You have 7 days before a reasoning-heavy online test and you are weak in everything. How do you triage topics and spend your daily time?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Prioritisation under a deadline.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would pick the topics most likely to appear and easiest to improve, practise them in short timed sets, and review mistakes daily. I would keep the last day for a light mock and rest."

2. Days 2 to 4: learn rules for the top 3 to 4 topics (for example syllogism, seating, series, blood relations) with 20 to 30 questions each.
3. Day 5: add one more topic and do a mixed timed set.
4. Day 6: full-length mock under test conditions; review every miss.
5. Day 7: light revision of the error log, check device and ID, sleep early.

### Q40 ⭐ 🔴 You have 240 seconds left and no negative marking. Three unattempted questions need 40 seconds (success chance 90%), 90 seconds (80%) and 150 seconds (50%). Which questions should you attempt to maximise the expected number correct, and what is that expectation?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Choosing under a time budget by comparing value per time and total fit.
**Say this in the interview:** "I check which combinations fit in 240 seconds. 40 plus 90 is 130 seconds for 1.7 expected correct, which beats the others. Then I use spare time to check."
**Answer:** **Attempt the 40-second and 90-second questions; expected correct is 0.9 + 0.8 = 1.7.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. All three need 280 seconds, more than 240, so they do not all fit.
2. 40 + 90 = 130 s fits: 0.9 + 0.8 = 1.7.
3. 40 + 150 = 190 s fits: 0.9 + 0.5 = 1.4.
4. 90 + 150 = 240 s fits exactly: 0.8 + 0.5 = 1.3.
5. Best is 1.7, using 130 s; 110 s remain for checking.
6. With the spare 110 s, either check your two answers or, since there is no negative marking, put a guess on the third question rather than leave it blank (a random guess is a small bonus; the number of options is not given, so it is not counted in 1.7).
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Rank by success per second: 0.9/40 = 0.0225, 0.8/90 = 0.0089, 0.5/150 = 0.0033; take in that order until the time runs out. Here this order gives the optimum, but in general you should also check combinations (this is called the "knapsack" problem: choosing items to get the most value within a fixed limit).
**Check:** Verified by script: all 8 subsets enumerated.
**Explain simply:**
- Expected number correct means the sum of the success chances of the questions you attempt.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the 150-second question was 95%?* Then 40 + 150 gives 1.85 and beats 1.7 (hand-checked: 0.9 + 0.95 = 1.85, 190 s fits). 2) *With only 120 seconds?* 40 + 90 = 130 s no longer fits, so the best single question is the 40-second one with 0.9 (script-verified: no pair fits in 120 s).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking the biggest success chance first without checking fit; attempting the long question at the cost of two short ones.
