### Q56 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Argument: "A vendor's tool correctly flagged 90 of the 100 fraud cases in testing, so 90% of the claims it flags will be fraud." What is the flaw?
- (a) It treats the share of fraud cases found as the share of flagged claims that are fraud; the second also depends on false alarms and how rare fraud is.
- (b) It tested only 100 cases.
- (c) Tools should never be tested.
- (d) It rounds 90 to a whole number.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can tell "of all fraud, how much did the tool catch" (recall) from "of everything it flagged, how much was fraud" (precision).
**Say this in the interview:** "Catching 90% of fraud is a different number from 90% of flags being fraud. The second depends on how many honest claims get flagged and how rare fraud is. I'd ask for the false-alarm rate and the fraud rate."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Two different fractions: *recall* (also called sensitivity) = fraud caught / all fraud = 90/100. *Precision* = fraud among flagged / all flagged. The argument swaps one for the other.
2. Precision needs the false alarms (honest claims wrongly flagged). The question does not give them, so the 90% claim cannot be reached from the facts.
3. Illustration (our assumed numbers, not from the question): 10,000 claims, 1% fraud = 100 fraud cases. The tool catches 90. It wrongly flags 5% of the 9,900 honest claims = 495. Flagged = 585, of which 90 are fraud, so precision is 90/585 = **15.4%**, not 90%.
4. Change the assumptions and the answer moves: with a 1% false-alarm rate, precision is 90/189 = 47.6%; if fraud is 20% of claims and false alarms 5%, precision is 1,800/2,200 = 81.8%. So it depends on the fraud rate and false alarms.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Ask "90% of what?" The denominator in the premise (all fraud) differs from the denominator in the conclusion (all flagged). Different denominators means a flaw.
**Check:** the three precision figures above were computed by script (15.4%, 47.6%, 81.8%).
**Explain simply:**
- Recall answers "how much fraud did we catch?"; precision answers "when it shouts fraud, how often is it right?"
- When the thing you hunt is rare, even a small false-alarm rate buries the true hits (the "base rate" effect: the base rate is how common the event is overall).
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What would you ask the vendor for?* The false-positive rate and the real-world fraud rate, or simply a table of flagged versus actual fraud.
- *Is recall or precision more important?* It depends on the cost: missed fraud costs money; false flags cost investigator time and annoy customers.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b): 100 cases is a small sample, but that is not the logical flaw here (the swap of fractions is). (c) and (d) are absurd or irrelevant.

### Q57 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Team X's average score rose from 60 to 62 and Team Y's from 80 to 82. Last year X had 20 employees and Y had 80; this year X has 80 and Y has 20. What are the company averages last year and this year, and what does this show about "every team improved, so the company improved"?

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** weighted averages and Simpson's paradox (a trend in every group reverses in the combined data because the group sizes changed).
**Say this in the interview:** "Last year the company average was 76, this year it is 66. Both teams improved by 2, yet the company fell by 10, because most staff moved into the lower-scoring team. Team mix matters, so 'every team improved' does not mean the whole improved."
**Answer:** **Last year 76, this year 66 (a drop of 10 points), even though each team rose by 2.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. A company average is a *weighted average*: each team's average times its headcount, summed, divided by total headcount.
2. Last year: (20 x 60 + 80 x 80) / 100 = (1,200 + 6,400) / 100 = **76**.
3. This year: (80 x 62 + 20 x 82) / 100 = (4,960 + 1,640) / 100 = **66**.
4. Team X is now 80% of staff (was 20%), and X scores lower. The mix shift costs far more than the +2 gain in each team adds.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Weighted average = 80 plus (share in X) x (60 minus 80). Last year 80 - 0.2 x 20 = 76; this year 80 - 0.8 x 20 + 2 = 66 (both teams are +2, so add 2 to the base).
**Check:** both averages computed by script (76.0 and 66.0).
**Explain simply:**
- Team averages can all rise while the total falls when people shift toward the lower group. This is Simpson's paradox (also called a mix effect).
- To compare fairly, hold the mix fixed: with last year's weights, this year's scores give 0.2 x 62 + 0.8 x 82 = 78, which is +2 over 76.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Is the company really worse off?* Not necessarily: the scores of like people did improve (+2 each). The fall comes from composition, so report both the mix-adjusted and raw figure.
- *What if headcounts had stayed 20/80?* Then the company average would be 78, a rise of 2.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Averaging the team averages (60 and 80 give 70; 62 and 82 give 72) and concluding the company rose by 2. A plain average ignores headcount.

### Q58 ⭐ 🟡 Why can an answer option be completely true and still not strengthen or weaken the given argument? Describe the "out of scope" and "irrelevant comparison" traps.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you judge options by their *relevance to the link between premise and conclusion*, not by whether they sound true.
**Say this in the interview:** "An option only strengthens or weakens if it changes how likely the conclusion is, given the premise. A true fact about a different topic, or about a comparison the argument never made, leaves that link untouched."
**Answer:** Truth and relevance are separate tests. An option is useful only if it bears on the **link** between the evidence and the conclusion.
**Explain simply:**
- Argument = premise (evidence) + conclusion + an unstated *assumption* joining them. Strengthen means making that assumption more believable; weaken means making it less believable.
- **Out of scope:** the option is about something the argument never discusses. Example: "Online ads raised our sales; therefore we should keep the ad budget." Option: "Our office moved to a new building last year." True, but it says nothing about whether ads cause sales.
- **Irrelevant comparison:** the option compares things the argument does not compare. Example: "Our app has 4.5 stars, so users like it." Option: "Our competitor's app has 4.7 stars." True, but the claim was about users liking this app, not about ranking against rivals.
- Worked mini-example: argument "Sales rose after the new ad, so the ad worked." Weakens: "A big festival also began that week" (an alternative cause). Strengthens: "Sales rose only in regions where the ad ran." Neither effect: "The ad cost 2 lakh rupees."
- Quick test: write the argument's assumption in one line, then ask "does this option make that line more or less likely?" If not, drop it.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Can an irrelevant option become relevant?* Yes, if the argument's conclusion is about a comparison (e.g., "best in the market"); then the competitor fact matters.
- *What is the opposite-direction trap?* An option that sounds supportive but actually weakens (or vice versa); read whether the question asks to strengthen or weaken.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking the option that sounds most true or most impressive; or one that is too extreme ("always", "never"); or one that merely repeats the premise.

### Q59 ⭐ 🟡 What is regression to the mean, and why can it make a training programme look effective when you train only the lowest scorers and re-test them later?

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you know that extreme scores partly reflect luck, and that selecting extremes builds in an apparent improvement.
**Say this in the interview:** "Any score is skill plus luck. Pick the lowest scorers and many were unlucky; on a re-test their luck averages out and they score higher with no training at all. Without a comparison group I can't credit the programme."
**Answer:** **Regression to the mean is the tendency for extreme results to be followed by less extreme ones, because part of any extreme result was chance. Selecting the lowest scorers guarantees some "improvement" on re-test even if the training does nothing.**
**Explain simply:**
- Score = true ability + random noise (mood, luck, guessing, a bad day). Low scorers are a mix of truly weak people and people who had bad noise.
- On a re-test the noise is drawn again, and the unlucky ones are no longer unlucky on average, so the group average moves up toward the overall mean.
- Worked example (a simulation I ran, assumed numbers): 100,000 people with true ability averaging 70 and noise on each test. Take the bottom 10% on test 1: their average was 50.1. With no training, the same people averaged 60.1 on test 2, a "gain" of about 10 points from nothing.
- The fix: include a **control group** (similar low scorers who receive no training, or random assignment of who gets trained) and compare the *gain* of the trained group with the control's gain. Or select on one measure and evaluate on an independent one.
- The same effect is not limited to low scores: top performers tend to slip next time ("the sophomore slump").
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Does it mean training never works?* No: it only means the raw before/after gain overstates the effect; the true effect is the gain beyond the control group's.
- *When is the effect small?* When scores are very reliable (little noise) or when you do not select on the extreme.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Crediting the programme for the full jump; thinking regression means people literally "revert" because of some pull (it is a statistical artefact of selection plus noise); confusing it with the gambler's fallacy.

### Q60 ⭐ 🔴 An A/B test has 500 users per variant. Variant A had 10 conversions and variant B had 12 (2.0% versus 2.4%). The analyst writes: "B beats A by 20%, so B is better." Which is the best evaluation?
- (a) Proven, because 20% is a large uplift.
- (b) Not established: a gap of 2 conversions out of 500 per variant is small enough to arise by chance (a two-sided Fisher exact test gives p about 0.83).
- (c) Proven, because B is higher than A.
- (d) Can never be established with an A/B test.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you separate a relative uplift from statistical evidence and notice that the sample is too small for the gap.
**Say this in the interview:** "The 20% is relative: 2.4 versus 2.0 is only 0.4 percentage points, or 2 extra conversions. A Fisher exact test gives p about 0.83, so this gap is very plausibly noise. Not established; I'd need a larger sample."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Relative uplift = 12/10 - 1 = 20%. Absolute difference = 2.4% - 2.0% = 0.4 percentage points (just 2 conversions).
2. Table: A has 10 converted and 490 not; B has 12 converted and 488 not. Fisher's exact test (it computes the exact chance of a gap this large or larger if both variants truly convert equally) gives a two-sided p-value of **0.830** (scipy, verified).
3. A p-value of 0.83 means a gap this big or bigger would occur about 83% of the time by chance alone. There is almost no evidence of a real difference. (A chi-square test without continuity correction gives about 0.67; with such small counts Fisher is the better choice, and both say "not significant".)
4. Not significant does not prove they are equal; it means the test cannot tell them apart. More data is needed.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Counts of 10 versus 12: the typical random wobble of a count near 10 is about square root of 10, roughly 3. A gap of 2 is well inside that wobble.
**Check:** p-value computed by script with a Fisher exact test on [[10,490],[12,488]] (0.8299).
**Explain simply:**
- Relative lift (20%) can be huge on a tiny base; always ask for the absolute difference and the sample size.
- p-value: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if there were truly no difference. Big p means "consistent with chance".
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What would convince you?* A much larger test, planned in advance (power calculation), or a confidence interval for the difference that excludes zero. Ten times the traffic at the same rates (100 versus 120 conversions in 5,000 each) would be far more persuasive, though still a judgement call.
- *Is A therefore better?* No; the data do not favour either.
**Common mistakes / traps:** (a) is the "big percentage" trap; (c) is "higher means better" ignoring chance; (d) overreaches, since A/B tests can establish differences with enough data.

### Q61 ⭐ 🔴 A firm says its training programme has "a 95% success rate" because 38 of the 40 participants completed it. What is the key question to ask, and what would a better success measure be?

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you spot a metric that measures an activity (completion) instead of the outcome that matters, and the sample question (who is in the 40).
**Say this in the interview:** "38 of 40 is 95%, but that is completion, not success. I'd ask what 'success' is meant to achieve, and whether the 40 includes everyone who started. A better measure is an outcome such as a skills test gain or job performance, compared with a similar group that was not trained."
**Answer:** **Key question: "Success at what?" Completion shows people finished, not that they learned or performed better (and we should check whether dropouts were excluded). A better measure is an outcome measured before and after, compared with a control group.**
**Explain simply:**
- Arithmetic is fine: 38/40 = 95% (verified). The problem is the *definition* of success and the *denominator*.
- **Denominator question:** were the 40 everyone enrolled, or only people who stayed? If 60 enrolled and 20 dropped out, the completion rate is 38/60 = 63%. (Hypothetical numbers, to show the point.)
- **Outcome measures:** test scores before versus after; on-the-job metrics such as error rate or sales after 3 to 6 months; promotion or retention.
- **Comparison:** compare against similar people who did not do the programme (or ideally were randomly chosen not to), otherwise improvements may come from experience, selection of keen people, or regression to the mean (see Q59).
- Worked mini-example: trained group's average test score rises 10 points; a comparable untrained group rises 6; the programme's effect is about 4 points, not 10.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Is completion useless?* No; it is a useful engagement indicator, just not an effectiveness measure.
- *How big a sample do you need?* 40 is small; ask for confidence intervals, not only the headline percentage.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Accepting "95%" because the sums are right; ignoring survivorship (people who dropped out vanish from the figure); treating before/after improvement as proof of cause.

### Q62 ⭐ 🔴 A firm says its AI screening tool "shortlists better candidates" because shortlisted candidates scored 20% higher at interview than last year's shortlist. Which information is most useful to evaluate this?
- (a) Whether interview scores or the interview panel changed, and how candidates rejected by the tool would have scored.
- (b) How fast the tool runs.
- (c) How many candidates applied.
- (d) What the vendor charges.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you look for alternative explanations (changed measuring stick, different applicant pool) and the missing counterfactual (what the rejected would have done).
**Say this in the interview:** "A 20% higher score only counts if the interview scoring and panel are the same as last year, and if I know how the people the tool rejected would have scored. Otherwise the gain may come from a changed yardstick, not a better tool."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Claim: tool causes better shortlist. Evidence: this year's shortlist scored 20% higher than last year's.
2. Alternative explanations: a different panel or marking scheme (a more lenient scorer), a different applicant pool, interviewers influenced by knowing the tool picked them, or chance.
3. Option (a) tests both: whether the yardstick changed, and the **counterfactual** (what would have happened without the tool), shown by scoring a sample of rejected candidates. If rejected candidates would have scored as well, the tool adds little. It also checks whether the tool hides good candidates (only looking at those it shortlisted cannot reveal that).
4. Options (b), (c), (d) are about speed, volume and cost, which do not bear on whether the shortlist is better.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Ask what else changed besides the tool, and what the comparison group is. The option that answers these wins; the others are operational facts.
**Check:** reasoned by hand (a judgement-based item, nothing to compute).
**Explain simply:**
- A fair comparison needs the same measuring stick and a like-for-like group. "Better than last year" fails both unless shown otherwise.
- Selection effect: you only observe outcomes for people the tool chose; the rejected group is the missing control.
- Interview score is also only a proxy for what we care about, which is later job performance.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Best design to test it?* Randomly send some applicants through the old process and some through the tool, then compare later job performance.
- *Could (c) ever matter?* Slightly, if applicant volume changed (a different pool), but it does not by itself test quality.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing speed or cost because they are concrete and easy to find (irrelevant to quality); accepting the 20% at face value; confusing "shortlist scores rose" with "the tool is the cause".
