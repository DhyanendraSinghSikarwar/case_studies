### Q15 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What evidence do you rely on when someone asks whether solving logic puzzles improves general reasoning? What can you honestly claim after practising, and what should you not claim?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a "be honest about limits" question in skill-development rounds.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate "I got better at these tasks" from "I became smarter", and whether you can say what the evidence does and does not show.
**Say this in the interview:** "The evidence that puzzles improve reasoning in general is weak and mixed, so I do not claim that. I claim what I can measure: my accuracy and speed on timed sets of the question types I practised went up, and I can explain the method."
**Answer:** **Rely on your own measured before-and-after results on similar tasks; claim task-specific improvement and clearer explanations; do not claim a higher IQ, better judgement in general, or guaranteed test or job results.**
**Explain simply:**
- Transfer means a skill learned on one task helps on a different task. Experts generally say transfer is most likely when the new task looks like the practised one (opinion piece, unverified, not read in full).
- One human study snippet we found (PMC4569435, 2015; from the search summary only, we did not read the paper, so treat as unverified) reported transfer from LSAT-style reasoning instruction to a transitive-inference task (if A is taller than B and B taller than C, then A is taller than C) but not to a rule-generation task. One study does not settle the question.
- So the honest position is narrow: practice on seating puzzles makes you faster at seating puzzles and similar ranking problems.
- Your own evidence is a log: same-style 20-question set in week 1 and week 4, with correct answers and minutes.
**Worked mini-example:** Week 1: 11 of 20 correct (55%); week 4: 16 of 20 (80%) on a comparable set. The honest sentence is "my accuracy on this kind of set rose from 55% to 80%". Not honest: "my reasoning improved by 25 points". Two sets of 20 are a small sample, and the later set may have been easier or partly remembered.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you make the claim stronger?* Use a fresh set of new variants you have never seen, similar difficulty, and repeat it a few times. 2) *Does this matter for the job?* Only indirectly: the interview tests the task, so task-specific skill is what you need; the on-the-job benefit (careful checking of conditions) is something you show with work samples, not claim.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Quoting brain-training articles as proof; reporting practice-set scores on questions you had already seen; saying "puzzles made me a better analyst" with no example.

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Your scores have stopped improving for two weeks. How do you diagnose the plateau (topic, method, speed or rest), and what do you change in the plan?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you diagnose from data (your error log) instead of just working harder.
**Say this in the interview:** "I open my error log and split the wrong answers by topic, by cause and by whether I ran out of time. Then I change one thing, the biggest cause, for a week and re-test."
**Answer:** **Sort the misses by cause (topic gap, wrong method, speed, tiredness), fix the largest group first, change only one thing at a time, and re-measure after a week. Rest is a real cause, so check sleep and the time of day too.**
**Explain simply:**
- A plateau is a flat stretch in your scores. It often means the easy gains are used up and the remaining errors have one dominant cause.
- Four causes, four checks:
  - Topic: one topic holds most misses. Fix: go back to that topic's Part (Parts 2 to 10 of this book) and relearn the method.
  - Method: you get the right answer only after long trial and error, or you skip a drawing. Fix: write the diagram or table every time, even when it feels slow.
  - Speed: wrong answers are mostly in the last third of the timed set, or marked "ran out of time". Fix: add a skip rule and short timed drills.
  - Rest: errors are careless slips (misread "not", "at most") and cluster in late-night sessions. Fix: move practice earlier and take a rest day.
- Do not change everything at once, or you cannot tell what worked.
**Worked mini-example:** Two weeks stuck at 14 of 20. Error log shows 6 misses: 3 seating (all from not drawing the row), 1 critical reasoning, 2 careless misreads. Largest cause is method on seating. Change: draw the row for every seating question for one week and do 5 extra seating questions a day; leave other settings alone. Re-test next week on a fresh 20.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the log shows no pattern?* Then the plateau may just be noise: 20-question sets swing by a few questions, so compare two-week averages, not single sets. 2) *When do you rest?* If scores fall and slips rise, take one or two full days off; this is our suggestion, not a rule.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Doing more of the same questions; blaming "bad luck" without checking the log; changing the plan every day.

### Q17 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you practise explaining a reasoning solution aloud or in writing (to a partner, to a recording, or to yourself), and what do you check in your own explanation?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites for analytics firms advise explaining your reasoning aloud (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can communicate a deduction clearly, since oral puzzles are judged on the path as well as the answer.
**Say this in the interview:** "After solving, I explain it in 60 seconds: the answer first, then the clue I used first and why, then the check. I record it on my phone and listen for gaps."
**Answer:** **Use a fixed script (answer, first deduction, chain of steps, check), say it aloud or write it in 5 to 8 lines, then audit it against a short checklist.**
**Explain simply:**
- Script: 1) state the answer; 2) name the clue you started with and why it is the strongest; 3) give each step as "because ... therefore ..."; 4) say why the answer is the only one; 5) say how you checked.
- Where: to a partner or friend (ask them to stop you at any unclear step); to a phone recording; or to yourself, as a short written note in the error log.
- Audit checklist for your own explanation:
  - Does every step follow from a stated clue or an earlier step?
  - Did I state my assumptions (for example "some" means at least one)?
  - Did I use jargon without explaining it?
  - Could a listener rebuild the final arrangement from my words alone?
  - Did I say why alternatives are ruled out?
**Worked mini-example:** Puzzle: three people A, B, C stand in a row; A is left of B, C is not at the right end, A is not at the left end. Spoken: "Answer: C, A, B. Start with the clue about A: A is not at the left end and A is left of B, so A cannot be at the right end either, so A is in the middle and B is at the right. That leaves C at the left. Check: A left of B yes, C not at the right end yes, A not at the left end yes. It is the only order, because A had only one possible place." That runs about 25 seconds (verified by listing all 6 orders by script: only C, A, B passes).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you get stuck mid-explanation?* Say so, say what you have ruled out, and try the next clue; interviewers usually value visible method. 2) *How often?* Explain one solved question a day, and one recorded answer a week (our suggestion).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading your working out line by line without the reason for each step; explaining only the right path and hiding the dead ends; speaking so fast no one can follow.

### Q18 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Design a 12-question self-check diagnostic that samples every reasoning topic once or twice. How do you decide, from the result, which topics go into the first month of your plan, and what do you do when a topic scores zero?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can design a fair test and turn its result into a plan, with an honest view of how little 12 questions can tell you.
**Say this in the interview:** "Twelve questions, one or two per topic, untimed first so I see knowledge, then I rank topics by score. The lowest topics, weighted by how likely my target tests face them, go into month one."
**Answer:** **Use the layout below (9 topics, 12 questions), score each topic, put the 3 lowest into month one, and for a zero score restart that topic from the easiest level with worked examples first.**
**Explain simply:**
- Layout (the nine topics are the nine practice areas of this book, Parts 2 to 10):

| Topic (Part) | Questions |
|---|---|
| Syllogisms and conditional logic (2) | 2 |
| Inequalities and ranking (3) | 1 |
| Seating and arrangements (4) | 2 |
| Multi-attribute and truth-teller puzzles (5) | 1 |
| Blood relations, direction, coding (6) | 2 |
| Series and analogy (7) | 1 |
| Critical reasoning (8) | 1 |
| Data-analyst logic, SQL/Excel traps (9) | 1 |
| Quantitative puzzles (10) | 1 |

  That is 2+1+2+1+2+1+1+1+1 = 12. Each question should be medium difficulty, single answer, picked from Parts 2 to 10.
- Take it untimed first (knowledge), then note the time if you like. A topic with 2 questions scores 0, 1 or 2; a topic with 1 scores 0 or 1.
- Decision rule (our suggestion): rank topics by score; the 3 lowest go into month one; if your target round is known (Q19), break ties towards the topics that round uses.
- Be honest about the limit: one or two questions per topic is a rough signal, not a measurement. One careless miss changes a score. Re-test with different questions after month one.
- A zero: do not just drill harder. Go back to the topic's basic method (learn the convention, study 3 solved examples, then solve easy items untimed). If you still score zero after a week, ask whether the method or the notation is the gap.
**Worked mini-example:** Scores: seating 0 of 2, series 0 of 1, critical reasoning 0 of 1, blood relations 1 of 2, the rest full marks. Month one: seating, series, critical reasoning (the three lowest), with blood relations as a light fourth topic. Seating is a zero, so it starts with 3-person row puzzles before 5-person ones.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not just use all 540 practice questions as a test?* Too long and you lose the fresh-eyes questions you need for later re-tests. 2) *What if two topics tie?* Pick the one more relevant to your target round.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating 12 questions as precise; timing the test first and mixing "slow" with "does not know"; reusing the same 12 questions as the re-test.

### Q19 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How should a 4-week reasoning plan differ for a service-MNC online test, an analytics-firm puzzle round such as Mu Sigma or Fractal, and a data-analyst interview with an oral puzzle, given that the exact formats are only reported on prep sites?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Prep sites list aptitude and reasoning for service companies and puzzles, series, seating, coding-decoding and Fermi estimation for Mu Sigma; one prep source says the Mu Sigma aptitude round (muAPT) was dropped from some 2025 drives while others say it is still the first round (conflict, unverified). For Fractal we found only generic timed multiple-choice practice, nothing format-specific.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you tailor preparation to the likely format while admitting that formats are unconfirmed.
**Say this in the interview:** "I first check the current format with the recruiter or recent candidates, because prep sites disagree. Then I weight the 4 weeks: speed for online tests, variety and explanation for puzzle rounds, spoken reasoning for oral interviews."
**Answer:** **Same four phases (basics, mixed practice, timed work, mocks and review), but shift the weights: speed and accuracy for the online test, puzzle variety and written reasoning for the analytics-firm round, spoken explanation for the oral round. This split is our suggestion, built on unverified prep-site reports.**
**Explain simply:**
- Step zero for all three: confirm the format (time limit, negative marking, topics, whether a calculator or paper is allowed) from the recruiter, the invitation email or recent candidates. Treat prep-site counts and timings as reported, unverified.
- Service-MNC online test (typically timed multiple choice, reported): weeks 1 to 2 topic basics (Parts 2, 3, 4, 6, 7); week 3 timed sets and a skip rule; week 4 full mocks (Part 11) and error review. Practise reading carefully fast.
- Analytics-firm puzzle round (reported mix of puzzles, series, seating, estimation): weeks 1 to 2 broader puzzle types (Parts 4, 5, 10, 7); week 3 estimation-style and trick puzzles; week 4 mixed sets, writing out the reasoning because such rounds may judge the method.
- Data-analyst interview with an oral puzzle: weeks 1 to 2 core puzzles and critical reasoning (Parts 5, 8, 10); weeks 3 to 4 solve aloud daily (Q17 method), plus data-analyst logic (Part 9: NULLs, conditions). Accuracy matters but so does a calm, structured path and checking.
**Worked mini-example:** A 20-hour budget (5 hours a week, hand-added), split as a suggestion: online test, 12 hours on topic drills and timed sets, 8 on mocks and review. Oral round: 8 hours on solving, 8 hours explaining aloud, 4 hours on Part 9. The numbers show different shapes, not a proven ratio.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you have all three in one month?* Do the common core (Weeks 1 to 2), then spend week 3 on the earliest date and week 4 on the next. 2) *What if the format changes?* Fall back on the core methods, which any format uses.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Stating a company's format as fact from a prep site; over-practising speed for an oral round; ignoring negative marking in an online test.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether practice is purposeful and matched to the target test.
**Say this in the interview:** "I decide the target first, then pick puzzle types that look like it, set a time limit and solve to a goal, and I write down each method. If I am just having fun, I count it as a break, not practice."
**Answer:** **Match puzzle type to target, filter by difficulty, add a time limit and an error-log note, and verify answers; use fun puzzles as occasional extras.**
**Explain simply:**
- Match to target:
  - Logic-grid puzzles (a table you fill by elimination) build constraint reasoning that helps in multi-attribute puzzles (Part 5).   - Aptitude-style multiple choice matches online tests; this book's Parts 2 to 11 are the safest source because the answers here were checked.

### Q21 ⭐ 🟡 How can you use an AI assistant to practise reasoning (hints, checking your steps, making new variants) without letting it do the thinking, and how do you protect yourself from its wrong answers?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a newer differentiator question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you use AI as a coach, not a crutch, and whether you verify its output.
**Say this in the interview:** "I attempt the problem first, then ask for a hint, not the answer. I paste my steps and ask where the first mistake is. Anything it tells me I re-check against the clues myself, and for puzzles I test by brute force."
**Answer:** **Solve first, ask for graded hints, have it check your steps and generate variants, and verify every answer yourself (clue-by-clue or by a short brute-force check), because AI can state wrong answers confidently.**
**Explain simply:**
- Prompts that keep you thinking:
  - "Give me only a first hint, not the solution."
  - "Here are my steps; point out the first step that does not follow, without finishing it."
  - "Make a similar puzzle with different numbers or names, with a unique answer; do not show the solution."
  - "Ask me to explain why the answer is unique."
- Protect yourself:
  - Re-check every clue against the final arrangement; one failing clue means the answer is wrong.
  - For small puzzles, write 10 lines of code that tries every arrangement; a unique pass is stronger evidence than the AI's say-so.
  - Be extra careful with its generated variants: ask it for the answer, then verify that the puzzle has exactly one answer (Q9).
  - Compare two attempts; if they disagree, neither is trustworthy until you check.
- Rule of thumb (our suggestion): no AI until you have spent at least 10 minutes, or written down what you tried.
**Worked mini-example:** You get Q11's seating puzzle (from this part) wrong. You paste your reasoning and ask for the first faulty step only. It points to your use of "to the left of" as "immediately left". You fix it, solve again, then verify the answer by checking the four conditions yourself. If the AI had instead said "5 arrangements", you would check, not accept.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is it acceptable in an interview to say you use AI?* Yes if you describe the checking habit; the risk is dependence, so show you can solve unaided. 2) *Can AI mark your timed tests?* Better to mark with a key you verified.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Asking for the answer straight away; copying its explanation without redoing it; accepting a generated puzzle without checking uniqueness.

### Q23 ⭐ 🟡 After about 12 weeks, what does 'job-ready' reasoning look like in measurable terms, and how do you decide to stop adding reasoning practice and shift time to SQL, Excel and portfolio work?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you set measurable exit criteria and balance reasoning prep against the rest of the job search.
**Say this in the interview:** "I define job-ready before I start: a few clear numbers on fresh sets and mixed timed mocks. When I hit them on two checks in a row, I cut reasoning to a short maintenance slot and move the time to SQL, Excel and portfolio."
**Answer:** **Job-ready is a set of measurable thresholds you choose in advance (our suggestion below); once met twice in a row on fresh material, drop reasoning to a short maintenance slot and move the freed time to SQL, Excel and portfolio work.** These numbers are our suggestions, not research findings or promises of results.
**Explain simply:**
- Suggested thresholds (adjust to your target, confirmed format first):
  - Untimed fresh set of 20 across topics: at least 80% correct.
  - Timed mixed mock (Part 11): at least 70% accuracy at or under 90 seconds per question on average (the same target used in Q6).
  - New-variant test: change numbers or clues on questions you solved before and solve them correctly at least 7 of 10 times.
  - Explanation test: explain two solved puzzles aloud in under 2 minutes each, with answer, steps and check.
  - No topic below 50% on the Q18 diagnostic.
- Stop rule: all thresholds met on two checks, a week or more apart, using unseen questions. Then keep about 20 to 30 minutes, 2 or 3 times a week, as maintenance (our suggestion).
- The reason to stop: reasoning gates are only one part of the process, and analyst interviews also test SQL, Excel and portfolio evidence. Time spent beyond the targets has a smaller payoff than time on those.
- Exception: if a specific target round is very reasoning-heavy and you are below its bar, keep practising and say why.
**Worked mini-example:** Week 11 check: untimed 17 of 20 (85%), timed 15 of 20 correct in 28 minutes (75%, 84 seconds each), variants 8 of 10, explanations fine, lowest topic 60%. All met. Week 12 repeats on new questions and meets them again. Result: from week 13, reasoning drops to 25 minutes, three times a week, and daily SQL and portfolio time goes up.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you pass the thresholds but fail a real test?* Review that test's format and errors; your thresholds were a proxy and need updating. 2) *What if 12 weeks is too long?* Shorten with the same method; the 12 weeks is a planning assumption, not a norm.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Moving the goal posts forever; stopping after one lucky set; using questions you have already seen; dropping practice completely and losing speed.
