### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Day shift had 9 accidents over 30,000 worker-hours; night shift had 6 accidents over 6,000 worker-hours. A manager says the day shift is more dangerous because it had more accidents. Compare accident rates per 10,000 worker-hours and say how many times riskier the night shift is.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you compare rates (counts divided by exposure) instead of raw counts.
**Say this in the interview:** "Day had more accidents because it had five times the hours. Per 10,000 worker-hours, day is 3 and night is 10, so night is about 3.3 times riskier."
**Answer:** **Day 3.0 and night 10.0 accidents per 10,000 worker-hours; night is 10 / 3 = about 3.33 times riskier.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. "Exposure" means how much opportunity for accidents there was; here it is worker-hours.
2. Day: 9 / 30,000 = 0.0003 per hour, times 10,000 = 3.0.
3. Night: 6 / 6,000 = 0.001 per hour, times 10,000 = 10.0.
4. Ratio: 10.0 / 3.0 = 3.33.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Put both on the same base. 30,000 hours is 3 blocks of 10,000, so day = 9 / 3 = 3. 6,000 hours is 0.6 of a block, so night = 6 / 0.6 = 10.
**Check:** Verified by script (3.0, 10.0, ratio 3.33).
**Explain simply:**
- A count is only fair to compare when both groups had the same exposure.
- A rate divides the count by the exposure so unequal groups can be compared.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If night had 3 accidents instead?* 3 / 0.6 = 5 per 10,000, still 1.67 times day. 2) *What else would you ask?* Severity of the accidents and the type of work on each shift.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Comparing 9 versus 6 directly; or saying night is "3.33% riskier" when it is 3.33 times (233% higher).

### Q43 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mean salary is 8.0 LPA and the median is 6.2 LPA. A slide says "the typical employee earns 8 LPA". What is the best critique?
- (a) A few very high salaries pull the mean above what a typical employee earns, so the median is the better description of typical pay.
- (b) The median is always the wrong measure.
- (c) Salaries cannot be averaged.
- (d) 8.0 must be rounded to 8 LPA.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Knowing that the mean is pulled by extreme values and the median is the middle value.
**Say this in the interview:** "Mean above median means the pay is right-skewed: a few high earners drag the average up. Half of employees earn 6.2 or less, so 6.2 describes the typical person better. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. The median is the middle salary: half earn less, half earn more.
2. The mean is the total divided by headcount, so one very large salary lifts it.
3. Mean (8.0) well above median (6.2) signals a few high salaries, so "typical = 8" overstates what most people earn.
4. Why the others fail: (b) is too strong, since the median is often the right measure but not always; (c) is false, averages of salaries are routinely computed; (d) is irrelevant, 8.0 and 8 are the same number.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can the mean ever be the better choice?* Yes, for totals such as payroll cost. 2) *Which would you quote for the budget?* The mean, since mean times headcount equals total pay.

### Q44 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A survey was emailed to all 2,000 employees as optional. 50 replied and 80% of them were satisfied. A slide says "80% of our employees are satisfied". What is the best critique?
- (a) Respondents chose to reply, so they may not represent the 2,000 employees.
- (b) The survey should have been on paper.
- (c) Satisfaction cannot be measured as a percentage.
- (d) The result should have been shown as a fraction.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting non-response and self-selection bias, and the gap between a sample and the population.
**Say this in the interview:** "Only 50 of 2,000 replied, which is 2.5%, and they chose themselves. The 80% describes those 50, not the company. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Response rate: 50 / 2,000 = 2.5%. The other 97.5% are unknown.
2. 80% of 50 is 40 satisfied people; that is 2% of the workforce we actually know about.
3. Self-selection: people with strong feelings (very happy or very unhappy) reply more often, so the sample may lean either way.
4. Why the others fail: (b) the medium is not the problem, the choice to reply is; (c) false, proportions are a normal measure of satisfaction; (d) 40 / 50 and 80% are the same information.
**Check:** Bounds: if all 1,950 non-responders were unsatisfied, only 40 / 2,000 = 2% would be satisfied; if all were satisfied, 98%. The true value could be anywhere in that range.
**Explain simply:**
- "Selection bias" means the people in your data were not picked fairly.
- A small sample is a second concern, but self-selection is the bigger one here.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you fix it?* Random sample with follow-ups to non-responders. 2) *Would 1,000 replies solve it?* Not fully; bias from who replies remains.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Blaming only the small size; a large self-selected sample is still biased.

### Q45 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A bank measures satisfaction by phoning only customers who contacted support in the last month. What is the most likely bias in the resulting score?

3. The direction of bias (likely too low) is a reasoned guess, not something the data proves.

### Q46 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Of the employees who left last year, 70% had not taken any training, so not taking training makes employees leave." What is the main flaw?
- (a) It does not say what percentage of employees who stayed had not taken training, so there is nothing to compare 70% with.
- (b) It uses a percentage instead of a number.
- (c) It covers only one year.
- (d) It says employees left.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Noticing a missing comparison group (base rate).
**Say this in the interview:** "70% of leavers untrained means little unless I know how many stayers were untrained. If 70% of everyone is untrained, the leavers are just typical. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. The data covers only leavers; stayers are missing.
2. Suppose 70% of all employees are untrained. Then leavers at 70% untrained match the company as a whole, and training makes no visible difference.
3. Only if stayers are much less often untrained (say 40%) would training look linked to leaving.
4. Even then the argument claims "makes", which needs more than a comparison (see confounders).
5. Why the others fail: (b) percentages are fine and often better; (c) one year is a limit but not the main flaw; (d) it is just the topic.
**Check:** Example: 100 leavers (70 untrained) and 900 stayers (630 untrained, 70%): untrained rate among leavers equals that among stayers, so no link at all. Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- You need two rows: the group with the outcome and the group without, then compare.
- "70% of X are Y" is not "70% of Y are X".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would you ask for?* Attrition rate for trained versus untrained employees. 2) *Is that then causal?* Not yet; motivated people may both train and stay.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading "70% of leavers untrained" as "70% of untrained employees leave" (confusing the direction of the percentage).

### Q47 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Resignations rose by 20% over a year, yet the attrition rate (resignations divided by average headcount) fell. Which statement must be true?
- (a) Headcount grew by more than 20%.
- (b) Headcount stayed the same.
- (c) Headcount fell.
- (d) Headcount grew by exactly 20%.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Reasoning with ratios: a fraction falls only if its bottom grows faster than its top.
**Say this in the interview:** "Rate = resignations / headcount. The top grew 20%, the rate fell, so the bottom must have grown by more than 20%. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. New rate / old rate = 1.20 / (headcount growth factor).
2. For the rate to fall, this must be below 1, so the growth factor must exceed 1.20.
3. That is headcount growth of more than 20%.
4. Why the others fail: (b) same headcount would make the rate rise 20%; (c) fewer people would raise it even more; (d) exactly 20% growth leaves the rate unchanged.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Top up 20%, rate down: the bottom must have outrun the top.
**Check:** Verified by script: headcount factor 1.1 gives rate ratio 1.09 (rises), 1.2 gives 1.00 (flat), 1.3 gives 0.92 (falls). Example: 10 resignations on 100 people (10%) becomes 12 on 130 (9.2%).
**Explain simply:**
- Average headcount is the denominator, so a growing company can have more exits and a lower rate.
- Always ask: more than what base?
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Resignations up 20%, rate up 10%: headcount?* Factor 1.2 / 1.1 = 1.09, so headcount grew about 9%. 2) *Is a falling rate good news?* Not necessarily; more people may still have left.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (d), forgetting that an equal rise leaves the rate flat, not lower.

### Q48 ⭐⭐ 🟡 The average salary of a company rose by 8%, although no existing employee's salary changed. Which statement best explains this?
- (a) The mix of employees changed: highly paid people joined or low-paid people left.
- (b) Everyone received a hidden bonus.
- (c) The median salary must have fallen.
- (d) The number of employees must have fallen.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Understanding composition (mix) effects on an average.
**Say this in the interview:** "If nobody's pay changed, the average can only move because who is in the company changed. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Average = total pay / headcount. With every existing salary fixed, only the set of people can change.
2. Adding high earners, or losing low earners, raises the average.
3. Example: 9 people at 10 and 1 at 10 (average 10); replace a 10 with someone paid 100 for the tenth and the average becomes (9 x 10 + 100) / 10 = 19.
4. Why the others fail: (b) contradicts "no salary changed"; (c) the median need not fall (it may stay put or rise); (d) fewer employees does not by itself raise an average, it depends who left.
**Check:** Verified by script on the example (19.0 versus 10).
**Explain simply:**
- A "mix effect" is a change in an average caused by a change in the composition, not in the individuals.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you check?* Average salary of people present in both years. 2) *Same idea elsewhere?* Average customer spend rising because low spenders left.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Taking "average rose" to mean "people got raises".

### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Which argument has the same flawed structure as: "All fraudulent claims are filed late. This claim was filed late. So it is fraudulent."
- (a) All members of the audit team hold the certificate. Meera holds the certificate. So Meera is on the audit team.
- (b) All interns have laptops. Arjun is an intern. So Arjun has a laptop.
- (c) No manager is an intern. Raj is a manager. So Raj is not an intern.
- (d) Some analysts are auditors. Divya is an analyst. So Divya is an auditor.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising the fallacy of affirming the consequent and matching structures.
**Say this in the interview:** "The original says all F are L, this one is L, so it is F. That reverses the rule. Option (a) does exactly the same with certificate holders. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Original form: All F are L; x is L; therefore x is F. This is invalid because late claims could also be honest ones (L is bigger than F). It is called "affirming the consequent" (or the undistributed middle).
2. (a) has the form All A are C; Meera is C; so Meera is A. Same form, same flaw: other people may hold the certificate.
3. Why the others fail: (b) is a valid argument (All I are L; x is I; so x is L). (c) is also valid (no M is I, x is M, so x is not I). (d) is flawed, but differently: "some" gives no certainty about any particular analyst, so it is an unsupported leap from "some", not a reversal of an "all" rule.
**Check:** Verified by brute force over all small models: the form of the original and (a) has counterexamples (true premises, false conclusion), the forms of (b) and (c) have none, and (d) also has counterexamples but from a different cause (it starts from "some").
**Explain simply:**
- "All F are L" does not mean "all L are F". Reversing a statement gives you the converse, which is not guaranteed.
- To test an argument, try to imagine true premises with a false conclusion.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Valid fix of the original?* "This claim was NOT filed late, so it is not fraudulent" (contrapositive). 2) *Name the fallacy.* Affirming the consequent.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (d) because it also feels flawed; the question asks for the same structure.

### Q50 ⭐⭐ 🟡 To evaluate the claim "the new shift pattern reduced absenteeism", which piece of information is most useful?
- (a) Absenteeism over the same period in comparable teams that kept the old shift pattern.
- (b) How many employees like the new shift pattern.
- (c) The cost of drawing up the new roster.
- (d) The name of the manager who proposed the pattern.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Knowing that a comparison group over the same period is the key to a causal claim.
**Say this in the interview:** "I need a comparable group that did not change, measured over the same time. If their absenteeism fell too, the pattern did not cause it. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. A before-and-after drop could be due to season, a policy elsewhere, or chance.
2. A comparison (control) group over the same period shows what would have happened without the change.
3. If control teams stayed flat while the new-pattern teams fell, the claim gains strength; if both fell, it weakens.
4. Why the others fail: (b) liking is about opinion, not absenteeism; (c) cost does not bear on the effect; (d) who proposed it is irrelevant to whether it worked.
**Check:** Hand-checked: only (a) could change our belief in either direction.
**Explain simply:**
- "Counterfactual" means what would have happened without the change; a control group stands in for it.
- "Comparable" means similar in size, work and season.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Best design?* Random assignment of teams to patterns. 2) *Only before-and-after data?* Weak; add trend and seasonal data.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b); satisfaction is attractive but does not test absenteeism.

### Q52 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Attrition (%) by quarter: Q1 30, Q2 29, Q3 28, Q4 27. A pay hike took effect at the start of Q5, and attrition was 26 in Q5 and 25 in Q6. HR says the hike cut attrition by 1 point in Q5. What is the best reading of this data alone?
- (a) The hike reduced attrition by 1 point, since Q5 is 1 point below Q4.
- (b) Attrition had been falling 1 point per quarter before the hike, so the Q5 fall does not show any effect of the hike.
- (c) The hike had no effect and will have none in future.
- (d) The hike reduced attrition by 5 points, from 30 to 25.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Separating an existing trend from an intervention effect.
**Say this in the interview:** "Attrition was already dropping exactly 1 point each quarter. Q5 and Q6 follow the same line, so these numbers show no extra effect from the hike. Answer (b)."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Pre-hike changes: 30, 29, 28, 27 means -1 each quarter.
2. If the trend simply continued, Q5 would be 26 and Q6 25, exactly what we see.
3. So the observed values equal the trend forecast; the hike's measured effect is 0 points (relative to trend), though the data cannot prove it had none.
4. Why the others fail: (a) compares only to Q4 and ignores the trend; (c) is too strong, since "no visible effect" is not "no effect, ever"; (d) credits the hike with the earlier fall of Q1 to Q4.

- A "pre-trend" is the direction a metric was already moving before the change.
- Effect = actual minus what the trend predicted.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If Q5 had been 22?* A drop of 4 points below trend, a strong sign of effect (still not proof). 2) *What would add confidence?* A comparison unit without the hike.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (c); absence of evidence of an effect is not evidence of no effect.

### Q53 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Promotion data by tenure band: (Tenure under 3 years) trained 40 employees, 4 promoted; untrained 160 employees, 16 promoted. (Tenure 3 years or more) trained 160 employees, 48 promoted; untrained 40 employees, 12 promoted. Overall, trained employees are promoted at 26% and untrained at 14%. What does the data show?
- (a) Training raises promotion chances, as shown by 26% versus 14%.
- (b) Tenure is a confounder: within each tenure band trained and untrained employees are promoted at the same rate, so the overall gap comes from who gets trained.
- (c) Untrained employees are being denied promotions.
- (d) Promotion causes employees to take training.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); confounding is a named interview topic in a KDnuggets statistics-traps article (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Stratifying by a third variable to expose a confounder.
**Say this in the interview:** "Overall 26% versus 14%, but within each tenure band the rates are identical: 10% versus 10% and 30% versus 30%. Trained people are mostly long-tenure, and long-tenure people are promoted more. Answer (b)."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Overall: trained (4 + 48) / 200 = 26%; untrained (16 + 12) / 200 = 14%.

5. Why the others fail: (a) ignores the equal within-band rates; (c) no band shows a lower untrained rate; (d) the data has no timing, and equal rates within bands give no sign of any effect either way.

**Check:** Verified by script (10%, 10%, 30%, 30%, 26%, 14%).
**Explain simply:**
- A confounder is a third factor linked to both the "cause" and the outcome.
- Comparing within groups of equal tenure removes it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does this prove training is useless?* No; it shows no difference here, and tenure may be only part of the story. 2) *Is this Simpson's paradox?* Close cousin: here the gap vanishes rather than reverses.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Trusting the overall 26% versus 14%; it is real arithmetic but not a like-for-like comparison.

### Q55 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Hiring data: Job family 1: men 80 applied, 56 hired; women 20 applied, 16 hired. Job family 2: men 20 applied, 6 hired; women 80 applied, 40 hired. Overall, men were hired at 62% and women at 56%. Which statement is correct?
- (a) Men have a higher hiring rate overall and in both job families.
- (b) Men have a higher hiring rate overall, but women have a higher rate within each job family.
- (c) Women have a higher hiring rate overall and in both job families.
- (d) Men and women have equal hiring rates in each job family.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); Simpson's paradox is a named interview topic in a KDnuggets article (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising Simpson's paradox: the overall comparison reverses inside every subgroup.
**Say this in the interview:** "Overall men lead 62% to 56%. But family 1 is 70% men versus 80% women, and family 2 is 30% men versus 50% women. Women lead in both. Answer (b)."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Overall: men (56 + 6) / 100 = 62%; women (16 + 40) / 100 = 56%.
2. Family 1: men 56 / 80 = 70%; women 16 / 20 = 80%.
3. Family 2: men 6 / 20 = 30%; women 40 / 80 = 50%.
4. Women lead in both families yet trail overall because most men applied to family 1 (easy to get into, 72% hired in total) and most women to family 2 (harder, 50% hired).
5. Why the others fail: (a) false in both families; (c) false overall; (d) the rates are 70/80 and 30/50, not equal.
**Check:** Verified by script (0.70, 0.80, 0.30, 0.50, 0.62, 0.56).
**Explain simply:**
- Simpson's paradox: a trend in the whole data reverses when you split it into groups, because group sizes (the mix) differ.
- Which view to trust depends on the question; here the family-level view compares like with like.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would you report?* Both views, with the application mix explained. 2) *Does this prove no bias?* No; why women apply to the harder family is a separate question.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Quoting only the overall 62% versus 56% and concluding men are favoured.
