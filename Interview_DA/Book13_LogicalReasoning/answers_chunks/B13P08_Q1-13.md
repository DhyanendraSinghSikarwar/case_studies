### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you split an argument into premises, conclusion and assumption, and which signal words help you find the conclusion and the premises?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); statement-assumption-conclusion items appear in prep-site topic lists for Wipro, Infosys, TCS and Accenture (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you read an argument as a structure (reason, claim, hidden link) instead of as a blur of sentences.
**Say this in the interview:** "I first find the conclusion, the claim the author wants me to accept. Then I mark the premises, the stated reasons. The assumption is the unstated bridge that makes the premises enough for the conclusion."
**Answer:** **Conclusion = the point being argued; premises = the stated evidence; assumption = the unstated link between them.**
**Explain simply:**
- Conclusion signals: "so", "therefore", "thus", "hence", "it follows that", "should", "must", "clearly". Premise signals: "because", "since", "as", "given that", "for", "the reason is".
- Test for the conclusion: ask "what is the author trying to convince me of?" Everything else is support.
- An assumption never appears in the text. It is what must also be true for the premises to lead to the conclusion.
- Background and opposing views ("Some managers say...") are neither; they only set the scene.
**Worked mini-example:** "Our sales rose 10% after the new ad, so the ad works." Premise: sales rose 10% after the ad. Conclusion: the ad works. Assumption: nothing else (season, discount, festival) explains the rise.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can a conclusion come first?* Yes: "We should keep Saturday shifts, because Saturday orders are 22% of volume." The word "because" marks the premise. 2) *Can there be two conclusions?* Yes, an intermediate one (supported by premises, then used as a premise) and a main one; the main one is the final claim.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing the first sentence as the conclusion; treating an opposing view as the conclusion; mistaking a restated premise for an assumption.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the negation test for an assumption question, why does it work, and how would you apply it to the argument "Sales rose after the new logo was launched, so the logo works"?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the negation idea is described on GMAT prep sites (GMAT Club, unverified as an interview item).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can test a candidate assumption instead of just feeling it is right.
**Say this in the interview:** "I negate the option. If the argument collapses when the option is false, the option is a necessary assumption. If the argument survives, it is not."
**Answer:** **Negate the answer choice; if the conclusion falls apart, it was a necessary assumption.**
**Explain simply:**
- Why it works: a necessary assumption is something the argument cannot do without. If it were false, the premises could no longer support the conclusion. So its opposite should wreck the argument.
- Negating means making the statement false in the simplest way ("always" becomes "not always"), not turning it into an extreme opposite.
- This tests *necessary* assumptions. It does not tell you the option is enough on its own.
**Worked mini-example:** Candidate: "Nothing else that could raise sales (a festival, a discount) happened at the same time as the launch." Negation: "Something else did happen at the same time and raised sales." Now the rise may not be due to the logo, so the argument collapses. Therefore the candidate is an assumption. A weak candidate: "The logo is blue." Negated ("it is not blue") the argument is unaffected, so it is not an assumption.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does it work on strengthen questions?* No, only on assumption questions that ask for a necessary assumption. 2) *Candidate: "Sales were not already rising before the launch."* Negation: they were rising already, so the logo may add nothing; the argument is badly hurt, so it is an assumption.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Negating too strongly ("the logo made sales fall"); using the test on options that only add support.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In strengthen and weaken questions, what kinds of statements usually strengthen an argument and what kinds usually weaken it (name at least four patterns for each)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weaken questions are reported by Magoosh as the most common critical-reasoning type (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can aim at the weak point of an argument: the link between evidence and claim.
**Say this in the interview:** "A cause argument is strengthened by ruling out other causes and showing the effect follows the cause; it is weakened by offering another cause, reversing the direction, or showing the cause was absent when the effect happened."
**Answer:** **Strengthen: make the assumption more likely true. Weaken: make it less likely true, or show the evidence is unreliable.**
**Explain simply:**
- Strengthen patterns: (1) rule out an alternative cause; (2) show a dose effect (more of A, more of B); (3) show a comparison or control group without A does not show B; (4) show the sample is representative; (5) show the cause came before the effect, repeatedly, in several places.
- Weaken patterns: (1) offer an alternative cause; (2) reverse the direction (B causes A); (3) show it is a coincidence or tiny sample; (4) show the sample is biased (self-selected); (5) show A happened without B, or B happened without A; (6) show a side effect that defeats the plan.
- Do not use outside opinions; judge only against the conclusion's own link.
**Worked mini-example:** "Teams that use a dashboard hit targets, so the dashboard helps." Strengthens: teams hit targets only after getting it, and teams without it did not improve. Weakens: only already-strong teams were given the dashboard.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is "other firms report similar results" strong?* Usually only weakly relevant. 2) *Does a statement that merely agrees with the conclusion strengthen it?* No; it must support the link.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking an option that is true but irrelevant; picking an option that strengthens when asked to weaken (the opposite-direction trap).

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 When two metrics move together, what are the main alternative explanations besides "A causes B", and what is one HR example of each?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); correlation versus causation is a named topic in KDnuggets' list of statistical traps in interviews (article, unverified for any specific firm).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you reach for a confounder, reverse causation and chance before accepting a cause.
**Say this in the interview:** "Besides A causing B, it could be B causing A, a third factor driving both, selection of who is in each group, or just chance in a small sample."
**Answer:** **Reverse causation, a confounder (third factor), selection bias, and chance.**
**Explain simply:**
- Reverse causation: B causes A. HR: "Employees with high engagement scores attend more training." Perhaps engaged people sign up for training more, not training raising engagement.
- Confounder (a hidden third factor driving both): "Employees who use the gym take fewer sick days." Age or baseline health may drive both.
- Selection bias (the groups differ in who ends up in them): "Volunteers for the mentoring programme get promoted faster." Ambitious people volunteer.
- Chance: "Two of the five people trained left, so training causes exit." A tiny sample can show any pattern.
- Also possible: a mix effect (Simpson-type), where subgroup sizes change the overall figure.
**Worked mini-example:** Ice cream sales and heatstroke cases both rise; the hot weather drives both (confounder).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you test a causal claim?* A randomised experiment or a fair before-and-after with a comparison group. 2) *Does correlation mean no cause?* No, it only means the cause is not shown.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating "we cannot prove cause" as "there is no cause"; naming only one alternative.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Argument: "Weekend sales were 20% higher after the retailer launched its loyalty app, so the app boosted sales." Which statement, if true, weakens the argument most?
- (a) The app has a 4.5-star rating in the app store.
- (b) The weekend used for the comparison fell during a major festival, while the earlier weekends did not.
- (c) The app has been downloaded 10,000 times.
- (d) The retailer has 40 stores.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weaken items are a standard critical-reasoning type (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you spot an alternative cause for a before-and-after comparison.
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument assumes nothing else changed. A festival is another reason for higher sales, so (b) weakens it most."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Conclusion: the app boosted sales. Evidence: a 20% rise in the weekend after launch.
2. Hidden assumption: no other factor explains the rise.
3. (b) supplies exactly such a factor: a festival boosts sales by itself.
4. (a) and (c) say the app is liked or downloaded, which if anything supports it. (d) is irrelevant (store count tells us nothing about the cause).
**Check:** Negate the assumption ("something else changed"): (b) is that statement. Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- A weakener usually offers another cause, a different explanation for the same data.
- Before-and-after comparisons are fragile because time also changes things (season, festivals, prices).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would strengthen it?* The same rise in only the stores that had the app, but not in stores without it. 2) *Would "downloads were 10,000" help?* Only if sales rose more among downloaders.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (a) or (c), which are positive facts but do not address the link; choosing (d) because it sounds like scale.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A company has 500 employees; 60% work in hybrid mode. Of the hybrid employees, 30% are based in Hyderabad. Which statement must be true?
- (a) Exactly 90 employees are both hybrid and based in Hyderabad.
- (b) Exactly 150 employees are based in Hyderabad.
- (c) 30% of all employees are based in Hyderabad.
- (d) Hyderabad has the largest number of hybrid employees of any city.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); percentage-of-a-percentage inference items are common in prep-site reasoning sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate what the data fixes from what it leaves open.
**Say this in the interview:** "60% of 500 is 300 hybrid; 30% of 300 is 90. That is exactly what (a) says. The others claim things about non-hybrid staff or other cities that we are not told."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Hybrid employees = 0.60 x 500 = 300.
2. Hybrid and in Hyderabad = 0.30 x 300 = 90, so (a) is forced.
3. (b) and (c) talk about all Hyderabad employees; the 30% applies only to hybrid staff, and we know nothing about the 200 non-hybrid. Hyderabad total is at least 90, not exactly 150.
4. (d): other cities share the other 70% of 300 = 210 hybrid staff; one other city could hold all 210, so Hyderabad need not be largest.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 0.6 x 0.3 = 0.18; 18% of 500 = 90. Then reject any option that widens the group beyond "hybrid".
**Check:** Verified by script (300 and 90). Options (b)-(d) hand-checked by counter-example.
**Explain simply:**
- "Must be true" means true in every possible situation consistent with the facts.
- A percentage applies only to the group named in the sentence.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if 40% of the hybrid group were in Pune?* Then 120 hybrid in Pune, which would be more than Hyderabad's 90. 2) *What is the least number of Hyderabad employees?* 90.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Applying 30% to all 500 (150); assuming the biggest named city is the biggest overall.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Employees who use the office gym take fewer sick days. Which of the following is NOT a plausible alternative explanation to "the gym reduces sick days"?
- (a) Healthier employees are more likely to join the gym.
- (b) Younger employees both use the gym more and fall sick less.
- (c) Gym visits are recorded by a swipe card.
- (d) In a small sample the pattern could be due to chance.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic traces to correlation-versus-causation guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can tell a real alternative explanation from an irrelevant detail.
**Say this in the interview:** "(a) is reverse causation, (b) is a confounder, (d) is chance. (c) is just how data is recorded and explains nothing about why the pattern appears."
**Answer:** **(c)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. An alternative explanation must give another reason for the same pattern.
2. (a): health leads to gym use, not the other way (reverse causation).
3. (b): age drives both gym use and sickness (confounder).
4. (d): a small sample can produce a pattern by luck.
5. (c) describes a measurement method; it offers no other cause. (It could matter only if swipe-card gaps made records wrong, but as stated it does not explain the link.)
**Check:** Each option tested against "does it offer another cause or reason for the pattern?" Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- The standard rivals to "A causes B" are reverse cause, a third factor, selection and chance.
- A fact about how data is stored is not a rival cause.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Name the bias in (a).* Self-selection (healthy people choose the gym). 2) *How would you test the gym effect fairly?* Compare the same people before and after joining, or randomise access.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Spotting a fact that "sounds data-related" and calling it a rival cause.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Which of these four statements claim a cause, and which only describe an association? (i) Employees with longer commutes are more likely to resign. (ii) Longer commutes make employees resign. (iii) Resignations are higher among long-commute employees, and the two appear linked. (iv) Because of long commutes, resignations rise. Choose the set that makes a causal claim.
- (a) (i) and (iii)
- (b) (ii) and (iv)
- (c) (ii) only
- (d) (i), (ii) and (iv)
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the cause-versus-association distinction is a named interview topic in KDnuggets (article, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you notice causal wording ("make", "because of") versus descriptive wording.
**Say this in the interview:** "(ii) says 'make' and (iv) says 'because of'; both state a cause. (i) and (iii) only report that the two go together; 'appear linked' is still an association."
**Answer:** **(b) (ii) and (iv)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. (i): "more likely to" compares rates; no cause is claimed.
2. (ii): "make employees resign" asserts causation.
3. (iii): "appear linked" is hedged association.
4. (iv): "because of" asserts causation.
5. So the causal set is (ii) and (iv).
**Check:** Words scanned for causal verbs and connectors; hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- Causal words: cause, make, lead to, result in, because of, due to, drives. Association words: linked, associated with, more likely, goes with.
- Careful analysts report associations as associations.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Rewrite (ii) as an association.* "Long-commute employees resign more often." 2) *Is "is related to" causal?* No.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating (i) as causal because it sounds alarming; including (iii) because it says "linked".

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Attrition fell from 20% to 15%. One manager says it "fell by 5%" and another says it "fell by 25%". Who is correct, and what are the precise terms?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); percentage versus percentage-point is a standard data-literacy item (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate a percentage-point change from a percent (relative) change.
**Say this in the interview:** "Both are right if worded precisely: it fell 5 percentage points, which is a 25% relative decline."
**Answer:** **Both: a drop of 5 percentage points (absolute change) and a 25% fall (relative change).**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Absolute change = 15% - 20% = -5 percentage points.
2. Relative change = (15 - 20) / 20 = -0.25 = -25%.
3. The first manager's "5%" is loose; it should say "5 percentage points". The second is correct as a relative change.
**Check:** Verified by script (-5 points, -25%).
**Explain simply:**
- A percentage point is the gap between two percentages; a percent change is the gap divided by the starting value.
- Always say which one you mean, and give the base (20% to 15%).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If attrition then rises from 15% back to 20%?* +5 points but +33.3% relative (5/15). 2) *Why are they not symmetric?* The base differs.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying "fell by 5%" when you mean points; assuming a 25% fall and a 25% rise cancel out.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Region X orders grew by 300% (from 2 to 8 orders), while Region Y orders grew by 10% (from 5,000 to 5,500). Which region contributed more to the company's growth in orders, and by how many orders?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); small-base growth is a common data-analyst trap (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you compare absolute contributions and not only growth rates.
**Say this in the interview:** "X added 6 orders and Y added 500. Y contributed far more, by 494 orders. The 300% sounds big only because X started from a tiny base."
**Answer:** **Region Y contributed more: +500 orders versus +6 for X, a difference of 494 orders.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. X: 8 - 2 = +6 orders (+300%, from a base of 2).
2. Y: 5,500 - 5,000 = +500 orders (+10%).
3. Combined growth: 506 orders on a base of 5,002, about 10.1%; Y accounts for 500/506, about 98.8%.
**Check:** Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Growth rate = change / starting value. A tiny starting value makes a huge rate from a small change.
- Contribution to growth = the absolute change.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When is the rate more useful?* To judge momentum or potential, or when bases are similar. 2) *Is X's growth meaningless?* Not meaningless, but unreliable with 2 orders; ask for more months.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing X because 300% is larger; adding the percentages (310%).

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Passage: "Several managers want to drop Saturday shifts. Yet Saturday orders are 22% of weekly volume, and Saturday customer ratings are the highest of the week. Dropping Saturday shifts would therefore hurt revenue and satisfaction. So the company should keep them." Which is the main conclusion?
- (a) Saturday orders are 22% of weekly volume.
- (b) Dropping Saturday shifts would hurt revenue and satisfaction.
- (c) The company should keep Saturday shifts.
- (d) Several managers want to drop Saturday shifts.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); main-conclusion items appear in prep-site statement-conclusion topics (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate the final claim from premises and intermediate conclusions.
**Say this in the interview:** "(c) is the final claim, introduced by 'So' and carrying 'should'. (b) is an intermediate conclusion that supports it."
**Answer:** **(c)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. (d) is background, an opposing view the author argues against.
2. (a) and the ratings fact are premises (evidence).
3. (b), introduced by "therefore", follows from those premises, but it is then used to support the next claim.
4. (c), introduced by "So" and a "should", is where the chain ends: it is the main conclusion.
**Check:** Structure: premises, then (b), then (c). Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- An intermediate conclusion is a claim that is both supported by earlier points and used to support the final one.
- Recommendation words ("should") usually mark the main conclusion.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the passage ended at "therefore ... hurt revenue and satisfaction", what would the main conclusion be?* (b). 2) *Is "therefore" always the main conclusion?* No, check whether another claim follows.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b) because "therefore" is attached to it; choosing (d) because it comes first.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the difference between an assumption question, an inference ("must be true" or "most strongly supported") question and a conclusion question, and which trap words show which one you are facing?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); FACE Prep guides distinguish assumption, inference and conclusion items (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you read the question stem before the options, since each type points in a different direction.
**Say this in the interview:** "An assumption is hidden support behind the argument; an inference is something that follows from what is given; a conclusion question asks for the author's main claim. I read the stem first to know which one it is."
**Answer:** **Assumption looks backward (what must also be true); inference looks forward (what follows); conclusion asks what the author is claiming.**
**Explain simply:**
- Assumption stem words: "assumes", "depends on", "relies on", "necessary for the argument", "required". The correct option is unstated and links premise to conclusion.
- Inference stem words: "must be true", "can be inferred", "most strongly supported", "follows from". The answer is a modest statement that the passage guarantees; no new facts.
- Conclusion stem words: "main conclusion", "the passage is mainly arguing", "the author concludes". The answer is the claim that others support.
- Trap words in options: "all", "only", "never", "every", "completely" (too strong); outside facts or causes the passage never mentions (scope shift).
**Worked mini-example:** Passage: "Of 200 staff, 120 are in Pune." Inference: "More than half of the staff are in Pune" (must be true, 60%). Not inference: "Pune is the best city" (outside scope).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is "must be true" the same as "likely true"?* No; it needs certainty from the text. 2) *Does an assumption have to be stated in the argument?* No, that is why it is hidden.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing an attractive true-in-real-life statement instead of what the text supports; picking an option that restates the premise as an "assumption".

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Our new onboarding portal cut the time to complete joining formalities from 5 days to 2 days, so vacant positions will now be filled faster." Which assumption is necessary for the argument?
- (a) Joining formalities are counted as part of the time a position stays vacant.
- (b) Every other step of hiring will also become faster.
- (c) All new hires prefer a portal to paper forms.
- (d) The portal cost less than the paper process.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); assumption items are standard in critical-reasoning sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you find the missing link between a process saving and the final outcome.
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument jumps from faster formalities to faster filling of vacancies. That only works if formalities are part of the vacant period. I test (a) by negating it: if formalities happen after the position is already counted as filled, the saved days do not matter."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: formalities now take 2 days instead of 5. Conclusion: vacancies get filled faster.
2. Gap: do the formality days count towards how long a position is vacant?
3. Negate (a): formalities are not part of the vacant period (for example the seat counts as filled at offer acceptance). Then saving 3 days there changes nothing, and the conclusion fails. So (a) is necessary.
4. (b) is too strong: the argument needs only the formality stage to be faster, not every step.
5. (c) and (d) are about preference and cost, which do not link to speed of filling.
**Check:** Negation test applied to all four; only (a) breaks the argument. Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- A necessary assumption is the smallest missing link; it should not be stronger than the argument needs.
- Words like "every" and "all" in an option are warning signs.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What else could weaken it?* Other steps (such as interviews) get slower or the bottleneck is elsewhere. 2) *Would "formalities were the longest step" strengthen it?* Yes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because it sounds like it helps; picking (c)/(d), which are plausible side facts but not links.
