### Q28 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Chart claim: a bar chart's vertical axis starts at 90, Team A scores 95 and Team B scores 100. B's bar looks twice as tall as A's. What is the apparent height ratio and the true ratio of B to A?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); misleading-axis charts are a standard data-literacy check.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you notice a truncated axis (a baseline that is not zero) and can separate what the picture shows from what the numbers say.
**Say this in the interview:** "The axis starts at 90, so the bars only show the part above 90: 5 versus 10, which looks like 2 times. The real ratio is 100 divided by 95, about 1.05, a 5% gap."
**Answer:** **Apparent ratio 2 : 1 (bar heights 10 and 5 units); true ratio 100/95 ≈ 1.05, so B is only about 5.3% higher.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. A bar's drawn height = value minus the axis start. A: 95 - 90 = 5. B: 100 - 90 = 10.
2. Apparent ratio = 10 / 5 = 2.0.
3. True ratio uses the real values: 100 / 95 = 1.0526, a difference of 5.26%.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Subtract the axis start from both values to get the apparent ratio; use the raw values for the true ratio. A bar chart is only honest when its baseline is zero, because the eye compares lengths.
**Check:** verified by script (2.0, 1.0526).
**Explain simply:**
- Truncated axis: the vertical axis begins above zero, which cuts the bottom off every bar and exaggerates gaps.
- A bar encodes size by its length, so a cut baseline breaks that promise. (A line chart may reasonably start above zero because it shows change, not length.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the axis started at 0?* Bars 95 and 100 would look 1.05 times apart, matching the truth. 2) *What if the axis started at 94?* Heights 1 and 6, so it looks 6 times as tall (checked: 6/1).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reporting the apparent ratio as the answer; saying "B is twice A" because the picture says so; forgetting that the true gap may still matter (5% can be large in some settings), the point is only that the picture overstates it.

### Q29 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Monthly orders: January 100, February 98, March 50, April 70. A slide says "orders up 40% since March". What is the April change versus January, and which flaw does the slide use?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); cherry-picked baselines are a standard data-claim check.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you test a percentage against its starting point and ask why that starting point was chosen.
**Say this in the interview:** "The 40% is true but measured from the lowest month. Against January, April is down 30%. The slide cherry-picks the baseline."
**Answer:** **April is 30% below January (100 to 70). The slide uses a cherry-picked (convenient) baseline: March, the low point.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Slide's claim: (70 - 50) / 50 = 40%. Arithmetically correct.
2. April versus January: (70 - 100) / 100 = -30%.
3. April versus February: (70 - 98) / 98 = -28.6%.
4. March was an unusual dip (50), so any later month looks like a big recovery from it. The honest comparison starts before the dip.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** When you see "up X% since...", ask "since when, and why then?" Re-compute from the earliest and the typical period.
**Check:** verified by script (40.0, -30.0, -28.6).
**Explain simply:**
- Baseline: the starting value a percentage is measured from. A tiny or abnormally low baseline makes any change look huge.
- Cherry-picking: choosing the time window that tells the story you want, and hiding the rest.
- Also note recovery from a dip is not growth: orders are still below where they began.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How much must orders rise from April to get back to January?* 100/70 - 1 = 42.9%. 2) *Is the slide lying?* The number is true; it misleads by framing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Checking only the arithmetic and calling the slide correct; computing -30% as the reverse of +40% (percentages from different bases do not cancel).

### Q30 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the difference between a necessary and a sufficient assumption, and how can you tell which one a question wants?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); assumption questions appear in prep-site topic lists for Wipro, Infosys, TCS and Accenture (prep-site claims, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you read the question stem precisely and use the right test for each type.
**Say this in the interview:** "A necessary assumption is something the argument cannot do without; if it is false, the argument fails. A sufficient assumption is enough on its own to guarantee the conclusion. The wording of the question tells me which."
**Answer:** **Necessary = must be true for the argument to work (test: negate it, the argument breaks). Sufficient = if true, the conclusion definitely follows (test: add it, no gap is left).**
**Explain simply:**
- Wording clues. Necessary: "the argument depends on", "requires", "must be assumed", "assumes which of the following". Sufficient: "if assumed, would allow the conclusion to be properly drawn", "which would justify the conclusion".
- Necessary can be weak and modest ("not all the sample dropped out"). Sufficient is usually strong and complete.
- Every sufficient assumption that really closes the gap contains the necessary one, but a necessary one is often not enough alone.
**Worked mini-example:** Argument: "Sales rose after the new logo, so the logo works." Necessary: nothing else raised sales at the same time (negate it and the argument fails). Sufficient: "Sales rise only when the logo changes, and the logo changed." That alone guarantees the conclusion, but it is much stronger than the argument needs.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can one statement be both?* Yes, when it is exactly the missing link. 2) *Which is a typical answer to "depends on"?* A necessary one, so use negation.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking a very strong, extreme option for a necessary-assumption question (too strong is not required); picking a modest option for a sufficient question (does not close the gap).

### Q31 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What kind of evidence turns a correlation into a credible causal claim (control group, random assignment, timing, dose-response, same-period comparison)? Rank them and say why.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); correlation versus causation is a named topic in analytics-interview guides (for example KDnuggets, article).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know why some evidence rules out rival explanations better than others.
**Say this in the interview:** "Random assignment with a control group is the strongest, because it removes confounders by design. Then a same-period comparison group, then dose-response, and timing is the weakest because it is necessary but not enough."
**Answer:** **Our ranking, strongest to weakest: 1) random assignment with a control group; 2) a control group compared over the same period (not random); 3) dose-response; 4) timing (cause before effect).**
**Explain simply:**
- 1. Random assignment: chance decides who gets the treatment, so both groups are alike on average in everything, including things you cannot measure. This is the only one that handles unknown confounders.
- 2. Control group over the same period: handles time-related changes (season, economy) but not differences between the groups (who chose the treatment). Keeping the period identical prevents "the whole company improved" being credited to the policy.
- 3. Dose-response: more of the cause goes with more of the effect. It is supportive, but a confounder can also grow with the dose (for example, more motivated people do more training).
- 4. Timing: the cause must come first, otherwise it cannot be the cause. It is required, but many things change at the same moment.
- The ranking is our judgement of how many rival explanations each item removes; real studies combine them.
**Worked mini-example:** Retention bonus. Randomly give 10 of 20 teams the bonus, compare attrition over the same 6 months, and check that teams with larger bonuses lost fewer people. That stacks all four.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can you randomise people?* Often not; then use a matched control group and say the claim is weaker. 2) *Is "before and after" with no control enough?* No, it only shows timing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating timing as proof ("after this, therefore because of this"); treating dose-response as proof; forgetting the control group must be measured in the same period.

### Q32 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is Simpson's paradox, why can the overall rate point one way while every subgroup points the other way, and what do you do as an analyst when you see it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); Simpson's paradox is a named topic in analytics-interview guides (for example KDnuggets, DataCamp).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand weighting (mix of groups) and know to segment before concluding.
**Say this in the interview:** "Simpson's paradox is when a trend in every subgroup reverses in the combined data, because the groups are very different in size and the mix differs between the options. I break the data down by the hidden variable and decide which view answers the question."
**Answer:** **The overall rate is a weighted average of subgroup rates; when the two options have very different mixes of easy and hard groups, the weights can flip the overall result.**
**Worked mini-example (numbers made up, checked by script):** Two training programs, success means passing the certification.

| Group | Program X | Program Y |
|---|---|---|
| Easy cases | 9 of 10 = 90% | 70 of 90 = 77.8% |
| Hard cases | 30 of 90 = 33.3% | 2 of 10 = 20% |
| Overall | 39 of 100 = **39%** | 72 of 100 = **72%** |

X wins in both groups, yet Y wins overall. Why: Y was given mostly easy cases (90 of 100) and X mostly hard ones (90 of 100). Overall rate = sum of (share of group x group rate), and the shares differ.
**Explain simply:**
- The hidden variable (here, case difficulty) is a confounder: it affects the outcome and is linked to who got which option.
- What to do: (1) segment by the suspected variable; (2) ask how people ended up in each group (assignment); (3) if the groups are comparable within segments, trust the segment view and report both; (4) compare like with like, or standardise to the same mix; (5) never publish only the overall number.
- Which view is right depends on the causal story, not on arithmetic. Here, difficulty is not caused by the program, so the segment view is right.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is the overall number ever right?* Yes, when the mix itself is the thing you care about or is caused by the option. 2) *How could you avoid it?* Randomise assignment so mixes match.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking the data is wrong (the sums are correct); always trusting subgroups or always trusting totals; ignoring group sizes.

### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is survivorship bias, and how could it mislead an analysis of employee engagement or training outcomes?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); selection bias is a named topic in analytics-interview guides (for example KDnuggets, article).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you ask who is missing from the data, not just what the data says.
**Say this in the interview:** "Survivorship bias is judging by the ones who stayed in the data and ignoring those who dropped out. An engagement survey of current employees misses the unhappy people who already left, so scores look better than reality."
**Answer:** **Drawing conclusions only from the survivors, so the missing cases (leavers, dropouts, failures) are never counted and results look too good.**
**Explain simply:**
- Engagement example: a survey sent in December covers only people still employed. If disengaged staff quit in the summer, the average engagement of survivors looks high even if the culture is driving people away.
- Training example: "Employees who completed the program score 20% higher in reviews." Dropouts (who may have struggled or left) are excluded, so the program gets credit for filtering out the weak.
- Attrition analysis: studying only long-tenured people to find "what makes people successful" copies traits that may be shared by those who failed too.
**Worked mini-example:** 100 people start a program, 40 drop out, 60 finish with an average rating of 4.2. If the dropouts would have rated 2.5, the full-group average is (60 x 4.2 + 40 x 2.5) / 100 = 3.52. The completers-only figure overstates by about 0.7 points (hand-computed).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you check for it?* Compare starters versus finishers, and look at exit data and dropout reasons. 2) *Fix?* Include everyone who started (intention-to-treat style) and track leavers.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calling it "small sample" (it is about who is missing, not how many); forgetting the HR version: exit-interview or survey non-response is also a missing-people problem.

### Q34 ⭐⭐ 🟡 List six questions you would ask before accepting a claim shown on a dashboard or slide (for example "attrition fell 5% after the new policy").
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); analysts are judged on structured thinking and stated assumptions in case-study guides (Upenn career-services blog, Jan 2026).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a repeatable checklist for scrutinising a number.
**Say this in the interview:** "I ask: what exactly is measured, compared with what, over what period, how big is the group, what else changed, and who is left out."
**Answer:** **Six questions (our suggested checklist):**
1. **Definition:** how is attrition defined (voluntary only? annualised? which employees)? Did the definition change?
2. **Baseline and comparison:** "fell 5%" from what to what, and compared with when? Is it 5 percentage points or 5% relative (for example 20% to 19% is 1 point but 5% relative)?
3. **Time window:** is it the same period (season, appraisal cycle)? Was a particular month chosen to look good?
4. **Size and noise:** how many people? A fall from 10 leavers to 9 can be chance. Is there a range or test?
5. **Other causes:** what else changed (hiring freeze, market, new manager)? Is there a comparison group without the policy?
6. **Who is missing or mixed:** survivors only, a changed headcount mix, subgroups hiding a reversed trend?
**Explain simply:**
- Percent versus percentage points: 20% to 19% is a drop of 1 percentage point and 5% in relative terms.
- A dashboard shows a number, never why it moved; the questions look for rival explanations.
**Worked mini-example:** 200 people, 20 leavers (10%) last year; 190 left in headcount after the cut, 19 leavers (10%) this year. The number of leavers fell 5% but the rate did not move.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which one first?* Definition and baseline, because errors there make the rest meaningless. 2) *What would convince you?* A comparison group over the same period and enough people.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Questioning only the arithmetic; accepting a count when the rate is the right measure; ignoring that the policy and another event may overlap.

### Q35 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Plan: "To shorten attendance-system queues, the firm will install two more biometric machines at the gate." Which statement, if true, weakens the plan most?
- (a) The queue delay is caused mainly by the central server taking long to verify each record, not by the number of machines.
- (b) The machines cost Rs 30,000 each.
- (c) Most employees arrive between 9:00 and 9:30.
- (d) The gate is wide enough for two more machines.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weaken questions are reported as the most common critical-reasoning type on prep sites (Magoosh, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you attack the assumption that the machine count is the bottleneck.
**Say this in the interview:** "The plan assumes more machines means shorter queues. If the real bottleneck is the server, extra machines will not help, so (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Plan: more machines, so shorter queues. Hidden assumption: machines are the limiting step.
2. (a) says the limit is server verification. Extra machines feed the same slow server, so the plan fails at its goal. Directly attacks the assumption.
3. Eliminate the rest (below).
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Cost is a side issue; it does not say the plan will not shorten queues (scope shift: cost versus effectiveness).
- (c) A peak at 9:00 to 9:30 explains why queues form, and if anything supports adding capacity for the peak.
- (d) Confirms the plan is feasible; it strengthens, not weakens.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item; one best answer by design).
**Explain simply:**
- Weaken the plan by showing it does not reach the goal (it addresses the wrong cause).
- Cost or space arguments weaken whether the plan is worth it, not whether it works.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which statement would strengthen it?* "Each machine handles one person at a time and the server is idle most of the day." 2) *What would you measure?* Time per verification step.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b) because it sounds like a real-world objection; the question asks about the plan's effect on queues.

### Q36 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "The average customer-satisfaction score rose from 3.9 to 4.2 after a new support lead joined." Which statement, if true, weakens the claim that the new lead improved satisfaction most?
- (a) During the same period the company stopped surveying customers who had raised complaints.
- (b) The new lead has ten years of experience.
- (c) The score is on a scale of 1 to 5.
- (d) Support calls are recorded.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); biased-sample and alternative-cause weakeners are standard critical-reasoning patterns.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you spot a measurement change that explains the rise without any real improvement.
**Say this in the interview:** "If unhappy customers stopped being surveyed, the average would rise even if service did not change. So (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Claim: score rose, so the lead improved satisfaction. Assumption: the score fairly measures satisfaction before and after.
2. (a) changes who is measured: removing the likely low scorers raises the average mechanically. This is a biased sample and an alternative explanation at once.
3. The others do not touch the link (below).
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Experience makes improvement more plausible, so it strengthens.
- (c) Describes the scale; 4.2 out of 5 is still higher than 3.9. Irrelevant.
- (d) Recording calls says nothing about the scores' trend; irrelevant.

- A changed survey method (who is asked, how) can move an average on its own.
- This is the survivorship idea from Q33 in a satisfaction setting.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Another good weakener?* "Product defects fell sharply during the same months." 2) *What data would you request?* Scores for the same customer mix before and after.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because it feels "real" (it strengthens); ignoring that the argument is about the average, which depends on who responds.

### Q37 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "The 4-day week succeeded at our Pune office, so it will succeed at all our offices." Which statement, if true, weakens the argument most?
- (a) The Pune office does project-based work, while all other offices run fixed-shift customer support with queues.
- (b) The Pune office has 120 employees.
- (c) The Pune office opened in 2019.
- (d) The 4-day week was announced in a town hall.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); generalising from one case is a standard flaw in critical-reasoning practice.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you test if the sample (one office) represents the target (all offices).
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument assumes Pune is like the other offices. (a) says it is different in exactly the way that matters for a 4-day week, so (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: it worked in Pune. Conclusion: it will work everywhere. Assumption: Pune is representative.
2. (a) shows a relevant difference: a fixed-shift service needs people present on set days, so cutting a day is harder than for project work. Pune's success may not transfer.
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Headcount alone gives no reason for a difference; no stated link to success.
- (c) Opening year is not shown to matter.
- (d) How it was announced is unrelated to whether it works operationally.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "People promoted from within are more committed than external hires, so the company should prefer internal promotions." Which statement, if true, strengthens the argument most?
- (a) The commitment gap remains when internal and external hires of the same grade and age are compared.
- (b) Internal promotions take less time to process.
- (c) External hires ask for higher salaries.
- (d) Internal candidates are known to the managers.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ruling out a confounder is a standard strengthening pattern.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you recognise that the best strengthener removes a rival explanation of the commitment gap.
**Say this in the interview:** "Committed internal people might just be older or in senior grades. If the gap persists after matching grade and age, that rival explanation is gone, so (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: internals are more committed. Conclusion: prefer internals. Assumption: the gap is due to being internal and not to other differences.
2. (a) compares like with like and the gap survives, which removes the confounders grade and age.
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Speed is a different benefit; it does not show commitment. Scope shift.
- (c) Salary difference is a cost point, it says nothing about commitment.
- (d) Familiarity helps selection but does not show that internal people are more committed.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item).
**Explain simply:**
- Matching (comparing groups equal on other traits) is a way to strip out confounders.
- Strengthening: make the link more believable, not add any pro-internal fact.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would weaken it?* "Internals are mostly older staff who were committed before promotion." 2) *Also needed?* Commitment may cause promotion (reverse direction).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking any option that favours internal hiring; the question is about the commitment claim, not general benefits.

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Label each situation as reverse causation, confounding or chance: (i) Cities with more police officers have more recorded crime. (ii) Among school children, shoe size correlates with reading score. (iii) Out of 400 unrelated metrics, one shows r = 0.6 with attrition over 8 months. Which is the correct labelling in order (i), (ii), (iii)?
- (a) Reverse causation, confounding, chance
- (b) Confounding, reverse causation, chance
- (c) Chance, confounding, reverse causation
- (d) Reverse causation, chance, confounding
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the three explanations of a correlation are standard in analytics-interview guides (for example DataCamp, tutorial).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you test each correlation with the three rival explanations.
**Say this in the interview:** "Police numbers rise because crime is high: reverse causation. Shoe size and reading both depend on age: confounding. One hit among 400 tests is expected by luck: chance. So (a)."
**Answer:** **(a) Reverse causation, confounding, chance**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. (i) Crime may cause cities to hire police (the effect drives the "cause"): reverse causation.
2. (ii) Older children have bigger feet and read better; age drives both: confounding (age is the confounder, a third variable behind both).
3. (iii) With 8 monthly points, r = 0.6 gives a two-sided p of about 0.116 (t-test for correlation, 6 degrees of freedom). Across 400 unrelated metrics you expect roughly 46 to pass that bar by luck, so one such hit means nothing: chance.
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) swaps (i) and (ii): shoe size does not cause reading, and reading does not change foot size, so no reverse direction; and age explains (ii) better than police explain (i).
- (c) calls police numbers chance; the link there has an obvious direction story.
- (d) calls shoe size chance; the age link is systematic across children, not luck.
**Check:** p-value and expected count verified by script (p = 0.116, expected 46.3); the labels are judgement.
**Explain simply:**
- Reverse causation: B causes A, not A causes B.
- Confounding: a third factor drives both. Chance: multiple tests produce flukes ("multiple comparisons").
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Fix for (iii)?* Pre-register one hypothesis, correct for multiple testing, confirm on new data. 2) *Fix for (ii)?* Compare children of the same age.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calling every correlation "confounding"; forgetting that with few data points and many metrics large correlations are common.

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Which study design best tests whether a new retention bonus reduces attrition?
- (a) Compare this year's company-wide attrition with last year's.
- (b) Compare employees who applied for the bonus with those who did not.
- (c) Randomly assign teams to get the bonus or not, and compare attrition over the same period.
- (d) Ask managers whether they think the bonus worked.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); experiment design is a standard analytics-interview topic.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you choose the design that removes confounders, self-selection and time effects.
**Say this in the interview:** "Random assignment with a control group measured over the same period isolates the bonus. So (c)."
**Answer:** **(c)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Goal: causal effect of the bonus on attrition.
2. (c) uses chance to form groups (equal on average in motivation, market exposure, anything unmeasured) and a same-period comparison (cancels season and market).
**Why the others fail:**
- (a) Before-and-after with no control: anything that changed in the year (job market, layoffs) is mixed in with the bonus.
- (b) Self-selection: people who apply for a bonus differ (for example, they already planned to stay), so the groups differ before the bonus.
- (d) Opinions are not outcomes; managers have biases and cannot see the counterfactual.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item; ranking as in Q31).
**Explain simply:**
- Randomisation breaks the link between who gets the bonus and any other trait.
- Assign whole teams so people in the same team cannot share the bonus news across groups.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if randomising is not allowed?* Use a matched comparison group over the same period and state the weaker claim. 2) *What would you measure?* Leavers over total headcount per team, after a fixed period.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (a) because it uses real company data; choosing (b) because it has a comparison group (the groups are self-selected).

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Team A has 10 employees with an average tenure of 6 years; Team B has 30 employees with an average tenure of 4 years. A manager says the combined average is 5 years. What is the correct combined average, and what error did the manager make?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weighted averages are a standard data-analyst check.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you weight averages by group size.
**Say this in the interview:** "I add total years: 10 x 6 + 30 x 4 = 180, divided by 40 people gives 4.5. The manager averaged the two averages without weighting by team size."
**Answer:** **4.5 years. The manager took the simple average of 6 and 4 (= 5) and ignored that Team B has three times as many people.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Total years in A: 10 x 6 = 60. In B: 30 x 4 = 120.
2. Total = 180 years over 40 employees: 180 / 40 = 4.5.
3. Equivalent weights: A is 25% of staff, B is 75%: 0.25 x 6 + 0.75 x 4 = 4.5.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** The answer must sit nearer the bigger group's average. Three times as many in B means 3 parts to 1: the combined value is 1/4 of the way from 4 to 6, so 4.5.
**Check:** verified by script (4.5).
**Explain simply:**
- Average of averages is right only when the groups are the same size.
- Weighted average: multiply each group's average by its size (or share), add, divide by the total size.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Teams are 20 and 20?* Then 5.0, the simple average is right (20 x 6 + 20 x 4 = 200, 200/40). 2) *Team B grows to 60?* (60 + 240) / 70 = 4.29 (hand-computed).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Simply averaging the averages; also the same error causes Simpson-type surprises when group sizes differ (Q32).
