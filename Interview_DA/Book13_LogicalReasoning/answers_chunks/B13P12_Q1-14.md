### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How did you build your logical reasoning skills, and how long did it take you?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); learning-journey questions are standard in skill-development rounds.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a real, honest practice method and can describe it without exaggeration.
**Say this in the interview:** "I used a template like this, filled with my own real facts: what I practised, how often, how I reviewed mistakes, and what changed in my work." Never copy the sample numbers below; use only what you actually did.
**Answer:** **This is a personal-experience question, so the answer below is a template. Fill every blank with your own true details; do not use the sample as your story.**
**Template (fill in your own facts):**
1. Starting point: "I had not practised reasoning since school. I began with a short diagnostic of [N] questions and scored [X]."
2. Method: "I practised [topics] for [minutes] on [days], kept an error log, and re-solved wrong questions after [a few days]."
3. Evidence: "My accuracy moved from [A] to [B] and my average time per question from [C] to [D]; I measured this on [which sets]."
4. Duration, honestly: "It took me about [your real number] weeks of regular practice, and I kept going on weaker topics afterwards." Say what you actually did, not a round number that sounds good.
5. Link to the job: "I now use the same habits (state the claim, list assumptions, test edge cases) when I read HR data."
**Explain simply:**
- Interviewers cannot check your exact hours, but they can hear whether details are consistent and specific.
- "How long" has no standard answer; any timeline in prep articles is anecdotal, not research.
- If you have not practised much yet, say that and describe the plan you are following (see Q3 and Q4).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What was hardest?* Name one real topic and what changed your method. 2) *What would you do differently?* Name one real change, for example starting the error log earlier.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Inventing a dramatic score jump; claiming a fixed number of weeks you did not follow; saying "I am naturally logical" with no evidence.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Give a zero-to-job-ready roadmap for logical reasoning for someone who has not practised since school: the phases, what you do in each, and the exit check that tells you to move to the next phase.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). The shape (basics first, speed later, weak topics, mocks last) matches several prep blogs, which sometimes sell mock tests (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you can plan learning in stages with measurable checkpoints.
**Say this in the interview:** "Four phases: diagnose, learn methods untimed, add speed, then mixed mocks. I move on only when an exit check is met, not when a calendar date arrives."
**Answer:** **A 4-phase roadmap (our suggestion; durations are a guide, not measured). Weeks are about 5 to 6 sessions each.**
| Phase | What you do | Exit check to move on |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Diagnose (week 1) | Take a 12-question mixed diagnostic (see Q18); start the error log | Every topic scored once; weakest 3 topics named |
| 2. Methods, untimed (weeks 2 to 5) | Learn one topic at a time in the order of Q7; solve untimed, write the method in your own words | In each topic: 8 of 10 correct untimed, and a new-variant question solved (Q8) |
| 3. Speed (weeks 6 to 9) | Timed sets of 10 to 20 on weak topics; review every error | At least 70% accuracy and about 90 seconds per question or better on two sets in a row (the Q6 targets; adjust to your test) |
| 4. Mixed mocks (weeks 10 to 12) | Full mixed sets from Part 11, plus oral explanation practice (Q17) | Two mocks at target, and you can explain any solved item aloud in under 2 minutes |
**Explain simply:**
- An "exit check" is a pass mark you set in advance, so you do not move on because you feel ready.
- Phase 2 is untimed on purpose: speed built on a wrong method just makes mistakes faster.
- After phase 4, shift time to SQL, Excel and portfolio work (Q23).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Only 4 weeks?* Merge phases 1 and 2 for the top 3 topics and keep one mock (Q12, Q19). 2) *What if you fail an exit check?* Stay in the phase and fix the error-log pattern (Q16).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Starting with timed mocks; skipping the diagnostic; treating the 12 weeks as a promise of results.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What does a daily reasoning practice plan look like for someone with 30 minutes on weekdays and 60 minutes on weekends: what goes into each slot (warm-up, new problems, review, error log)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). One prep blog advises scheduling different question types on different days (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A realistic, structured routine rather than "I practise whenever I can".
**Say this in the interview:** "Weekdays: 5 minutes warm-up, 15 on new problems, 7 on review, 3 on the error log. Weekends: a timed set plus a longer review."
**Answer:** **Weekday 30 minutes = 5 + 15 + 7 + 3. Weekend 60 minutes = 5 + 25 + 20 + 10. Weekly total = 5 x 30 + 2 x 60 = 270 minutes (checked by script).** This is our suggestion, not a researched schedule.
| Slot | Weekday (30 min) | Weekend (60 min) |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 5: two quick items from a topic you already know | 5: same |
| New problems | 15: 3 to 5 questions on this week's topic, untimed | 25: one timed set of 10 on a mix of topics |
| Review | 7: re-solve one old wrong question without looking at the answer | 20: review every wrong or guessed item in the set |
| Error log | 3: log each mistake (see Q10) | 10: log, then pick next week's topic from the summary |
**Explain simply:**
- Warm-up keeps old methods fresh. Review is where most of the learning happens, so it is not squeezed out.
- A "guessed but correct" item goes in the log too; it is a weak spot that looks like a pass.
- Rotate topics by day (for example Monday and Thursday seating, Tuesday syllogisms) instead of doing one topic for a week.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *You miss two days?* Do not double up; resume the plan and keep the weekend set. 2) *Only 20 minutes?* Drop warm-up to 2 and new problems to 10.
**Common mistakes / traps:** All time on new questions with no review; never logging errors; long sessions once a week instead of short regular ones.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Over four weeks you solved a 20-question set each week: Week 1, 11 correct in 36 minutes; Week 2, 13 correct in 32 minutes; Week 3, 15 correct in 30 minutes; Week 4, 16 correct in 26 minutes. Your target is at least 70% accuracy and at most 90 seconds per question on average. In which week did you first meet both targets, and which target was the harder one to hit?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Turning raw scores into rates and comparing against a target.
**Say this in the interview:** "I convert each week into accuracy and seconds per question. Week 3 is 75% and exactly 90 seconds, so both targets were first met in week 3, with speed only just."
**Answer:** **Week 3. Speed was the tighter target (met with zero margin); accuracy cleared its target by 5 points.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Accuracy = correct / 20 x 100. Seconds per question = minutes x 60 / 20.
2. Week 1: 55%, 108 s. Week 2: 65%, 96 s. Week 3: 75%, 90 s. Week 4: 80%, 78 s.
3. Accuracy target (70% or more) first met in Week 3 (65% to 75%).
4. Speed target (90 s or less) first met in Week 3 (96 s to exactly 90 s; "at most 90" includes 90).
5. So both are met first in Week 3 and stay met in Week 4.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** For speed, test the target directly: 90 s x 20 questions = 30 minutes. Any set finished in 30 minutes or less meets the speed target; 70% of 20 = 14 correct or more meets the accuracy target. Week 3 (15 in 30) is the first to pass both.
**Check:** Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Both targets arrived in the same week, so "harder" needs a measure. Starting gaps: accuracy was 15 points short (55 vs 70), speed was 18 seconds over (108 vs 90, 20% slower than target). Speed started proportionally further away and landed on the limit exactly, so it was the harder one (our judgement).
- If the target were "under 90 seconds", Week 3 would fail on speed and Week 4 would be the first to meet both.
**Likely follow-ups:** *What if the target were 80% accuracy?* Then only Week 4 (80%) meets accuracy, and Week 4 would be the first week meeting both.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Comparing minutes with seconds; reading "at most 90" as "below 90"; looking at only the correct count instead of the rate.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 In what order would you learn the reasoning topics (syllogisms and conditional logic, inequalities and ranking, seating, multi-attribute puzzles, blood relations and coding, series and analogy, critical reasoning, data-analyst logic, quantitative puzzles), and why that order?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you build from simple to complex and link topics to the job.
**Say this in the interview:** "I start with the topics that teach basic deduction, move to arrangement puzzles, then topics that are mostly pattern work, and put the job-specific ones early because they matter most for a data analyst."
**Answer:** **Our suggested order (a judgement, not research):** 1) syllogisms and conditional logic, 2) inequalities and ranking, 3) data-analyst logic, 4) seating, 5) blood relations and coding, 6) series and analogy, 7) critical reasoning, 8) multi-attribute puzzles, 9) quantitative puzzles.
**Explain simply:**
- Step 1 to 2: "if-then" rules and ordering are the building blocks every later puzzle uses.
- Step 3 early: SQL and Excel conditions (AND, OR, NOT, blank values) use the same logic and pay off in the actual job, so it is not left until last.
- Step 4 to 6: arrangement and rule-spotting puzzles train diagramming and pattern checking, using the same deduction habits.
- Step 7: critical reasoning (assumptions, cause vs correlation) needs the conditional logic from step 1 and is key in analyst interviews.
- Step 8 to 9: multi-attribute grids and quantitative puzzles are the heaviest, so they come once methods and speed exist.
**Example:** Before a 5-person, 3-attribute grid puzzle, you should already handle a 4-person seating row without error.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Test is in 2 weeks?* Reorder by what your target test lists (Q19). 2) *Can you swap steps 5 and 6?* Yes; they are independent.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you test yourself to know that you have really learned a reasoning topic and not just remembered the solved examples? Describe a self-check test with an untimed part, a timed part and a new-variant part, with a pass mark for each.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you separate understanding from memory and set measurable pass marks.
**Say this in the interview:** "I test three ways: untimed for correctness, timed for speed, and a changed version of a solved question to prove I understand the method, not the answer."
**Answer:** **A 3-part test per topic. Pass marks are our suggestion; set your own before you start.**
| Part | Content | Pass mark |
|---|---|---|
| A. Untimed | 10 new questions, no hints | 8 of 10 correct |
| B. Timed | 10 new questions at about 90 seconds each (15 minutes) | 7 of 10 correct within time |
| C. New variant | 3 questions made by changing a solved one (change a clue, a number or a name) | 3 of 3 solved, with the changed answer explained |
**Explain simply:**
- Memory trap: if you only remember "the answer was C", changing one clue breaks you. A variant shows whether you have the method.
- "New" means not from the sets you already studied. Use a different part of the book or a friend's set (Q9).
- If A passes and B fails, the problem is speed, not understanding; if A fails, go back to the method.
**Example:** Solved: "A sits left of B". Variant: "A sits two places left of B". If you redo the deduction rather than recall, you pass part C.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you pass A and C but not B?* Do short timed sets on the same topic; see Q16. 2) *How often?* After finishing each topic, then again after a week to test retention.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Re-using solved questions as the test; setting the pass mark after seeing the score; counting lucky guesses as passes.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you build your own practice set of 10 questions on a topic, and how do you make sure every question has one correct answer and no hidden assumption?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Deeper understanding of how puzzles are constructed, and quality control.
**Say this in the interview:** "I write each question from a known answer, state every convention in the text, and then brute-force all possibilities to confirm there is exactly one valid answer."
**Answer:** **Write a question, then prove it has exactly one answer by checking every possibility. If more than one (or none) fits, fix the wording.**
**Steps (our suggestion):**
1. Choose a topic and mix difficulty: 4 easy, 4 medium, 2 hard.
2. Start from a hidden answer (for example the order B, C, A, D), then write clues it satisfies.
3. State conventions in the text: "some means at least one", "a row has positions 1 to 4 left to right", "no two people share a place".
4. List every possible arrangement or case (4 people give 24 orders) and cross out those breaking a clue. Exactly one should survive. A spreadsheet or a few lines of code can do this (see Q11).
5. Have a friend solve it cold and say where they hesitated; each hesitation may be a hidden assumption.
6. Write the answer key with the reasoning, not only the final letter.
**Explain simply:**
- "Hidden assumption" means something the writer takes for granted that the solver does not know, such as "left" meaning the speaker's left.
- Brute force means trying every case; it is slow by hand but easy for a computer on small puzzles, and it proves uniqueness.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Two answers survive?* Add one clue that removes one of them, then re-run. 2) *Zero survive?* Two clues contradict; remove or change one.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing clues that fit your answer but also fit others; skipping the friend test; forgetting to state whether "left of" means "directly" left or "somewhere" left.

### Q11 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mini-project: write a small program or spreadsheet that checks a seating puzzle by trying every arrangement. Four people A, B, C and D sit in a row in positions 1 to 4 from left to right. A does not sit at either end. B sits somewhere to the left of C. D sits immediately to the right of A. C and D do not sit next to each other. How many arrangements satisfy all four conditions, and what is the order from position 1 to 4?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn clues into tests and prove the answer is unique.
**Say this in the interview:** "Four people give 24 orders. I test each against the four clues, and exactly one survives: B, C, A, D."
**Answer:** **Exactly 1 arrangement: position 1 = B, 2 = C, 3 = A, 4 = D.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. All orders of 4 people: 4 x 3 x 2 x 1 = 24.
2. A is not at an end, so A is in position 2 or 3.
3. D is right after A, so (A, D) is (2, 3) or (3, 4).
4. If A=2, D=3: B and C fill 1 and 4; B left of C means B=1, C=4; but C=4 is next to D=3, which breaks the last clue. Fail.
5. If A=3, D=4: B and C fill 1 and 2; B left of C means B=1, C=2; C=2 is not next to D=4. All clues pass.
```
Position:  1   2   3   4
Person:    B   C   A   D
```
**Code (interview version, runs in Python):**
```python
from itertools import permutations

# Try every order of the four people in positions 1 to 4
for order in permutations("ABCD"):
    position = {person: place + 1 for place, person in enumerate(order)}

    a_not_at_end = position["A"] not in (1, 4)
    b_left_of_c = position["B"] < position["C"]
    d_right_after_a = position["D"] == position["A"] + 1
    c_d_not_adjacent = abs(position["C"] - position["D"]) != 1

    # Keep the order only if all four clues hold
    if a_not_at_end and b_left_of_c and d_right_after_a and c_d_not_adjacent:
        print(order)
```
Real output: `('B', 'C', 'A', 'D')`, one line only, so the answer is unique.
**Spreadsheet version:** list the 24 orders in rows, one column per clue returning TRUE or FALSE, and a final column with AND of the four; count the TRUE rows (hand-described, same logic as the code).
**Check:** Verified by script (one survivor out of 24) and by hand above.
**Explain simply:**
- "Brute force" means testing every possibility; fine when there are only 24.
- Naming each clue as its own test makes the code readable and easy to change.
**Likely follow-ups:** *Drop the clue "C and D not next to each other"?* Then two arrangements pass: A=2, D=3 gives B, A, D, C, and the original B, C, A, D (verified by editing the clue and re-running the same logic).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading "somewhere to the left" as "immediately to the left"; forgetting positions run left to right; stopping at the first arrangement found without checking for others.

### Q13 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mini-project: pick one claim from an HR report (for example 'employees who attended training have lower attrition') and write a one-page note that lists the conclusion, the assumptions behind it and one alternative explanation. Which reasoning skills does this practise, and how would you show it to an interviewer?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Ability to separate a claim from its evidence and spot weak links, especially cause vs correlation.
**Say this in the interview:** "I write the claim, what must be true for it to hold, and at least one other explanation that fits the same data. Then I say what data would separate them."
**Answer:** **A one-page note with five parts: claim, evidence, assumptions, alternative explanation, next check.**
**Worked note (invented numbers, for illustration):**
- Claim: "Employees who attended training have lower attrition." Data: 10% attrition among 200 who attended vs 20% among 300 who did not.
- Conclusion as written: training reduces attrition.
- Assumptions: (1) the two groups were similar before training; (2) attendance, not something else, caused the gap; (3) attrition is counted over the same period for both.
- Alternative explanation: motivated, high-performing employees chose to attend and were less likely to leave anyway (selection effect, so the cause may run from engagement to attendance).
- Next check: compare similar employees (same department, tenure, rating); or look at attrition before and after training for the same people.
**Skills practised:** spotting assumptions, cause vs correlation, alternative explanations, and writing a conclusion no stronger than the evidence.
**Explain simply:**
- Correlation means two things move together; it does not prove one causes the other.
- A "confounder" is a third factor (here motivation) that influences both.
**How to show it:** put 2 or 3 such notes in a portfolio folder (a short README on GitHub or a PDF), each one page, using non-confidential or invented data; in the interview, walk through one in 2 minutes.
**Likely follow-ups:** *What if the interviewer says "training still works"?* "It may; I am saying the data as given cannot prove it, and here is the check that would."
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing "training caused the drop" without alternatives; using real employee data in a public portfolio; listing alternatives with no way to test them.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mini-project: take five business rules written in words (for example 'an employee gets a bonus if the rating is 4 or more and tenure is over 1 year, unless on notice') and turn each into an Excel or SQL condition. How do you test the conditions with edge cases (exactly on the boundary, blank values) before you trust them?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Translating words into precise conditions and testing boundaries and blanks.
**Say this in the interview:** "I write each rule as a condition, then test just below, on, and just above every boundary, plus blank values, and decide what a blank should mean before I trust it."
**Answer:** **Write the condition, build a small test table with edge cases, check each result against what the rule says in words, and agree how blanks (NULL in SQL) are treated.**
**Rule 1 as SQL (run on sqlite3) with a 7-row test table:**
```sql
-- Bonus if rating >= 4, tenure over 1 year, and not on notice
SELECT name,
       CASE
           WHEN rating >= 4 AND tenure_years > 1 AND on_notice = 0 THEN 'bonus'
           ELSE 'no bonus'
       END AS plain_version,
       CASE
           WHEN COALESCE(rating, 0) >= 4
            AND COALESCE(tenure_years, 0) > 1
            AND COALESCE(on_notice, 0) = 0 THEN 'bonus'
           ELSE 'no bonus'
       END AS blank_safe_version
FROM employees;
```
