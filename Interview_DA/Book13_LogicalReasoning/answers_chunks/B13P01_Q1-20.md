### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Which day-to-day analyst skills is a logical reasoning test trying to measure, and which important analyst skills does it fail to measure?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found). Vendor test pages list Data Analyst as a target role for logical reasoning tests (vendor claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand why the gate exists and can talk about reasoning as a work skill, not a trick.
**Say this in the interview:** "The test is a quick proxy for careful thinking: reading rules exactly, spotting what follows and what does not, and working under time. It cannot show business sense, communication, SQL or Excel skill, or how I handle messy real data."
**Answer:** **It measures: precise reading, deduction from given rules, pattern spotting, structured working and accuracy under time. It does not measure: domain knowledge, tool skills (SQL, Excel, BI), data cleaning on messy data, asking good questions, storytelling and stakeholder handling.**
**Explain simply:**
- Deduction means reaching a conclusion that must be true from the given facts. Analysts do this when they check whether a report's claim really follows from the data.
- Pattern spotting maps to noticing trends, outliers and broken rules in a dataset.
- A test has fixed facts and one right answer; real work has missing facts, so a test cannot see how you ask for them.
- Employers use it because it is cheap to run for many applicants (our reading, not a sourced claim).
**Worked mini-example:** Rule "all orders above 5,000 need approval". Deduction: an order of 7,000 needs approval. Real work adds: is the 5,000 in rupees or thousands, and who approves? The test checks the first, the job needs both.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does a high score prove you are a good analyst?* No, it only shows one slice of ability. 2) *How do you show the missing skills?* Through projects, SQL tests and clear explanations in the interview.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying the test is "just IQ" or "useless", or claiming it measures everything an analyst does.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a section, a correct answer earns 1 mark and a wrong answer loses 0.25 marks, and every question has 4 options. What is the expected score of a blind guess, and of a guess after you have confidently eliminated two options?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn a marking scheme into a guess-or-skip decision.
**Say this in the interview:** "Expected value is probability right times the reward minus probability wrong times the penalty. Blind: 0.25 times 1 minus 0.75 times 0.25 is 0.0625. After eliminating two: 0.5 minus 0.125 is 0.375."
**Answer:** **Blind guess: +0.0625 marks per question (1/16). After eliminating two options: +0.375 marks.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Expected value (EV) is the average result if you repeated the same situation many times.
2. Blind guess: chance right 1/4, wrong 3/4. EV = (1/4)(1) - (3/4)(1/4) = 0.25 - 0.1875 = 0.0625.
3. Two options removed: chance right 1/2. EV = 0.5 - 0.5(0.25) = 0.375.
**Check:** verified by script (exact fractions): 1/16 and 3/8.
**Explain simply:**
- A tiny positive EV means guessing helps only slightly; skipping scores 0.
- Each real elimination raises the odds, so confident elimination is worth more than raw luck.
- Caveat: 0.0625 is an average; one question is still a gamble.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Penalty 0.5, blind guess?* EV = 0.25 - 0.375 = -0.125, so do not blind guess (script-verified). 2) *Eliminate one option (3 left) at 0.25 penalty?* EV = 1/3 - (2/3)(0.25) = 0.1667, worth guessing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to multiply the penalty by the probability of being wrong, or treating "eliminated" as certain when you only half-eliminated.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A section has 25 questions in 30 minutes, so the plan is 72 seconds per question. The first 5 questions took 9 minutes in total. What average time per question is left for the remaining questions, and by what percentage is it lower than the original plan?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Quick time-budget arithmetic and awareness that overspending early squeezes later questions.
**Say this in the interview:** "30 minus 9 leaves 21 minutes for 20 questions, which is 63 seconds each. That is 9 seconds below 72, so 12.5 percent lower."
**Answer:** **63 seconds per question, which is 12.5% lower than 72 seconds.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Remaining time = 30 - 9 = 21 minutes = 1,260 seconds.
2. Remaining questions = 25 - 5 = 20.
3. 1,260 / 20 = 63 seconds.
4. Drop = (72 - 63) / 72 = 9/72 = 0.125 = 12.5%.
**Check:** verified by script: 63.0 s and 0.125.
**Explain simply:**
- The first 5 questions used 108 s each on average (540 / 5), well above 72.
- Percentage lower = (old - new) / old.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the first 5 took 6 minutes?* 24 min for 20 questions = 72 s, exactly on plan (script-verified). 2) *What do you do?* Skip the slowest questions early.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing 21 minutes by 25, or computing the percentage against 63 instead of 72.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Candidate A attempts 15 questions and gets 60% of them right. Candidate B attempts 10 questions and gets 90% right. Each correct answer is worth 1 mark. Who scores higher (a) with no negative marking, and (b) with a penalty of 1/3 mark per wrong answer?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you see that accuracy and attempts trade off differently under different rules.
**Say this in the interview:** "A gets 9 right and 6 wrong; B gets 9 right and 1 wrong. With no penalty they tie at 9. With a one-third penalty A scores 7 and B scores about 8.67, so B wins."
**Answer:** **(a) Tie at 9 marks each. (b) B scores higher: B about 8.67 versus A 7.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. A: 15 x 0.6 = 9 right, 6 wrong. B: 10 x 0.9 = 9 right, 1 wrong.
2. (a) No penalty: A = 9, B = 9.
3. (b) A = 9 - 6/3 = 9 - 2 = 7. B = 9 - 1/3 = 8.67.
**Check:** verified by script with exact fractions (7 and 26/3).
**Explain simply:**
- Attempting more helps only if the extra answers are right often enough.
- Here A's 5 extra attempts added no net right answers but 5 extra wrong ones.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *A with a penalty of 0 and 12 right of 15?* A would lead (12 versus 9). 2) *At what penalty does B overtake A?* Any positive penalty, since B has fewer wrong answers.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying A wins because 15 is more than 10, or taking 60% of 10.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A test gives +4 for a correct answer and -1 for a wrong one. At what probability of being right does attempting a question break even, and does a blind guess on a 4-option question pay?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Setting up a break-even probability.
**Say this in the interview:** "Break even where 4p equals 1 minus p times 1, so p is 0.2. A blind guess has p of 0.25, above 0.2, so it pays slightly: +0.25 marks."
**Answer:** **Break-even probability = 1/5 = 20%. A blind guess (25%) pays: expected +0.25 marks per question.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. EV = 4p - 1(1 - p) = 5p - 1.
2. Set EV = 0, so p = 1/5.
3. Blind guess, 4 options: p = 0.25, EV = 5(0.25) - 1 = 0.25.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Break-even p = penalty / (reward + penalty) = 1/5. You need to be right at least once in 5 tries.
**Check:** verified by script: EV(0.25) = 1/4, EV(0.2) = 0.
**Explain simply:**
- Break-even is the chance at which guessing neither gains nor loses on average.
- Above it, attempt; below it, skip.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *+3 and -1?* Break-even 1/4, so a blind 4-option guess is exactly zero. 2) *+1 and -0.25?* 0.25/1.25 = 0.2, same as here.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Putting reward over (reward + penalty), which gives 0.8 instead of 0.2; the penalty goes on top.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Which words in a reasoning question stem (for example "only", "at least", "at most", "must be", "could be", "not necessarily") most often cause misreading, and what routine do you use to read a stem without losing marks?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Careful reading, the commonest source of lost marks.
**Say this in the interview:** "Words that set strength or limits flip the answer. I read the question line first, underline the limiting words, then read the data, and re-read the question before answering."
**Answer:** **Risky words: only, at least, at most, exactly, must be (always true), could be (possible), not necessarily (not forced), except, not, all, some, no.**
**Explain simply:**
- Must be true means true in every possible case; could be true means true in at least one case.
- At least 3 means 3 or more; at most 3 means 3 or fewer.
- "Not necessarily true" means some case exists where it is false.
**Worked mini-example:** Fact: "at least 2 of 5 people are tall". Must be true: at least 1 is tall. Could be true: all 5 are tall. Not necessarily true: exactly 2 are tall.
**Our suggested routine:** (1) read the question first, (2) circle limiting words, (3) read the data, (4) solve, (5) re-read the question and tick the answer to it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Where do you lose most marks?* On EXCEPT and NOT items, so circle them. 2) *What if "some" appears?* Treat it as at least one.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "could be" with a "must be" test, or missing a word like "not".

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In a 25-minute section of 20 questions, you plan 8 easy questions at 45 seconds, 8 medium questions at 80 seconds and skip the 4 hard ones in the first pass. How much time is left for each hard question if you share the remaining time equally?

### Q18 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A test deducts 1/3 mark for each wrong answer and every question has 4 options. What is the expected value of a blind guess, and what is the minimum number of options you must eliminate before guessing becomes worthwhile?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** The classic "fair penalty" idea.
**Say this in the interview:** "A blind guess is 1/4 minus 3/4 times 1/3, which is exactly zero. Eliminating one option gives 1/3 minus 2/3 times 1/3, which is 1/9 above zero, so one elimination is enough."
**Answer:** **Blind guess: expected value 0. Minimum eliminations for a positive expected value: 1 (EV = +1/9 = +0.111 marks); with 2 eliminated, +1/3.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Blind: EV = (1/4)(1) - (3/4)(1/3) = 1/4 - 1/4 = 0.
2. One option removed: p = 1/3. EV = 1/3 - (2/3)(1/3) = 3/9 - 2/9 = 1/9.
3. Two removed: p = 1/2. EV = 1/2 - (1/2)(1/3) = 1/3.
**Check:** verified by script (exact fractions): 0, 1/9, 1/3.
**Explain simply:**
- With penalty 1/(options - 1) the scheme is designed so pure guessing averages zero.
- "Worthwhile" here means a positive average; the gain from one elimination is small, so only eliminate when truly sure.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Penalty 1/2 with 4 options?* Blind EV = -1/8, so skip; one elimination: 1/3 - (2/3)(1/2) = 0, break-even. 2) *5 options and penalty 1/4?* Blind EV = 1/5 - (4/5)(1/4) = 0.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying a blind guess "has a 25% chance, so it is fine".

### Q19 ⭐⭐ 🟡 One section has 15 questions in 35 minutes. Another section has 12 questions with 75 seconds allowed per question. How many seconds per question does the first allow, how many minutes does the second take in total, and which one demands faster reading?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Unit conversion and pace comparison.
**Say this in the interview:** "35 minutes is 2,100 seconds; divided by 15 is 140 seconds. The second is 12 times 75 which is 900 seconds, 15 minutes. 75 seconds is the tighter pace, so the second demands faster reading."
**Answer:** **First section: 140 seconds per question. Second section: 15 minutes in total. The second section demands faster reading (75 s versus 140 s per question).**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. 35 x 60 = 2,100 s; 2,100 / 15 = 140 s.
2. 12 x 75 = 900 s; 900 / 60 = 15 min.
3. 75 < 140, so the second leaves less time per question.
**Check:** verified by script: 140.0 s and 15.0 min.
**Explain simply:**
- Compare pace as seconds per question, not total minutes: 35 minutes sounds longer but is spread over only 15 questions.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which suits long puzzles?* The 140 s section. 2) *Questions per minute in each?* 15/35 = 0.43 versus 12/15 = 0.8.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Comparing 35 minutes with 15 minutes directly.

### Q20 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How does your strategy change when sections have separate timers and you cannot go back, compared with a test that has one total timer and free navigation between questions?
**Asked at:** Reports mention per-section time limits and cutoffs (Infosys, Glassdoor snippet, unverified). Navigation rules vary by platform.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Adapting strategy to test rules.
**Say this in the interview:** "With separate timers and no going back, I spend my time inside each section, answer every item before moving on and never carry hope of returning. With one timer and free navigation I do a quick first pass and return to the hard ones."
**Answer:** **Separate timers, no going back: decide on each question at once (answer, guess or skip permanently), finish each section's time, use leftover time to double-check inside the section. One timer, free navigation: first pass for easy and medium questions, flag hard ones, then return by value for time.**
**Explain simply:**
- Time in one section cannot be moved to another, so saving time there is wasted.
- Without going back, a skip is final, so a guess (if there is no penalty, or after elimination) beats leaving blank.
- With free navigation, spend time where marks per minute are highest.
**Worked mini-example:** Section of 10 questions, 12 minutes, no return: allow about 72 s each; if question 4 stalls at 90 s, pick the most likely option and move on.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Where do you check answers?* At the end of each section while its timer runs. 2) *If sectional cutoffs exist?* Balance effort across sections.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating a no-return test like a free-navigation one and "saving" a question for later.
