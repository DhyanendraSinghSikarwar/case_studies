### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you split an argument into premises, conclusion and assumption, and which signal words help you find the conclusion and the premises?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); statement-assumption-conclusion items appear in prep-site topic lists for Wipro, Infosys, TCS and Accenture (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you read an argument as a structure (reason, claim, hidden link) instead of as a blur of sentences.
**Say this in the interview:** "I first find the conclusion, the claim the author wants me to accept. Then I mark the premises, the stated reasons. The assumption is the unstated bridge that makes the premises enough for the conclusion."
**Answer:** **Conclusion = the point being argued; premises = the stated evidence; assumption = the unstated link between them.**
**Explain simply:**
- Conclusion signals: "so", "therefore", "thus", "hence", "it follows that", "should", "must", "clearly". Premise signals: "because", "since", "as", "given that", "for", "the reason is".
- Test for the conclusion: ask "what is the author trying to convince me of?" Everything else is support.
- An assumption never appears in the text. It is what must also be true for the premises to lead to the conclusion.
- Background and opposing views ("Some managers say...") are neither; they only set the scene.
**Worked mini-example:** "Our sales rose 10% after the new ad, so the ad works." Premise: sales rose 10% after the ad. Conclusion: the ad works. Assumption: nothing else (season, discount, festival) explains the rise.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can a conclusion come first?* Yes: "We should keep Saturday shifts, because Saturday orders are 22% of volume." The word "because" marks the premise. 2) *Can there be two conclusions?* Yes, an intermediate one (supported by premises, then used as a premise) and a main one; the main one is the final claim.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing the first sentence as the conclusion; treating an opposing view as the conclusion; mistaking a restated premise for an assumption.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the negation test for an assumption question, why does it work, and how would you apply it to the argument "Sales rose after the new logo was launched, so the logo works"?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the negation idea is described on GMAT prep sites (GMAT Club, unverified as an interview item).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can test a candidate assumption instead of just feeling it is right.
**Say this in the interview:** "I negate the option. If the argument collapses when the option is false, the option is a necessary assumption. If the argument survives, it is not."
**Answer:** **Negate the answer choice; if the conclusion falls apart, it was a necessary assumption.**
**Explain simply:**
- Why it works: a necessary assumption is something the argument cannot do without. If it were false, the premises could no longer support the conclusion. So its opposite should wreck the argument.
- Negating means making the statement false in the simplest way ("always" becomes "not always"), not turning it into an extreme opposite.
- This tests *necessary* assumptions. It does not tell you the option is enough on its own.
**Worked mini-example:** Candidate: "Nothing else that could raise sales (a festival, a discount) happened at the same time as the launch." Negation: "Something else did happen at the same time and raised sales." Now the rise may not be due to the logo, so the argument collapses. Therefore the candidate is an assumption. A weak candidate: "The logo is blue." Negated ("it is not blue") the argument is unaffected, so it is not an assumption.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does it work on strengthen questions?* No, only on assumption questions that ask for a necessary assumption. 2) *Candidate: "Sales were not already rising before the launch."* Negation: they were rising already, so the logo may add nothing; the argument is badly hurt, so it is an assumption.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Negating too strongly ("the logo made sales fall"); using the test on options that only add support.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In strengthen and weaken questions, what kinds of statements usually strengthen an argument and what kinds usually weaken it (name at least four patterns for each)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weaken questions are reported by Magoosh as the most common critical-reasoning type (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can aim at the weak point of an argument: the link between evidence and claim.
**Say this in the interview:** "A cause argument is strengthened by ruling out other causes and showing the effect follows the cause; it is weakened by offering another cause, reversing the direction, or showing the cause was absent when the effect happened."
**Answer:** **Strengthen: make the assumption more likely true. Weaken: make it less likely true, or show the evidence is unreliable.**
**Explain simply:**
- Strengthen patterns: (1) rule out an alternative cause; (2) show a dose effect (more of A, more of B); (3) show a comparison or control group without A does not show B; (4) show the sample is representative; (5) show the cause came before the effect, repeatedly, in several places.
- Weaken patterns: (1) offer an alternative cause; (2) reverse the direction (B causes A); (3) show it is a coincidence or tiny sample; (4) show the sample is biased (self-selected); (5) show A happened without B, or B happened without A; (6) show a side effect that defeats the plan.
- Do not use outside opinions; judge only against the conclusion's own link.
**Worked mini-example:** "Teams that use a dashboard hit targets, so the dashboard helps." Strengthens: teams hit targets only after getting it, and teams without it did not improve. Weakens: only already-strong teams were given the dashboard.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is "other firms report similar results" strong?* Usually only weakly relevant. 2) *Does a statement that merely agrees with the conclusion strengthen it?* No; it must support the link.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking an option that is true but irrelevant; picking an option that strengthens when asked to weaken (the opposite-direction trap).

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 When two metrics move together, what are the main alternative explanations besides "A causes B", and what is one HR example of each?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); correlation versus causation is a named topic in KDnuggets' list of statistical traps in interviews (article, unverified for any specific firm).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you reach for a confounder, reverse causation and chance before accepting a cause.
**Say this in the interview:** "Besides A causing B, it could be B causing A, a third factor driving both, selection of who is in each group, or just chance in a small sample."
**Answer:** **Reverse causation, a confounder (third factor), selection bias, and chance.**
**Explain simply:**
- Reverse causation: B causes A. HR: "Employees with high engagement scores attend more training." Perhaps engaged people sign up for training more, not training raising engagement.
- Confounder (a hidden third factor driving both): "Employees who use the gym take fewer sick days." Age or baseline health may drive both.
- Selection bias (the groups differ in who ends up in them): "Volunteers for the mentoring programme get promoted faster." Ambitious people volunteer.
- Chance: "Two of the five people trained left, so training causes exit." A tiny sample can show any pattern.
- Also possible: a mix effect (Simpson-type), where subgroup sizes change the overall figure.
**Worked mini-example:** Ice cream sales and heatstroke cases both rise; the hot weather drives both (confounder).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you test a causal claim?* A randomised experiment or a fair before-and-after with a comparison group. 2) *Does correlation mean no cause?* No, it only means the cause is not shown.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating "we cannot prove cause" as "there is no cause"; naming only one alternative.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Argument: "Weekend sales were 20% higher after the retailer launched its loyalty app, so the app boosted sales." Which statement, if true, weakens the argument most?
- (a) The app has a 4.5-star rating in the app store.
- (b) The weekend used for the comparison fell during a major festival, while the earlier weekends did not.
- (c) The app has been downloaded 10,000 times.
- (d) The retailer has 40 stores.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weaken items are a standard critical-reasoning type (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you spot an alternative cause for a before-and-after comparison.
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument assumes nothing else changed. A festival is another reason for higher sales, so (b) weakens it most."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Conclusion: the app boosted sales. Evidence: a 20% rise in the weekend after launch.
2. Hidden assumption: no other factor explains the rise.
3. (b) supplies exactly such a factor: a festival boosts sales by itself.
4. (a) and (c) say the app is liked or downloaded, which if anything supports it. (d) is irrelevant (store count tells us nothing about the cause).
**Check:** Negate the assumption ("something else changed"): (b) is that statement. Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- A weakener usually offers another cause, a different explanation for the same data.
- Before-and-after comparisons are fragile because time also changes things (season, festivals, prices).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would strengthen it?* The same rise in only the stores that had the app, but not in stores without it. 2) *Would "downloads were 10,000" help?* Only if sales rose more among downloaders.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (a) or (c), which are positive facts but do not address the link; choosing (d) because it sounds like scale.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A company has 500 employees; 60% work in hybrid mode. Of the hybrid employees, 30% are based in Hyderabad. Which statement must be true?
- (a) Exactly 90 employees are both hybrid and based in Hyderabad.
- (b) Exactly 150 employees are based in Hyderabad.
- (c) 30% of all employees are based in Hyderabad.
- (d) Hyderabad has the largest number of hybrid employees of any city.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); percentage-of-a-percentage inference items are common in prep-site reasoning sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate what the data fixes from what it leaves open.
**Say this in the interview:** "60% of 500 is 300 hybrid; 30% of 300 is 90. That is exactly what (a) says. The others claim things about non-hybrid staff or other cities that we are not told."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Hybrid employees = 0.60 x 500 = 300.
2. Hybrid and in Hyderabad = 0.30 x 300 = 90, so (a) is forced.
3. (b) and (c) talk about all Hyderabad employees; the 30% applies only to hybrid staff, and we know nothing about the 200 non-hybrid. Hyderabad total is at least 90, not exactly 150.
4. (d): other cities share the other 70% of 300 = 210 hybrid staff; one other city could hold all 210, so Hyderabad need not be largest.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 0.6 x 0.3 = 0.18; 18% of 500 = 90. Then reject any option that widens the group beyond "hybrid".
**Check:** Verified by script (300 and 90). Options (b)-(d) hand-checked by counter-example.
**Explain simply:**
- "Must be true" means true in every possible situation consistent with the facts.
- A percentage applies only to the group named in the sentence.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if 40% of the hybrid group were in Pune?* Then 120 hybrid in Pune, which would be more than Hyderabad's 90. 2) *What is the least number of Hyderabad employees?* 90.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Applying 30% to all 500 (150); assuming the biggest named city is the biggest overall.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Employees who use the office gym take fewer sick days. Which of the following is NOT a plausible alternative explanation to "the gym reduces sick days"?
- (a) Healthier employees are more likely to join the gym.
- (b) Younger employees both use the gym more and fall sick less.
- (c) Gym visits are recorded by a swipe card.
- (d) In a small sample the pattern could be due to chance.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic traces to correlation-versus-causation guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can tell a real alternative explanation from an irrelevant detail.
**Say this in the interview:** "(a) is reverse causation, (b) is a confounder, (d) is chance. (c) is just how data is recorded and explains nothing about why the pattern appears."
**Answer:** **(c)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. An alternative explanation must give another reason for the same pattern.
2. (a): health leads to gym use, not the other way (reverse causation).
3. (b): age drives both gym use and sickness (confounder).
4. (d): a small sample can produce a pattern by luck.
5. (c) describes a measurement method; it offers no other cause. (It could matter only if swipe-card gaps made records wrong, but as stated it does not explain the link.)
**Check:** Each option tested against "does it offer another cause or reason for the pattern?" Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- The standard rivals to "A causes B" are reverse cause, a third factor, selection and chance.
- A fact about how data is stored is not a rival cause.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Name the bias in (a).* Self-selection (healthy people choose the gym). 2) *How would you test the gym effect fairly?* Compare the same people before and after joining, or randomise access.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Spotting a fact that "sounds data-related" and calling it a rival cause.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Which of these four statements claim a cause, and which only describe an association? (i) Employees with longer commutes are more likely to resign. (ii) Longer commutes make employees resign. (iii) Resignations are higher among long-commute employees, and the two appear linked. (iv) Because of long commutes, resignations rise. Choose the set that makes a causal claim.
- (a) (i) and (iii)
- (b) (ii) and (iv)
- (c) (ii) only
- (d) (i), (ii) and (iv)
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the cause-versus-association distinction is a named interview topic in KDnuggets (article, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you notice causal wording ("make", "because of") versus descriptive wording.
**Say this in the interview:** "(ii) says 'make' and (iv) says 'because of'; both state a cause. (i) and (iii) only report that the two go together; 'appear linked' is still an association."
**Answer:** **(b) (ii) and (iv)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. (i): "more likely to" compares rates; no cause is claimed.
2. (ii): "make employees resign" asserts causation.
3. (iii): "appear linked" is hedged association.
4. (iv): "because of" asserts causation.
5. So the causal set is (ii) and (iv).
**Check:** Words scanned for causal verbs and connectors; hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- Causal words: cause, make, lead to, result in, because of, due to, drives. Association words: linked, associated with, more likely, goes with.
- Careful analysts report associations as associations.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Rewrite (ii) as an association.* "Long-commute employees resign more often." 2) *Is "is related to" causal?* No.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating (i) as causal because it sounds alarming; including (iii) because it says "linked".

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Attrition fell from 20% to 15%. One manager says it "fell by 5%" and another says it "fell by 25%". Who is correct, and what are the precise terms?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); percentage versus percentage-point is a standard data-literacy item (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate a percentage-point change from a percent (relative) change.
**Say this in the interview:** "Both are right if worded precisely: it fell 5 percentage points, which is a 25% relative decline."
**Answer:** **Both: a drop of 5 percentage points (absolute change) and a 25% fall (relative change).**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Absolute change = 15% - 20% = -5 percentage points.
2. Relative change = (15 - 20) / 20 = -0.25 = -25%.
3. The first manager's "5%" is loose; it should say "5 percentage points". The second is correct as a relative change.
**Check:** Verified by script (-5 points, -25%).
**Explain simply:**
- A percentage point is the gap between two percentages; a percent change is the gap divided by the starting value.
- Always say which one you mean, and give the base (20% to 15%).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If attrition then rises from 15% back to 20%?* +5 points but +33.3% relative (5/15). 2) *Why are they not symmetric?* The base differs.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying "fell by 5%" when you mean points; assuming a 25% fall and a 25% rise cancel out.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Region X orders grew by 300% (from 2 to 8 orders), while Region Y orders grew by 10% (from 5,000 to 5,500). Which region contributed more to the company's growth in orders, and by how many orders?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); small-base growth is a common data-analyst trap (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you compare absolute contributions and not only growth rates.
**Say this in the interview:** "X added 6 orders and Y added 500. Y contributed far more, by 494 orders. The 300% sounds big only because X started from a tiny base."
**Answer:** **Region Y contributed more: +500 orders versus +6 for X, a difference of 494 orders.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. X: 8 - 2 = +6 orders (+300%, from a base of 2).
2. Y: 5,500 - 5,000 = +500 orders (+10%).
3. Combined growth: 506 orders on a base of 5,002, about 10.1%; Y accounts for 500/506, about 98.8%.
**Check:** Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Growth rate = change / starting value. A tiny starting value makes a huge rate from a small change.
- Contribution to growth = the absolute change.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When is the rate more useful?* To judge momentum or potential, or when bases are similar. 2) *Is X's growth meaningless?* Not meaningless, but unreliable with 2 orders; ask for more months.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing X because 300% is larger; adding the percentages (310%).

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Passage: "Several managers want to drop Saturday shifts. Yet Saturday orders are 22% of weekly volume, and Saturday customer ratings are the highest of the week. Dropping Saturday shifts would therefore hurt revenue and satisfaction. So the company should keep them." Which is the main conclusion?
- (a) Saturday orders are 22% of weekly volume.
- (b) Dropping Saturday shifts would hurt revenue and satisfaction.
- (c) The company should keep Saturday shifts.
- (d) Several managers want to drop Saturday shifts.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); main-conclusion items appear in prep-site statement-conclusion topics (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you separate the final claim from premises and intermediate conclusions.
**Say this in the interview:** "(c) is the final claim, introduced by 'So' and carrying 'should'. (b) is an intermediate conclusion that supports it."
**Answer:** **(c)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. (d) is background, an opposing view the author argues against.
2. (a) and the ratings fact are premises (evidence).
3. (b), introduced by "therefore", follows from those premises, but it is then used to support the next claim.
4. (c), introduced by "So" and a "should", is where the chain ends: it is the main conclusion.
**Check:** Structure: premises, then (b), then (c). Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- An intermediate conclusion is a claim that is both supported by earlier points and used to support the final one.
- Recommendation words ("should") usually mark the main conclusion.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the passage ended at "therefore ... hurt revenue and satisfaction", what would the main conclusion be?* (b). 2) *Is "therefore" always the main conclusion?* No, check whether another claim follows.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b) because "therefore" is attached to it; choosing (d) because it comes first.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the difference between an assumption question, an inference ("must be true" or "most strongly supported") question and a conclusion question, and which trap words show which one you are facing?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); FACE Prep guides distinguish assumption, inference and conclusion items (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you read the question stem before the options, since each type points in a different direction.
**Say this in the interview:** "An assumption is hidden support behind the argument; an inference is something that follows from what is given; a conclusion question asks for the author's main claim. I read the stem first to know which one it is."
**Answer:** **Assumption looks backward (what must also be true); inference looks forward (what follows); conclusion asks what the author is claiming.**
**Explain simply:**
- Assumption stem words: "assumes", "depends on", "relies on", "necessary for the argument", "required". The correct option is unstated and links premise to conclusion.
- Inference stem words: "must be true", "can be inferred", "most strongly supported", "follows from". The answer is a modest statement that the passage guarantees; no new facts.
- Conclusion stem words: "main conclusion", "the passage is mainly arguing", "the author concludes". The answer is the claim that others support.
- Trap words in options: "all", "only", "never", "every", "completely" (too strong); outside facts or causes the passage never mentions (scope shift).
**Worked mini-example:** Passage: "Of 200 staff, 120 are in Pune." Inference: "More than half of the staff are in Pune" (must be true, 60%). Not inference: "Pune is the best city" (outside scope).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is "must be true" the same as "likely true"?* No; it needs certainty from the text. 2) *Does an assumption have to be stated in the argument?* No, that is why it is hidden.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing an attractive true-in-real-life statement instead of what the text supports; picking an option that restates the premise as an "assumption".

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Our new onboarding portal cut the time to complete joining formalities from 5 days to 2 days, so vacant positions will now be filled faster." Which assumption is necessary for the argument?
- (a) Joining formalities are counted as part of the time a position stays vacant.
- (b) Every other step of hiring will also become faster.
- (c) All new hires prefer a portal to paper forms.
- (d) The portal cost less than the paper process.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); assumption items are standard in critical-reasoning sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you find the missing link between a process saving and the final outcome.
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument jumps from faster formalities to faster filling of vacancies. That only works if formalities are part of the vacant period. I test (a) by negating it: if formalities happen after the position is already counted as filled, the saved days do not matter."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: formalities now take 2 days instead of 5. Conclusion: vacancies get filled faster.
2. Gap: do the formality days count towards how long a position is vacant?
3. Negate (a): formalities are not part of the vacant period (for example the seat counts as filled at offer acceptance). Then saving 3 days there changes nothing, and the conclusion fails. So (a) is necessary.
4. (b) is too strong: the argument needs only the formality stage to be faster, not every step.
5. (c) and (d) are about preference and cost, which do not link to speed of filling.
**Check:** Negation test applied to all four; only (a) breaks the argument. Hand-checked.
**Explain simply:**
- A necessary assumption is the smallest missing link; it should not be stronger than the argument needs.
- Words like "every" and "all" in an option are warning signs.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What else could weaken it?* Other steps (such as interviews) get slower or the bottleneck is elsewhere. 2) *Would "formalities were the longest step" strengthen it?* Yes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because it sounds like it helps; picking (c)/(d), which are plausible side facts but not links.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "New hires score the same on the post-induction quiz after watching a 2-hour recorded video as after the 2-day classroom induction, so the company can replace the classroom induction with the video." Which is an assumption of the argument?
- (a) The quiz measures what the induction is meant to achieve.
- (b) New hires enjoy videos more than classrooms.
- (c) The classroom induction has no social benefit at all.
- (d) The video will be watched in a group.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); statement-assumption items appear in prep-site topic lists for Wipro, Infosys, TCS and Accenture (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you spot that the evidence (a quiz score) must stand in for the real goal (a well-inducted employee).
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument jumps from equal quiz scores to equal induction. That only works if the quiz captures what the induction is for. So the answer is (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: equal quiz scores after video and classroom. Conclusion: the video can replace the classroom.
2. The gap: a quiz is a proxy (a stand-in measure). The argument needs the proxy to cover the real purpose of induction (culture, people, process knowledge).
3. Negation test of (a): "the quiz does NOT measure what induction is for." Then equal scores prove nothing and the conclusion collapses. So (a) is necessary.
4. Why the others fail: (b) is irrelevant, because enjoyment is not the claim (scope shift). (c) is too strong: the video can replace the classroom even if the classroom has some small social benefit, as long as it is not important; "no benefit at all" is not required. (d) is not needed; the argument is about the video itself, and a group viewing is a detail.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Ask "what bridges the premise to the conclusion?" Premise is a quiz, conclusion is about the whole induction, so look for the option that links quiz to purpose. Be wary of "no ... at all" wording.
**Check:** Negating (b), (c) and (d) leaves the argument standing; negating (a) breaks it (hand-checked).
**Explain simply:**
- An assumption is an unstated statement the argument needs. A necessary assumption is one whose opposite would destroy the argument.
- A proxy metric is an easy-to-measure stand-in (quiz score) for the thing you truly care about (readiness to work).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which new fact would weaken it?* Hires from the video group make more process errors in month 3. 2) *What would strengthen it?* Equal job performance after 6 months for both groups.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (c) because it sounds protective; the extreme word "at all" makes it unnecessary.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "A vendor quotes Rs 40 per payslip while our internal payroll team costs Rs 55 per payslip, so outsourcing payroll will save money." Which is an assumption of the argument?
- (a) The vendor's quote and the internal cost cover the same scope of work.
- (b) The vendor has more experience than the internal team.
- (c) Employees prefer vendor-issued payslips.
- (d) Payslip volumes will rise next year.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you check that two numbers are like-for-like before comparing them.
**Say this in the interview:** "Rs 40 versus Rs 55 only compares if both prices include the same work. So the assumption is (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: Rs 40 (vendor) is less than Rs 55 (internal) per payslip. Conclusion: outsourcing saves money.
2. Gap: is Rs 40 the whole cost? The vendor might charge extra for tax filings, corrections, set-up, or the internal team may also do compliance work.
3. Negation of (a): "the scopes differ" (for example the vendor quote excludes statutory filing). Then the saving may vanish. So (a) is necessary.
4. Wrong options: (b) experience is not needed for cost saving (irrelevant to the claim). (c) employee preference is a different goal (scope shift). (d) volume growth is not required; if anything the saving per payslip holds at any volume, and the argument does not depend on it.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** For any cost comparison, ask "same scope, same period, same quality?" The option saying "like for like" is nearly always the assumption.
**Check:** Raw numbers: the apparent saving is Rs 15 per payslip (about 27% of Rs 55), computed by script; this saving is real only if scopes match.
**Explain simply:**
- Like-for-like means comparing things measured the same way.
- A hidden fee can turn a cheaper quote into a dearer one.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What other hidden cost matters?* One-time migration or exit cost. 2) *If the vendor charges Rs 40 plus a fixed Rs 5 lakh yearly fee, when is outsourcing cheaper?* When volume is high enough that the Rs 15 per payslip saving exceeds the fee (break-even volume = fee divided by 15).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (d) because it sounds financial; it adds a forecast the argument does not use.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Employees who chose to take the optional Excel course have higher performance ratings, so the course should be made compulsory for everyone." Which is an assumption of the argument?
- (a) Employees who did not choose the course would benefit from it in a similar way.
- (b) Excel is the only skill that matters for performance.
- (c) The course is free of charge.
- (d) Performance ratings are completely free of manager bias.

### Q30 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the difference between a necessary and a sufficient assumption, and how can you tell which one a question wants?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); assumption questions appear in prep-site topic lists for Wipro, Infosys, TCS and Accenture (prep-site claims, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you read the question stem precisely and use the right test for each type.
**Say this in the interview:** "A necessary assumption is something the argument cannot do without; if it is false, the argument fails. A sufficient assumption is enough on its own to guarantee the conclusion. The wording of the question tells me which."
**Answer:** **Necessary = must be true for the argument to work (test: negate it, the argument breaks). Sufficient = if true, the conclusion definitely follows (test: add it, no gap is left).**
**Explain simply:**
- Wording clues. Necessary: "the argument depends on", "requires", "must be assumed", "assumes which of the following". Sufficient: "if assumed, would allow the conclusion to be properly drawn", "which would justify the conclusion".
- Necessary can be weak and modest ("not all the sample dropped out"). Sufficient is usually strong and complete.
- Every sufficient assumption that really closes the gap contains the necessary one, but a necessary one is often not enough alone.
**Worked mini-example:** Argument: "Sales rose after the new logo, so the logo works." Necessary: nothing else raised sales at the same time (negate it and the argument fails). Sufficient: "Sales rise only when the logo changes, and the logo changed." That alone guarantees the conclusion, but it is much stronger than the argument needs.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can one statement be both?* Yes, when it is exactly the missing link. 2) *Which is a typical answer to "depends on"?* A necessary one, so use negation.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking a very strong, extreme option for a necessary-assumption question (too strong is not required); picking a modest option for a sufficient question (does not close the gap).

### Q31 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What kind of evidence turns a correlation into a credible causal claim (control group, random assignment, timing, dose-response, same-period comparison)? Rank them and say why.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); correlation versus causation is a named topic in analytics-interview guides (for example KDnuggets, article).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know why some evidence rules out rival explanations better than others.
**Say this in the interview:** "Random assignment with a control group is the strongest, because it removes confounders by design. Then a same-period comparison group, then dose-response, and timing is the weakest because it is necessary but not enough."
**Answer:** **Our ranking, strongest to weakest: 1) random assignment with a control group; 2) a control group compared over the same period (not random); 3) dose-response; 4) timing (cause before effect).**
**Explain simply:**
- 1. Random assignment: chance decides who gets the treatment, so both groups are alike on average in everything, including things you cannot measure. This is the only one that handles unknown confounders.
- 2. Control group over the same period: handles time-related changes (season, economy) but not differences between the groups (who chose the treatment). Keeping the period identical prevents "the whole company improved" being credited to the policy.
- 3. Dose-response: more of the cause goes with more of the effect. It is supportive, but a confounder can also grow with the dose (for example, more motivated people do more training).
- 4. Timing: the cause must come first, otherwise it cannot be the cause. It is required, but many things change at the same moment.
- The ranking is our judgement of how many rival explanations each item removes; real studies combine them.
**Worked mini-example:** Retention bonus. Randomly give 10 of 20 teams the bonus, compare attrition over the same 6 months, and check that teams with larger bonuses lost fewer people. That stacks all four.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can you randomise people?* Often not; then use a matched control group and say the claim is weaker. 2) *Is "before and after" with no control enough?* No, it only shows timing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating timing as proof ("after this, therefore because of this"); treating dose-response as proof; forgetting the control group must be measured in the same period.

### Q32 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is Simpson's paradox, why can the overall rate point one way while every subgroup points the other way, and what do you do as an analyst when you see it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); Simpson's paradox is a named topic in analytics-interview guides (for example KDnuggets, DataCamp).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand weighting (mix of groups) and know to segment before concluding.
**Say this in the interview:** "Simpson's paradox is when a trend in every subgroup reverses in the combined data, because the groups are very different in size and the mix differs between the options. I break the data down by the hidden variable and decide which view answers the question."
**Answer:** **The overall rate is a weighted average of subgroup rates; when the two options have very different mixes of easy and hard groups, the weights can flip the overall result.**
**Worked mini-example (numbers made up, checked by script):** Two training programs, success means passing the certification.

| Group | Program X | Program Y |
|---|---|---|
| Easy cases | 9 of 10 = 90% | 70 of 90 = 77.8% |
| Hard cases | 30 of 90 = 33.3% | 2 of 10 = 20% |
| Overall | 39 of 100 = **39%** | 72 of 100 = **72%** |

X wins in both groups, yet Y wins overall. Why: Y was given mostly easy cases (90 of 100) and X mostly hard ones (90 of 100). Overall rate = sum of (share of group x group rate), and the shares differ.
**Explain simply:**
- The hidden variable (here, case difficulty) is a confounder: it affects the outcome and is linked to who got which option.
- What to do: (1) segment by the suspected variable; (2) ask how people ended up in each group (assignment); (3) if the groups are comparable within segments, trust the segment view and report both; (4) compare like with like, or standardise to the same mix; (5) never publish only the overall number.
- Which view is right depends on the causal story, not on arithmetic. Here, difficulty is not caused by the program, so the segment view is right.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is the overall number ever right?* Yes, when the mix itself is the thing you care about or is caused by the option. 2) *How could you avoid it?* Randomise assignment so mixes match.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking the data is wrong (the sums are correct); always trusting subgroups or always trusting totals; ignoring group sizes.

### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is survivorship bias, and how could it mislead an analysis of employee engagement or training outcomes?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); selection bias is a named topic in analytics-interview guides (for example KDnuggets, article).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you ask who is missing from the data, not just what the data says.
**Say this in the interview:** "Survivorship bias is judging by the ones who stayed in the data and ignoring those who dropped out. An engagement survey of current employees misses the unhappy people who already left, so scores look better than reality."
**Answer:** **Drawing conclusions only from the survivors, so the missing cases (leavers, dropouts, failures) are never counted and results look too good.**
**Explain simply:**
- Engagement example: a survey sent in December covers only people still employed. If disengaged staff quit in the summer, the average engagement of survivors looks high even if the culture is driving people away.
- Training example: "Employees who completed the program score 20% higher in reviews." Dropouts (who may have struggled or left) are excluded, so the program gets credit for filtering out the weak.
- Attrition analysis: studying only long-tenured people to find "what makes people successful" copies traits that may be shared by those who failed too.
**Worked mini-example:** 100 people start a program, 40 drop out, 60 finish with an average rating of 4.2. If the dropouts would have rated 2.5, the full-group average is (60 x 4.2 + 40 x 2.5) / 100 = 3.52. The completers-only figure overstates by about 0.7 points (hand-computed).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you check for it?* Compare starters versus finishers, and look at exit data and dropout reasons. 2) *Fix?* Include everyone who started (intention-to-treat style) and track leavers.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calling it "small sample" (it is about who is missing, not how many); forgetting the HR version: exit-interview or survey non-response is also a missing-people problem.

### Q34 ⭐⭐ 🟡 List six questions you would ask before accepting a claim shown on a dashboard or slide (for example "attrition fell 5% after the new policy").
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); analysts are judged on structured thinking and stated assumptions in case-study guides (Upenn career-services blog, Jan 2026).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a repeatable checklist for scrutinising a number.
**Say this in the interview:** "I ask: what exactly is measured, compared with what, over what period, how big is the group, what else changed, and who is left out."
**Answer:** **Six questions (our suggested checklist):**
1. **Definition:** how is attrition defined (voluntary only? annualised? which employees)? Did the definition change?
2. **Baseline and comparison:** "fell 5%" from what to what, and compared with when? Is it 5 percentage points or 5% relative (for example 20% to 19% is 1 point but 5% relative)?
3. **Time window:** is it the same period (season, appraisal cycle)? Was a particular month chosen to look good?
4. **Size and noise:** how many people? A fall from 10 leavers to 9 can be chance. Is there a range or test?
5. **Other causes:** what else changed (hiring freeze, market, new manager)? Is there a comparison group without the policy?
6. **Who is missing or mixed:** survivors only, a changed headcount mix, subgroups hiding a reversed trend?
**Explain simply:**
- Percent versus percentage points: 20% to 19% is a drop of 1 percentage point and 5% in relative terms.
- A dashboard shows a number, never why it moved; the questions look for rival explanations.
**Worked mini-example:** 200 people, 20 leavers (10%) last year; 190 left in headcount after the cut, 19 leavers (10%) this year. The number of leavers fell 5% but the rate did not move.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which one first?* Definition and baseline, because errors there make the rest meaningless. 2) *What would convince you?* A comparison group over the same period and enough people.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Questioning only the arithmetic; accepting a count when the rate is the right measure; ignoring that the policy and another event may overlap.

### Q35 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Plan: "To shorten attendance-system queues, the firm will install two more biometric machines at the gate." Which statement, if true, weakens the plan most?
- (a) The queue delay is caused mainly by the central server taking long to verify each record, not by the number of machines.
- (b) The machines cost Rs 30,000 each.
- (c) Most employees arrive between 9:00 and 9:30.
- (d) The gate is wide enough for two more machines.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weaken questions are reported as the most common critical-reasoning type on prep sites (Magoosh, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you attack the assumption that the machine count is the bottleneck.
**Say this in the interview:** "The plan assumes more machines means shorter queues. If the real bottleneck is the server, extra machines will not help, so (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Plan: more machines, so shorter queues. Hidden assumption: machines are the limiting step.
2. (a) says the limit is server verification. Extra machines feed the same slow server, so the plan fails at its goal. Directly attacks the assumption.
3. Eliminate the rest (below).
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Cost is a side issue; it does not say the plan will not shorten queues (scope shift: cost versus effectiveness).
- (c) A peak at 9:00 to 9:30 explains why queues form, and if anything supports adding capacity for the peak.
- (d) Confirms the plan is feasible; it strengthens, not weakens.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item; one best answer by design).
**Explain simply:**
- Weaken the plan by showing it does not reach the goal (it addresses the wrong cause).
- Cost or space arguments weaken whether the plan is worth it, not whether it works.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which statement would strengthen it?* "Each machine handles one person at a time and the server is idle most of the day." 2) *What would you measure?* Time per verification step.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b) because it sounds like a real-world objection; the question asks about the plan's effect on queues.

### Q36 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "The average customer-satisfaction score rose from 3.9 to 4.2 after a new support lead joined." Which statement, if true, weakens the claim that the new lead improved satisfaction most?
- (a) During the same period the company stopped surveying customers who had raised complaints.
- (b) The new lead has ten years of experience.
- (c) The score is on a scale of 1 to 5.
- (d) Support calls are recorded.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); biased-sample and alternative-cause weakeners are standard critical-reasoning patterns.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you spot a measurement change that explains the rise without any real improvement.
**Say this in the interview:** "If unhappy customers stopped being surveyed, the average would rise even if service did not change. So (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Claim: score rose, so the lead improved satisfaction. Assumption: the score fairly measures satisfaction before and after.
2. (a) changes who is measured: removing the likely low scorers raises the average mechanically. This is a biased sample and an alternative explanation at once.
3. The others do not touch the link (below).
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Experience makes improvement more plausible, so it strengthens.
- (c) Describes the scale; 4.2 out of 5 is still higher than 3.9. Irrelevant.
- (d) Recording calls says nothing about the scores' trend; irrelevant.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item).
**Explain simply:**
- A changed survey method (who is asked, how) can move an average on its own.
- This is the survivorship idea from Q33 in a satisfaction setting.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Another good weakener?* "Product defects fell sharply during the same months." 2) *What data would you request?* Scores for the same customer mix before and after.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because it feels "real" (it strengthens); ignoring that the argument is about the average, which depends on who responds.

### Q37 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "The 4-day week succeeded at our Pune office, so it will succeed at all our offices." Which statement, if true, weakens the argument most?
- (a) The Pune office does project-based work, while all other offices run fixed-shift customer support with queues.
- (b) The Pune office has 120 employees.
- (c) The Pune office opened in 2019.
- (d) The 4-day week was announced in a town hall.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); generalising from one case is a standard flaw in critical-reasoning practice.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you test if the sample (one office) represents the target (all offices).
**Say this in the interview:** "The argument assumes Pune is like the other offices. (a) says it is different in exactly the way that matters for a 4-day week, so (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: it worked in Pune. Conclusion: it will work everywhere. Assumption: Pune is representative.
2. (a) shows a relevant difference: a fixed-shift service needs people present on set days, so cutting a day is harder than for project work. Pune's success may not transfer.
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Headcount alone gives no reason for a difference; no stated link to success.
- (c) Opening year is not shown to matter.
- (d) How it was announced is unrelated to whether it works operationally.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item).
**Explain simply:**
- Generalisation is safe only if the case is typical of the group. Relevant differences (work type, shifts, customers) break it.
- A difference must connect to the outcome; any random difference does not count.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What strengthens it?* "All offices do similar project work." 2) *Better recommendation?* Pilot in a different type of office first.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b) because size sounds relevant but nothing links 120 people to success; assuming "one office" itself, not the difference, is the weakness.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "People promoted from within are more committed than external hires, so the company should prefer internal promotions." Which statement, if true, strengthens the argument most?
- (a) The commitment gap remains when internal and external hires of the same grade and age are compared.
- (b) Internal promotions take less time to process.
- (c) External hires ask for higher salaries.
- (d) Internal candidates are known to the managers.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ruling out a confounder is a standard strengthening pattern.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you recognise that the best strengthener removes a rival explanation of the commitment gap.
**Say this in the interview:** "Committed internal people might just be older or in senior grades. If the gap persists after matching grade and age, that rival explanation is gone, so (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Premise: internals are more committed. Conclusion: prefer internals. Assumption: the gap is due to being internal and not to other differences.
2. (a) compares like with like and the gap survives, which removes the confounders grade and age.
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) Speed is a different benefit; it does not show commitment. Scope shift.
- (c) Salary difference is a cost point, it says nothing about commitment.
- (d) Familiarity helps selection but does not show that internal people are more committed.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item).
**Explain simply:**
- Matching (comparing groups equal on other traits) is a way to strip out confounders.
- Strengthening: make the link more believable, not add any pro-internal fact.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would weaken it?* "Internals are mostly older staff who were committed before promotion." 2) *Also needed?* Commitment may cause promotion (reverse direction).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking any option that favours internal hiring; the question is about the commitment claim, not general benefits.

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Label each situation as reverse causation, confounding or chance: (i) Cities with more police officers have more recorded crime. (ii) Among school children, shoe size correlates with reading score. (iii) Out of 400 unrelated metrics, one shows r = 0.6 with attrition over 8 months. Which is the correct labelling in order (i), (ii), (iii)?
- (a) Reverse causation, confounding, chance
- (b) Confounding, reverse causation, chance
- (c) Chance, confounding, reverse causation
- (d) Reverse causation, chance, confounding
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the three explanations of a correlation are standard in analytics-interview guides (for example DataCamp, tutorial).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you test each correlation with the three rival explanations.
**Say this in the interview:** "Police numbers rise because crime is high: reverse causation. Shoe size and reading both depend on age: confounding. One hit among 400 tests is expected by luck: chance. So (a)."
**Answer:** **(a) Reverse causation, confounding, chance**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. (i) Crime may cause cities to hire police (the effect drives the "cause"): reverse causation.
2. (ii) Older children have bigger feet and read better; age drives both: confounding (age is the confounder, a third variable behind both).
3. (iii) With 8 monthly points, r = 0.6 gives a two-sided p of about 0.116 (t-test for correlation, 6 degrees of freedom). Across 400 unrelated metrics you expect roughly 46 to pass that bar by luck, so one such hit means nothing: chance.
**Why the others fail:**
- (b) swaps (i) and (ii): shoe size does not cause reading, and reading does not change foot size, so no reverse direction; and age explains (ii) better than police explain (i).
- (c) calls police numbers chance; the link there has an obvious direction story.
- (d) calls shoe size chance; the age link is systematic across children, not luck.
**Check:** p-value and expected count verified by script (p = 0.116, expected 46.3); the labels are judgement.
**Explain simply:**
- Reverse causation: B causes A, not A causes B.
- Confounding: a third factor drives both. Chance: multiple tests produce flukes ("multiple comparisons").
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Fix for (iii)?* Pre-register one hypothesis, correct for multiple testing, confirm on new data. 2) *Fix for (ii)?* Compare children of the same age.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calling every correlation "confounding"; forgetting that with few data points and many metrics large correlations are common.

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Which study design best tests whether a new retention bonus reduces attrition?
- (a) Compare this year's company-wide attrition with last year's.
- (b) Compare employees who applied for the bonus with those who did not.
- (c) Randomly assign teams to get the bonus or not, and compare attrition over the same period.
- (d) Ask managers whether they think the bonus worked.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); experiment design is a standard analytics-interview topic.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you choose the design that removes confounders, self-selection and time effects.
**Say this in the interview:** "Random assignment with a control group measured over the same period isolates the bonus. So (c)."
**Answer:** **(c)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Goal: causal effect of the bonus on attrition.
2. (c) uses chance to form groups (equal on average in motivation, market exposure, anything unmeasured) and a same-period comparison (cancels season and market).
**Why the others fail:**
- (a) Before-and-after with no control: anything that changed in the year (job market, layoffs) is mixed in with the bonus.
- (b) Self-selection: people who apply for a bonus differ (for example, they already planned to stay), so the groups differ before the bonus.
- (d) Opinions are not outcomes; managers have biases and cannot see the counterfactual.
**Check:** hand-checked (judgement item; ranking as in Q31).
**Explain simply:**
- Randomisation breaks the link between who gets the bonus and any other trait.
- Assign whole teams so people in the same team cannot share the bonus news across groups.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if randomising is not allowed?* Use a matched comparison group over the same period and state the weaker claim. 2) *What would you measure?* Leavers over total headcount per team, after a fixed period.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (a) because it uses real company data; choosing (b) because it has a comparison group (the groups are self-selected).

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Team A has 10 employees with an average tenure of 6 years; Team B has 30 employees with an average tenure of 4 years. A manager says the combined average is 5 years. What is the correct combined average, and what error did the manager make?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weighted averages are a standard data-analyst check.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you weight averages by group size.
**Say this in the interview:** "I add total years: 10 x 6 + 30 x 4 = 180, divided by 40 people gives 4.5. The manager averaged the two averages without weighting by team size."
**Answer:** **4.5 years. The manager took the simple average of 6 and 4 (= 5) and ignored that Team B has three times as many people.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Total years in A: 10 x 6 = 60. In B: 30 x 4 = 120.
2. Total = 180 years over 40 employees: 180 / 40 = 4.5.
3. Equivalent weights: A is 25% of staff, B is 75%: 0.25 x 6 + 0.75 x 4 = 4.5.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** The answer must sit nearer the bigger group's average. Three times as many in B means 3 parts to 1: the combined value is 1/4 of the way from 4 to 6, so 4.5.
**Check:** verified by script (4.5).
**Explain simply:**
- Average of averages is right only when the groups are the same size.
- Weighted average: multiply each group's average by its size (or share), add, divide by the total size.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Teams are 20 and 20?* Then 5.0, the simple average is right (20 x 6 + 20 x 4 = 200, 200/40). 2) *Team B grows to 60?* (60 + 240) / 70 = 4.29 (hand-computed).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Simply averaging the averages; also the same error causes Simpson-type surprises when group sizes differ (Q32).

### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Day shift had 9 accidents over 30,000 worker-hours; night shift had 6 accidents over 6,000 worker-hours. A manager says the day shift is more dangerous because it had more accidents. Compare accident rates per 10,000 worker-hours and say how many times riskier the night shift is.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you compare rates (counts divided by exposure) instead of raw counts.
**Say this in the interview:** "Day had more accidents because it had five times the hours. Per 10,000 worker-hours, day is 3 and night is 10, so night is about 3.3 times riskier."
**Answer:** **Day 3.0 and night 10.0 accidents per 10,000 worker-hours; night is 10 / 3 = about 3.33 times riskier.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. "Exposure" means how much opportunity for accidents there was; here it is worker-hours.
2. Day: 9 / 30,000 = 0.0003 per hour, times 10,000 = 3.0.
3. Night: 6 / 6,000 = 0.001 per hour, times 10,000 = 10.0.
4. Ratio: 10.0 / 3.0 = 3.33.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Put both on the same base. 30,000 hours is 3 blocks of 10,000, so day = 9 / 3 = 3. 6,000 hours is 0.6 of a block, so night = 6 / 0.6 = 10.
**Check:** Verified by script (3.0, 10.0, ratio 3.33).
**Explain simply:**
- A count is only fair to compare when both groups had the same exposure.
- A rate divides the count by the exposure so unequal groups can be compared.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If night had 3 accidents instead?* 3 / 0.6 = 5 per 10,000, still 1.67 times day. 2) *What else would you ask?* Severity of the accidents and the type of work on each shift.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Comparing 9 versus 6 directly; or saying night is "3.33% riskier" when it is 3.33 times (233% higher).

### Q43 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mean salary is 8.0 LPA and the median is 6.2 LPA. A slide says "the typical employee earns 8 LPA". What is the best critique?
- (a) A few very high salaries pull the mean above what a typical employee earns, so the median is the better description of typical pay.
- (b) The median is always the wrong measure.
- (c) Salaries cannot be averaged.
- (d) 8.0 must be rounded to 8 LPA.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Knowing that the mean is pulled by extreme values and the median is the middle value.
**Say this in the interview:** "Mean above median means the pay is right-skewed: a few high earners drag the average up. Half of employees earn 6.2 or less, so 6.2 describes the typical person better. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. The median is the middle salary: half earn less, half earn more.
2. The mean is the total divided by headcount, so one very large salary lifts it.
3. Mean (8.0) well above median (6.2) signals a few high salaries, so "typical = 8" overstates what most people earn.
4. Why the others fail: (b) is too strong, since the median is often the right measure but not always; (c) is false, averages of salaries are routinely computed; (d) is irrelevant, 8.0 and 8 are the same number.
**Check:** Small example: salaries 4, 5, 6, 6, 29 give mean 10 and median 6; the mean sits above all but one person.
**Explain simply:**
- Skewed data has a long tail on one side; salary has a long right tail.
- Report the median for "typical", and show the spread if you can.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can the mean ever be the better choice?* Yes, for totals such as payroll cost. 2) *Which would you quote for the budget?* The mean, since mean times headcount equals total pay.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because it sounds decisive; "always" and "never" options are usually too strong.

### Q44 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A survey was emailed to all 2,000 employees as optional. 50 replied and 80% of them were satisfied. A slide says "80% of our employees are satisfied". What is the best critique?
- (a) Respondents chose to reply, so they may not represent the 2,000 employees.
- (b) The survey should have been on paper.
- (c) Satisfaction cannot be measured as a percentage.
- (d) The result should have been shown as a fraction.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting non-response and self-selection bias, and the gap between a sample and the population.
**Say this in the interview:** "Only 50 of 2,000 replied, which is 2.5%, and they chose themselves. The 80% describes those 50, not the company. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Response rate: 50 / 2,000 = 2.5%. The other 97.5% are unknown.
2. 80% of 50 is 40 satisfied people; that is 2% of the workforce we actually know about.
3. Self-selection: people with strong feelings (very happy or very unhappy) reply more often, so the sample may lean either way.
4. Why the others fail: (b) the medium is not the problem, the choice to reply is; (c) false, proportions are a normal measure of satisfaction; (d) 40 / 50 and 80% are the same information.
**Check:** Bounds: if all 1,950 non-responders were unsatisfied, only 40 / 2,000 = 2% would be satisfied; if all were satisfied, (40 + 1,950) / 2,000 = 99.5%. The true value could be anywhere in that range.
**Explain simply:**
- "Selection bias" means the people in your data were not picked fairly.
- A small sample is a second concern, but self-selection is the bigger one here.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you fix it?* Random sample with follow-ups to non-responders. 2) *Would 1,000 replies solve it?* Not fully; bias from who replies remains.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Blaming only the small size; a large self-selected sample is still biased.

### Q45 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A bank measures satisfaction by phoning only customers who contacted support in the last month. What is the most likely bias in the resulting score?
- (a) Selection bias, since people who contacted support had a problem and may not represent all customers.
- (b) Rounding bias.
- (c) Survivorship bias towards new customers.
- (d) No bias, since the sample is large.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you check who is in the sample before trusting the score.
**Say this in the interview:** "The sample is only customers who had a reason to call support, so it is not all customers. That is selection bias, answer (a); the score is likely lower than the true one."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. The target population is all customers; the sample is only those with a recent support contact.
2. Contacting support usually means a problem, so these customers are probably less satisfied, while silent happy customers are missed.
3. The direction of bias (likely too low) is a reasoned guess, not something the data proves.
4. Why the others fail: (b) rounding is a tiny arithmetic effect, not a sampling issue; (c) survivorship bias is about only seeing those who "survived" a process, not who contacted support; (d) a large sample cannot fix a biased way of choosing it.
**Check:** Hand-checked by reading the sampling rule against the population.
**Explain simply:**
- Selection bias: the way people got into the sample makes it differ from the group you care about.
- Sample size reduces random error, not bias.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you measure fairly?* Random sample of all customers. 2) *Could the bias go the other way?* Yes, if support fixed problems well, those customers may be unusually pleased.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (d); "large" sounds reassuring but is irrelevant to who was chosen.

### Q46 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Of the employees who left last year, 70% had not taken any training, so not taking training makes employees leave." What is the main flaw?

- (c) It covers only one year.
- (d) It says employees left.

**What the interviewer is checking:** Noticing a missing comparison group (base rate).

### Q47 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Resignations rose by 20% over a year, yet the attrition rate (resignations divided by average headcount) fell. Which statement must be true?

### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Which argument has the same flawed structure as: "All fraudulent claims are filed late. This claim was filed late. So it is fraudulent."
- (a) All members of the audit team hold the certificate. Meera holds the certificate. So Meera is on the audit team.
- (b) All interns have laptops. Arjun is an intern. So Arjun has a laptop.
- (c) No manager is an intern. Raj is a manager. So Raj is not an intern.
- (d) Some analysts are auditors. Divya is an analyst. So Divya is an auditor.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising the fallacy of affirming the consequent and matching structures.
**Say this in the interview:** "The original says all F are L, this one is L, so it is F. That reverses the rule. Option (a) does exactly the same with certificate holders. Answer (a)."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Original form: All F are L; x is L; therefore x is F. This is invalid because late claims could also be honest ones (L is bigger than F). It is called "affirming the consequent" (or the undistributed middle).
2. (a) has the form All A are C; Meera is C; so Meera is A. Same form, same flaw: other people may hold the certificate.
3. Why the others fail: (b) is a valid argument (All I are L; x is I; so x is L). (c) is also valid (no M is I, x is M, so x is not I). (d) is flawed, but differently: "some" gives no certainty about any particular analyst, so it is an unsupported leap from "some", not a reversal of an "all" rule.

**Explain simply:**
- "All F are L" does not mean "all L are F". Reversing a statement gives you the converse, which is not guaranteed.
- To test an argument, try to imagine true premises with a false conclusion.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Valid fix of the original?* "This claim was NOT filed late, so it is not fraudulent" (contrapositive). 2) *Name the fallacy.* Affirming the consequent.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (d) because it also feels flawed; the question asks for the same structure.

### Q50 ⭐⭐ 🟡 To evaluate the claim "the new shift pattern reduced absenteeism", which piece of information is most useful?

### Q52 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Attrition (%) by quarter: Q1 30, Q2 29, Q3 28, Q4 27. A pay hike took effect at the start of Q5, and attrition was 26 in Q5 and 25 in Q6. HR says the hike cut attrition by 1 point in Q5. What is the best reading of this data alone?
- (a) The hike reduced attrition by 1 point, since Q5 is 1 point below Q4.
- (b) Attrition had been falling 1 point per quarter before the hike, so the Q5 fall does not show any effect of the hike.
- (c) The hike had no effect and will have none in future.
- (d) The hike reduced attrition by 5 points, from 30 to 25.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Separating an existing trend from an intervention effect.
**Say this in the interview:** "Attrition was already dropping exactly 1 point each quarter. Q5 and Q6 follow the same line, so these numbers show no extra effect from the hike. Answer (b)."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Pre-hike changes: 30, 29, 28, 27 means -1 each quarter.
2. If the trend simply continued, Q5 would be 26 and Q6 25, exactly what we see.

### Q53 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Promotion data by tenure band: (Tenure under 3 years) trained 40 employees, 4 promoted; untrained 160 employees, 16 promoted. (Tenure 3 years or more) trained 160 employees, 48 promoted; untrained 40 employees, 12 promoted. Overall, trained employees are promoted at 26% and untrained at 14%. What does the data show?
- (a) Training raises promotion chances, as shown by 26% versus 14%.
- (b) Tenure is a confounder: within each tenure band trained and untrained employees are promoted at the same rate, so the overall gap comes from who gets trained.
- (c) Untrained employees are being denied promotions.
- (d) Promotion causes employees to take training.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); confounding is a named interview topic in a KDnuggets statistics-traps article (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Stratifying by a third variable to expose a confounder.
**Say this in the interview:** "Overall 26% versus 14%, but within each tenure band the rates are identical: 10% versus 10% and 30% versus 30%. Trained people are mostly long-tenure, and long-tenure people are promoted more. Answer (b)."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**

5. Why the others fail: (a) ignores the equal within-band rates; (c) no band shows a lower untrained rate; (d) the data has no timing, and equal rates within bands give no sign of any effect either way.

**Explain simply:**
- A confounder is a third factor linked to both the "cause" and the outcome.
- Comparing within groups of equal tenure removes it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does this prove training is useless?* No; it shows no difference here, and tenure may be only part of the story. 2) *Is this Simpson's paradox?* Close cousin: here the gap vanishes rather than reverses.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Trusting the overall 26% versus 14%; it is real arithmetic but not a like-for-like comparison.

### Q56 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Argument: "A vendor's tool correctly flagged 90 of the 100 fraud cases in testing, so 90% of the claims it flags will be fraud." What is the flaw?
- (a) It treats the share of fraud cases found as the share of flagged claims that are fraud; the second also depends on false alarms and how rare fraud is.
- (b) It tested only 100 cases.
- (c) Tools should never be tested.
- (d) It rounds 90 to a whole number.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you can tell "of all fraud, how much did the tool catch" (recall) from "of everything it flagged, how much was fraud" (precision).
**Say this in the interview:** "Catching 90% of fraud is a different number from 90% of flags being fraud. The second depends on how many honest claims get flagged and how rare fraud is. I'd ask for the false-alarm rate and the fraud rate."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Two different fractions: *recall* (also called sensitivity) = fraud caught / all fraud = 90/100. *Precision* = fraud among flagged / all flagged. The argument swaps one for the other.
2. Precision needs the false alarms (honest claims wrongly flagged). The question does not give them, so the 90% claim cannot be reached from the facts.
3. Illustration (our assumed numbers, not from the question): 10,000 claims, 1% fraud = 100 fraud cases. The tool catches 90. It wrongly flags 5% of the 9,900 honest claims = 495. Flagged = 585, of which 90 are fraud, so precision is 90/585 = **15.4%**, not 90%.
4. Change the assumptions and the answer moves: with a 1% false-alarm rate, precision is 90/189 = 47.6%; if fraud is 20% of claims and false alarms 5%, precision is 1,800/2,200 = 81.8%. So it depends on the fraud rate and false alarms.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Ask "90% of what?" The denominator in the premise (all fraud) differs from the denominator in the conclusion (all flagged). Different denominators means a flaw.
**Check:** the three precision figures above were computed by script (15.4%, 47.6%, 81.8%).
**Explain simply:**
- Recall answers "how much fraud did we catch?"; precision answers "when it shouts fraud, how often is it right?"
- When the thing you hunt is rare, even a small false-alarm rate buries the true hits (the "base rate" effect: the base rate is how common the event is overall).
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What would you ask the vendor for?* The false-positive rate and the real-world fraud rate, or simply a table of flagged versus actual fraud.
- *Is recall or precision more important?* It depends on the cost: missed fraud costs money; false flags cost investigator time and annoy customers.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing (b): 100 cases is a small sample, but that is not the logical flaw here (the swap of fractions is). (c) and (d) are absurd or irrelevant.

### Q57 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Team X's average score rose from 60 to 62 and Team Y's from 80 to 82. Last year X had 20 employees and Y had 80; this year X has 80 and Y has 20. What are the company averages last year and this year, and what does this show about "every team improved, so the company improved"?

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** weighted averages and Simpson's paradox (a trend in every group reverses in the combined data because the group sizes changed).
**Say this in the interview:** "Last year the company average was 76, this year it is 66. Both teams improved by 2, yet the company fell by 10, because most staff moved into the lower-scoring team. Team mix matters, so 'every team improved' does not mean the whole improved."
**Answer:** **Last year 76, this year 66 (a drop of 10 points), even though each team rose by 2.**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. A company average is a *weighted average*: each team's average times its headcount, summed, divided by total headcount.
2. Last year: (20 x 60 + 80 x 80) / 100 = (1,200 + 6,400) / 100 = **76**.
3. This year: (80 x 62 + 20 x 82) / 100 = (4,960 + 1,640) / 100 = **66**.
4. Team X is now 80% of staff (was 20%), and X scores lower. The mix shift costs far more than the +2 gain in each team adds.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Weighted average = 80 plus (share in X) x (60 minus 80). Last year 80 - 0.2 x 20 = 76; this year 80 - 0.8 x 20 + 2 = 66 (both teams are +2, so add 2 to the base).
**Check:** both averages computed by script (76.0 and 66.0).
**Explain simply:**
- Team averages can all rise while the total falls when people shift toward the lower group. This is Simpson's paradox (also called a mix effect).
- To compare fairly, hold the mix fixed: with last year's weights, this year's scores give 0.2 x 62 + 0.8 x 82 = 78, which is +2 over 76.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Is the company really worse off?* Not necessarily: the scores of like people did improve (+2 each). The fall comes from composition, so report both the mix-adjusted and raw figure.
- *What if headcounts had stayed 20/80?* Then the company average would be 78, a rise of 2.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Averaging the team averages (60 and 80 give 70; 62 and 82 give 72) and concluding the company rose by 2. A plain average ignores headcount.

### Q58 ⭐ 🟡 Why can an answer option be completely true and still not strengthen or weaken the given argument? Describe the "out of scope" and "irrelevant comparison" traps.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you judge options by their *relevance to the link between premise and conclusion*, not by whether they sound true.
**Say this in the interview:** "An option only strengthens or weakens if it changes how likely the conclusion is, given the premise. A true fact about a different topic, or about a comparison the argument never made, leaves that link untouched."
**Answer:** Truth and relevance are separate tests. An option is useful only if it bears on the **link** between the evidence and the conclusion.
**Explain simply:**
- Argument = premise (evidence) + conclusion + an unstated *assumption* joining them. Strengthen means making that assumption more believable; weaken means making it less believable.
- **Out of scope:** the option is about something the argument never discusses. Example: "Online ads raised our sales; therefore we should keep the ad budget." Option: "Our office moved to a new building last year." True, but it says nothing about whether ads cause sales.
- **Irrelevant comparison:** the option compares things the argument does not compare. Example: "Our app has 4.5 stars, so users like it." Option: "Our competitor's app has 4.7 stars." True, but the claim was about users liking this app, not about ranking against rivals.
- Worked mini-example: argument "Sales rose after the new ad, so the ad worked." Weakens: "A big festival also began that week" (an alternative cause). Strengthens: "Sales rose only in regions where the ad ran." Neither effect: "The ad cost 2 lakh rupees."
- Quick test: write the argument's assumption in one line, then ask "does this option make that line more or less likely?" If not, drop it.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Can an irrelevant option become relevant?* Yes, if the argument's conclusion is about a comparison (e.g., "best in the market"); then the competitor fact matters.
- *What is the opposite-direction trap?* An option that sounds supportive but actually weakens (or vice versa); read whether the question asks to strengthen or weaken.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking the option that sounds most true or most impressive; or one that is too extreme ("always", "never"); or one that merely repeats the premise.

### Q59 ⭐ 🟡 What is regression to the mean, and why can it make a training programme look effective when you train only the lowest scorers and re-test them later?

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you know that extreme scores partly reflect luck, and that selecting extremes builds in an apparent improvement.
**Say this in the interview:** "Any score is skill plus luck. Pick the lowest scorers and many were unlucky; on a re-test their luck averages out and they score higher with no training at all. Without a comparison group I can't credit the programme."
**Answer:** **Regression to the mean is the tendency for extreme results to be followed by less extreme ones, because part of any extreme result was chance. Selecting the lowest scorers guarantees some "improvement" on re-test even if the training does nothing.**
**Explain simply:**
- Score = true ability + random noise (mood, luck, guessing, a bad day). Low scorers are a mix of truly weak people and people who had bad noise.
- On a re-test the noise is drawn again, and the unlucky ones are no longer unlucky on average, so the group average moves up toward the overall mean.
- Worked example (a simulation we ran with assumed numbers, seed fixed, re-run in review): 100,000 people with true ability normally distributed, mean 70 and standard deviation 8, plus independent test noise with standard deviation 8 on each test. Take the bottom 10% on test 1: their average was 50.1. With no training, the same people averaged 60.1 on test 2, a "gain" of about 10 points from nothing.
- The fix: include a **control group** (similar low scorers who receive no training, or random assignment of who gets trained) and compare the *gain* of the trained group with the control's gain. Or select on one measure and evaluate on an independent one.
- The same effect is not limited to low scores: top performers tend to slip next time ("the sophomore slump").
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Does it mean training never works?* No: it only means the raw before/after gain overstates the effect; the true effect is the gain beyond the control group's.
- *When is the effect small?* When scores are very reliable (little noise) or when you do not select on the extreme.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Crediting the programme for the full jump; thinking regression means people literally "revert" because of some pull (it is a statistical artefact of selection plus noise); confusing it with the gambler's fallacy.

### Q60 ⭐ 🔴 An A/B test has 500 users per variant. Variant A had 10 conversions and variant B had 12 (2.0% versus 2.4%). The analyst writes: "B beats A by 20%, so B is better." Which is the best evaluation?
- (a) Proven, because 20% is a large uplift.
- (b) Not established: a gap of 2 conversions out of 500 per variant is small enough to arise by chance (a two-sided Fisher exact test gives p about 0.83).
- (c) Proven, because B is higher than A.
- (d) Can never be established with an A/B test.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you separate a relative uplift from statistical evidence and notice that the sample is too small for the gap.
**Say this in the interview:** "The 20% is relative: 2.4 versus 2.0 is only 0.4 percentage points, or 2 extra conversions. A Fisher exact test gives p about 0.83, so this gap is very plausibly noise. Not established; I'd need a larger sample."
**Answer:** **(b)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Relative uplift = 12/10 - 1 = 20%. Absolute difference = 2.4% - 2.0% = 0.4 percentage points (just 2 conversions).
2. Table: A has 10 converted and 490 not; B has 12 converted and 488 not. Fisher's exact test (it computes the exact chance of a gap this large or larger if both variants truly convert equally) gives a two-sided p-value of **0.830** (scipy, verified).
3. A p-value of 0.83 means a gap this big or bigger would occur about 83% of the time by chance alone. There is almost no evidence of a real difference. (A chi-square test without continuity correction gives about 0.67; with such small counts Fisher is the better choice, and both say "not significant".)
4. Not significant does not prove they are equal; it means the test cannot tell them apart. More data is needed.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Counts of 10 versus 12: the typical random wobble of a count near 10 is about square root of 10, roughly 3. A gap of 2 is well inside that wobble.
**Check:** p-value computed by script with a Fisher exact test on [[10,490],[12,488]] (0.8299).
**Explain simply:**
- Relative lift (20%) can be huge on a tiny base; always ask for the absolute difference and the sample size.
- p-value: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if there were truly no difference. Big p means "consistent with chance".
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *What would convince you?* A much larger test, planned in advance (power calculation), or a confidence interval for the difference that excludes zero. Even ten times the traffic at the same rates (100 versus 120 conversions in 5,000 each) gives p about 0.20, still not significant; it takes about a hundred times the traffic (1,000 versus 1,200 in 50,000 each, p below 0.0001) to make this small a gap convincing (both checked by script).
- *Is A therefore better?* No; the data do not favour either.
**Common mistakes / traps:** (a) is the "big percentage" trap; (c) is "higher means better" ignoring chance; (d) overreaches, since A/B tests can establish differences with enough data.

### Q61 ⭐ 🔴 A firm says its training programme has "a 95% success rate" because 38 of the 40 participants completed it. What is the key question to ask, and what would a better success measure be?

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you spot a metric that measures an activity (completion) instead of the outcome that matters, and the sample question (who is in the 40).
**Say this in the interview:** "38 of 40 is 95%, but that is completion, not success. I'd ask what 'success' is meant to achieve, and whether the 40 includes everyone who started. A better measure is an outcome such as a skills test gain or job performance, compared with a similar group that was not trained."
**Answer:** **Key question: "Success at what?" Completion shows people finished, not that they learned or performed better (and we should check whether dropouts were excluded). A better measure is an outcome measured before and after, compared with a control group.**
**Explain simply:**
- Arithmetic is fine: 38/40 = 95% (verified). The problem is the *definition* of success and the *denominator*.
- **Denominator question:** were the 40 everyone enrolled, or only people who stayed? If 60 enrolled and 20 dropped out, the completion rate is 38/60 = 63%. (Hypothetical numbers, to show the point.)
- **Outcome measures:** test scores before versus after; on-the-job metrics such as error rate or sales after 3 to 6 months; promotion or retention.
- **Comparison:** compare against similar people who did not do the programme (or ideally were randomly chosen not to), otherwise improvements may come from experience, selection of keen people, or regression to the mean (see Q59).
- Worked mini-example: trained group's average test score rises 10 points; a comparable untrained group rises 6; the programme's effect is about 4 points, not 10.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Is completion useless?* No; it is a useful engagement indicator, just not an effectiveness measure.
- *How big a sample do you need?* 40 is small; ask for confidence intervals, not only the headline percentage.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Accepting "95%" because the sums are right; ignoring survivorship (people who dropped out vanish from the figure); treating before/after improvement as proof of cause.

### Q62 ⭐ 🔴 A firm says its AI screening tool "shortlists better candidates" because shortlisted candidates scored 20% higher at interview than last year's shortlist. Which information is most useful to evaluate this?
- (a) Whether interview scores or the interview panel changed, and how candidates rejected by the tool would have scored.
- (b) How fast the tool runs.
- (c) How many candidates applied.
- (d) What the vendor charges.

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** whether you look for alternative explanations (changed measuring stick, different applicant pool) and the missing counterfactual (what the rejected would have done).
**Say this in the interview:** "A 20% higher score only counts if the interview scoring and panel are the same as last year, and if I know how the people the tool rejected would have scored. Otherwise the gain may come from a changed yardstick, not a better tool."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step reasoning:**
1. Claim: tool causes better shortlist. Evidence: this year's shortlist scored 20% higher than last year's.
2. Alternative explanations: a different panel or marking scheme (a more lenient scorer), a different applicant pool, interviewers influenced by knowing the tool picked them, or chance.
3. Option (a) tests both: whether the yardstick changed, and the **counterfactual** (what would have happened without the tool), shown by scoring a sample of rejected candidates. If rejected candidates would have scored as well, the tool adds little. It also checks whether the tool hides good candidates (only looking at those it shortlisted cannot reveal that).
4. Options (b), (c), (d) are about speed, volume and cost, which do not bear on whether the shortlist is better.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Ask what else changed besides the tool, and what the comparison group is. The option that answers these wins; the others are operational facts.
**Check:** reasoned by hand (a judgement-based item, nothing to compute).
**Explain simply:**
- A fair comparison needs the same measuring stick and a like-for-like group. "Better than last year" fails both unless shown otherwise.
- Selection effect: you only observe outcomes for people the tool chose; the rejected group is the missing control.
- Interview score is also only a proxy for what we care about, which is later job performance.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- *Best design to test it?* Randomly send some applicants through the old process and some through the tool, then compare later job performance.
- *Could (c) ever matter?* Slightly, if applicant volume changed (a different pool), but it does not by itself test quality.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing speed or cost because they are concrete and easy to find (irrelevant to quality); accepting the 20% at face value; confusing "shortlist scores rose" with "the tool is the cause".
