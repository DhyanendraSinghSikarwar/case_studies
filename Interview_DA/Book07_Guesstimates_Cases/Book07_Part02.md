### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of cups of chai sold in Hyderabad in a day.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); chai sizing is a plausible India interview topic per news coverage and prep material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you fix a clear definition (cups bought outside the home) and build the number from people or from stalls, with a cross-check.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will count only chai bought outside the home, estimate it two ways, from people and from stalls, and I expect roughly 45 lakh cups a day."
**Clarify first:** Does "chai" include coffee? (No, tea only.) Cups bought at stalls, cafes and offices only, not brewed at home? (Yes.) Hyderabad city, about 1 crore people in the urban area? (Yes.) A normal weekday.
**Approach:** Both. Top-down from people (quick), bottom-up from stalls (reality check). Two routes that land close give confidence.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 1 crore | rough metro size |
| Share who drink chai on a typical day | 50% | tea is the default drink |
| Cups per drinker per day | 2 | morning and evening |
| Share of cups bought outside home | 40% | stalls, offices, cafes |
| People per chai stall | 400 | one stall per few lanes |
| Cups per stall per day | 200 | busy stall, assumed |
**Calculation:**
1. Top-down: 1 crore x 50% = 50 lakh drinkers; x 2 cups = 1 crore cups; x 40% outside = 40 lakh cups.
2. Bottom-up: 1 crore / 400 = 25,000 stalls; x 200 cups = 50 lakh cups.
3. The routes differ by 25%; take the middle, 45 lakh.
**Result:** about 45 lakh cups a day (plausible range 30 to 60 lakh).
**Sanity check:** 45 lakh over 1 crore people is about 0.45 outside cups per person per day, roughly one outside cup for every second person. Believable for a tea-loving city.
**Sensitivity:** The "share bought outside" moves the answer most: at 30% the top-down figure drops to 30 lakh, at 50% it rises to 50 lakh. Drinker share matters less (40% to 60% gives 32 to 48 lakh).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Revenue?* At an assumed Rs 10 a cup, 45 lakh cups is about Rs 4.5 crore a day. 2) *Weekend difference?* Offices close, so fewer cups; I would trim 10 to 15%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Counting home-brewed cups with stall cups; not stating the definition; giving one route and no check.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of cars in Hyderabad.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "cars in a city" is a standard prep-site guesstimate (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Household-based segmentation and awareness that businesses also own cars.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will go through households, then add commercial cars: about 6 lakh cars, with a range of 4.5 to 7.5 lakh."
**Clarify first:** Cars only (not two-wheelers, autos, trucks)? Private plus taxis? Hyderabad urban area, assumed 1 crore people? Registered cars on the road today.
**Approach:** Bottom-up by households (people own cars as households do), with a per-1,000-people check.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Households | 25 lakh | 1 crore people / about 4 per home |
| Households owning a car | 20% | middle and upper income only |
| Cars per owning household | 1.1 | few own two |
| Commercial/taxi/company uplift | 10% on top | cabs, fleet, company cars |
**Calculation:**
1. Owning households: 25 lakh x 20% = 5 lakh.
2. Private cars: 5 lakh x 1.1 = 5.5 lakh.
3. Add 10% commercial: 5.5 lakh x 1.10 = 6.05 lakh.
**Result:** about 6 lakh cars (range 4.5 to 7.5 lakh, from ownership of 15% to 25%).
**Sanity check:** 6 lakh / 1 crore = about 60 cars per 1,000 people, i.e. one car per 16 people. Reasonable for an Indian metro, well below a Western city.
**Sensitivity:** Car ownership rate dominates: each 5 points of ownership shifts the total by about 1.5 lakh.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Two-wheelers?* Many times more; see Q3. 2) *Growth?* Estimate cars added per year and compare with registrations.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Skipping commercial cars; mixing city limits with the wider metro region; using people instead of households.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of two-wheelers in Bengaluru.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a staple vehicle-count guesstimate on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Choosing households as the unit and sanity-checking against a per-person figure.
**Say this in the interview:** "Households times two-wheeler ownership, plus a business uplift: about 26 lakh two-wheelers."
**Clarify first:** Scooters, motorbikes and mopeds all count; e-bikes included? (Yes, small share.) Bengaluru urban, assumed 1.3 crore people. Vehicles in use today.
**Approach:** Bottom-up by households, checked by vehicles per 1,000 people.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 1.3 crore | rough urban size |
| People per household | 4 | typical urban home |
| Households with a two-wheeler | 60% | the main personal vehicle |
| Two-wheelers per such household | 1.2 | some own two |
| Commercial uplift | 10% | delivery riders, small business |
**Calculation:**
1. Households: 1.3 crore / 4 = 32.5 lakh.
2. With a two-wheeler: 32.5 lakh x 60% = 19.5 lakh.
3. Vehicles: 19.5 lakh x 1.2 = 23.4 lakh.
4. Add 10%: 23.4 lakh x 1.10 = 25.7 lakh.
**Result:** about 26 lakh (range 21 to 30 lakh, from ownership of 50% to 70%).
**Sanity check:** 26 lakh / 1.3 crore = about 200 per 1,000 people, one two-wheeler per five people. Plausible given the heavy two-wheeler use in Indian cities.
**Sensitivity:** Ownership share is the swing factor; 10 points up or down changes the total by about 4 lakh.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Cars vs two-wheelers ratio?* I would need Bengaluru's car count; using Hyderabad's figures from Q2 and Q21 as a rough guide (about 20 lakh two-wheelers to 6 lakh cars), it is about 3 to 4 two-wheelers per car. 2) *HR angle?* Commute allowance policy depends on this mix.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming one vehicle per person; forgetting the same household has both a car and a two-wheeler.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of coffee shops in Bengaluru.
**Asked at:** Mu Sigma guesstimate rounds are reported to include "coffee shops in Bangalore" (prep-site report, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Defining "coffee shop" and sizing from demand (cups) divided by shop capacity.
**Say this in the interview:** "I will size cups demanded and divide by cups a shop sells: about 9,000 coffee shops."
**Clarify first:** Sit-down cafes and coffee counters, including chains, independents and filter-coffee joints; exclude tea stalls and restaurants where coffee is a side item. Today's count in Bengaluru urban, 1.3 crore people.
**Approach:** Top-down demand over per-shop capacity, then a people-per-shop cross-check.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 1.3 crore | rough urban size |
| Share buying coffee at a shop on a day | 15% | office crowds and students |
| Cups per buyer per day | 1.3 | most have one |
| Cups per shop per day | 250 | mix of small and big outlets |
| People per shop (check) | 1,500 | dense areas have more |
**Calculation:**
1. Buyers: 1.3 crore x 15% = 19.5 lakh.
2. Cups: 19.5 lakh x 1.3 = 25.35 lakh a day.
3. Shops: 25.35 lakh / 250 = about 10,100.
4. Check: 1.3 crore / 1,500 = about 8,700.
**Result:** about 9,000 shops (range 6,000 to 13,000).
**Sanity check:** One shop per 1,500 people is about 1 per 400 households: you would pass several on a main road, which fits.
**Sensitivity:** Cups per shop matters most: 150 gives about 17,000 shops, 400 gives about 6,300.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which areas have most?* IT corridors and student areas. 2) *Chains vs independents?* Independents dominate by count.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Not defining the shop; using the wrong cups per shop; forgetting that 15% buyers is per day, not per month.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of petrol stations in India.
**Asked at:** Mu Sigma guesstimate rounds are reported to include "petrol stations in India" (prep-site report, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Building fuel demand by vehicle type and dividing by what one pump can sell.
**Say this in the interview:** "Total fuel demand per day divided by sales of one station: about 1 lakh stations, range 80,000 to 1.4 lakh."
**Clarify first:** Retail outlets selling petrol and diesel (not CNG-only or industrial depots). India-wide, now.
**Approach:** Top-down by fuel demand per vehicle class, divided by a typical outlet's daily litres. People-per-station is the check.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Two-wheelers | 20 crore, 0.75 litre a day | about 30 km at 40 km per litre |
| Cars | 4 crore, 1.5 litres a day | lower daily use |
| Trucks and buses | 1 crore, 20 litres a day | heavy diesel use |
| Sales per station | 4,000 litres a day | average outlet |
**Calculation:**
1. Two-wheelers: 20 crore x 0.75 = 15 crore litres.
2. Cars: 4 crore x 1.5 = 6 crore litres.
3. Heavy vehicles: 1 crore x 20 = 20 crore litres.
4. Total: 41 crore litres a day.
5. Stations: 41 crore / 4,000 = 1.03 lakh.
**Result:** about 1 lakh stations (3,000 litres per station gives 1.37 lakh; 5,000 gives 82,000).
**Sanity check:** 140 crore people / 1 lakh = one station per 14,000 people, a believable spacing along roads and in towns.
**Sensitivity:** Litres per station and the truck assumption move the answer most.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *EV impact?* Small today; it would trim demand slowly. 2) *Highways vs city?* City stations sell more per outlet.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting trucks (the biggest fuel user here); one national daily figure with no breakdown.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of smartphones sold in Delhi in a year.
**Asked at:** appears on a practice platform (Dataford) listing; attribution to a company comes from a search summary only (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Using users divided by replacement cycle rather than guessing sales directly.
**Say this in the interview:** "Smartphone users divided by how often they replace the phone: about 40 lakh a year."
**Clarify first:** New units sold to Delhi residents, all price bands, no second-hand or refurbished. Delhi at about 2 crore people. One calendar year.
**Approach:** Top-down from the user base and replacement cycle; a check on sales per person.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 2 crore | rough city size |
| Share aged 15+ | 75% | people able to own one |
| Of them with a smartphone | 75% | high in a metro |
| Replacement cycle | 3 years | typical for a phone |
| Extra from NCR shoppers | 10% | Delhi markets serve nearby towns |
**Calculation:**
1. Adults: 2 crore x 75% = 1.5 crore.
2. Users: 1.5 crore x 75% = 1.125 crore.
3. Yearly replacements: 1.125 crore / 3 = 37.5 lakh.
4. Add 10%: 37.5 lakh x 1.10 = 41 lakh.
**Result:** about 40 lakh a year (31 lakh at a 4-year cycle; 62 lakh at 2 years, both including the 10% NCR uplift).
**Sanity check:** 40 lakh / 2 crore = 0.2 phones per resident a year, about one purchase per five people.
**Sensitivity:** The replacement cycle is the lever (2 vs 4 years doubles the answer).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Value in rupees?* Multiply by an assumed average price. 2) *Festive effect?* Sales skew to the festive quarter.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating all residents as buyers; ignoring replacement versus first-time buyers.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of sweet shops in a town of 5 lakh people.
**Asked at:** reported in prep-site summaries of Flipkart interviews (snippet-level, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Linking household spend to the revenue of one shop, and defining which shops count.
**Say this in the interview:** "Total sweet spend divided by what one shop sells: about 150 shops."
**Clarify first:** Dedicated sweet shops only (not bakeries, restaurants or tiny stalls)? A normal town, all-year view including festivals.
**Approach:** Demand over supply per shop, then a people-per-shop check.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| People per household | 4.5 | town household |
| Sweet spend per household per year | Rs 4,000 | festival heavy |
| Share bought from dedicated shops | 70% | rest home-made or packaged |
| Turnover per shop | Rs 20 lakh a year | about Rs 5,500 a day |
**Calculation:**
1. Households: 5 lakh / 4.5 = about 1.11 lakh.
2. Spend: 1.11 lakh x Rs 4,000 = Rs 44 crore.
3. Shop sales: Rs 44 crore x 70% = Rs 31 crore.
4. Shops: Rs 31 crore / Rs 20 lakh = about 155.
5. Check: 5 lakh / 3,000 people per shop = about 170.
**Result:** about 150 shops (100 to 300).
**Sanity check:** One shop per 3,000 people is about one per neighbourhood market.
**Sensitivity:** Turnover per shop: Rs 10 lakh gives about 310 shops; Rs 40 lakh about 78.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Festival season?* Sales spike but the shop count does not. 2) *Rural?* Fewer, smaller shops.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Counting all food outlets; mixing monthly and yearly spend.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What clarifying questions do you ask before sizing a market (units or rupees, geography, time period, what counts as the product)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); clarifying scope is expected at the start of every guesstimate round (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you fix the scope before computing, so the number answers the question asked.
**Say this in the interview:** "I ask four things first: what exactly counts as the product, the unit, the place, and the time period. Then I repeat the scope back."
**Explain simply:**
- **Product definition:** What is in and out? Chai only, or all hot drinks? A "car" with or without taxis? Without this, two people give numbers that cannot be compared.
- **Unit:** units (cups, cars) or rupees (revenue, called gross order value if it is orders)? Rupees need a price, so extra steps.
- **Geography:** city limits or the wider metro; India-wide or one tier of cities.
- **Time period:** per day, week or year; today or a future year; normal day or festival.
- **Purpose and who sells or buys:** why is the number needed? A launch needs the reachable segment, not everyone.
- **Mini-example:** "Chai in Hyderabad" becomes "cups of tea bought outside the home, Hyderabad urban, per normal weekday, in cups". Now the formula is clear.
- **Practice step:** pick any five guesstimate questions and write the four scope lines for each in under a minute.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many questions?* Two to four; ask the ones that change the answer. 2) *What if the interviewer says "your choice"?* State your pick and why.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Skipping clarification; asking ten questions and losing time; not repeating the scope back.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you decide between a top-down and a bottom-up approach for a market-size question?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); top-down versus bottom-up is standard advice in prep material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you choose a method on purpose and know how to cross-check one with the other.
**Say this in the interview:** "I use top-down when I know the total population and a simple share; bottom-up when the market is made of countable units like stores or stalls. If time allows, I do both."
**Explain simply:**
- **Top-down:** start from a big total (population or households) and cut it down by shares. Fast; weak when the shares are guesses.
- **Bottom-up:** start from one unit (a stall, a store, a household) and multiply by how many exist. Slower; grounded in something you can picture.
- **Choose top-down when** the product is used by most people (chai, smartphones) and you can slice by simple shares.
- **Choose bottom-up when** supply is countable (petrol stations, sweet shops) or when you can estimate one shop's capacity well.
- **Mini-example:** chai in Hyderabad. Top-down: 1 crore x 50% drinkers x 2 cups x 40% outside = 40 lakh. Bottom-up: 25,000 stalls x 200 cups = 50 lakh. Close, so I trust about 45 lakh.
- **Practice step:** for every question, write both routes in one line each before you start.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which is more accurate?* Neither alone; agreement of both is the evidence. 2) *Demand-side vs supply-side?* Top-down from people is demand; stalls and shops are supply.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Doing only one route; picking top-down for a niche product where shares are unknown.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Assume Hyderabad has 25 lakh households, 20% of them own at least one car, and a car-owning household owns 1.1 cars on average. How many cars are there, and what would you check next?
(These numbers are exercise inputs, not facts.)
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); numeric exercise built for practice.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clean multiplication of a chain of shares and sensible next checks.
**Say this in the interview:** "25 lakh x 20% x 1.1 gives 5.5 lakh private cars. Next I would add commercial cars and compare with cars per 1,000 people."
**Definition in plain words:** Cars = households x share owning a car x cars per owning household.
**Worked answer:**
1. Owning households: 25 lakh x 0.20 = 5 lakh.
2. Cars: 5 lakh x 1.1 = 5.5 lakh.
**Result:** 5,50,000 (5.5 lakh) private cars.
**Check:** Assuming a population of about 1 crore (my own assumption, not given), 5.5 lakh is 55 cars per 1,000 people. What I would check next: (a) company, taxi and fleet cars, not in household counts; (b) whether 25 lakh households means city limits or the wider region; (c) whether 1.1 is per owning household (as stated), not per all households; (d) registered versus actually driven cars (old, scrapped or shifted vehicles).
**Caveats:** Adding 10% commercial cars would give about 6 lakh. The 20% ownership rate is the weakest input; at 15% or 25% the answer is 4.1 or 6.9 lakh.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If 1.1 meant all households?* Then cars = 25 lakh x 1.1 = 27.5 lakh, clearly too high, so wording matters. 2) *How to validate?* Compare with registration counts if available.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Multiplying 1.1 by all households; forgetting commercial cars.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Assume a chai stall sells 200 cups a day at Rs 10 a cup and a locality has 150 such stalls. What is the daily chai revenue of the locality in rupees and in lakhs?
(These numbers are exercise inputs, not facts.)
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); numeric exercise built for practice.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Accurate arithmetic and the rupee-to-lakh conversion (1 lakh = 1,00,000).
**Say this in the interview:** "One stall earns Rs 2,000 a day; 150 stalls give Rs 3,00,000, which is Rs 3 lakh a day."
**Definition in plain words:** Revenue = cups x price. Locality revenue = revenue per stall x number of stalls.
**Worked answer:**
1. Per stall: 200 x Rs 10 = Rs 2,000 a day.
2. Locality: Rs 2,000 x 150 = Rs 3,00,000.
3. In lakhs: 3,00,000 / 1,00,000 = 3.
**Result:** Rs 3,00,000 = Rs 3 lakh a day.
**Check:** Total cups = 200 x 150 = 30,000; x Rs 10 = Rs 3,00,000. Same answer by cups first. Over 30 days that is Rs 90 lakh a month.
**Caveats:** This is gross revenue, not profit (milk, tea, gas, rent are not subtracted). All stalls selling an identical 200 cups is a simplification.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Profit?* Needs a margin assumption. 2) *Is 30,000 cups plausible?* Depends on locality population; that is the sanity check.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Mixing "lakh" and "thousand" zeros; reporting revenue as profit.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Estimate the number of Swiggy or Zomato delivery partners needed in Hyderabad.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); Swiggy had no dedicated guesstimate report in the sources; the question is a delivery-operations practice case (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Linking orders to rider capacity at peak, not at the daily average.
**Say this in the interview:** "I size peak-hour orders, divide by orders a rider can do in an hour, and then scale up for riders who are not online at peak: about 15,000 to 17,000 partners."
**Clarify first:** Both platforms together, food only, partners on the rolls (not only on the road), a normal day in Hyderabad.
**Approach:** Bottom-up from orders per day, then peak capacity.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Households | 25 lakh | about 1 crore people |
| Households ordering food via apps | 30% | metro usage |
| Orders per ordering household per month | 5 | assumed |
| Share of orders in peak hours | 60% | lunch and dinner |
| Peak window | 5 hours | two windows of 2.5 hours |
| Orders per rider per peak hour | 1.5 | about 40 minutes each |
| Share of partners online at peak | 60% | part-time, multi-app |
**Calculation:**
1. Orders a month: 25 lakh x 30% x 5 = 37.5 lakh; per day 1.25 lakh.
2. Peak orders: 1.25 lakh x 60% = 75,000 over 5 hours = 15,000 an hour.
3. Riders on road at peak: 15,000 / 1.5 = 10,000.
4. Partners needed on the rolls: 10,000 / 0.60 = about 16,700.
**Result:** about 16,000 partners (12,500 to 25,000 depending on rider speed, 2.0 or 1.0 orders an hour).
**Sanity check:** 1.25 lakh orders a day over about 16,700 partners is under 8 orders per partner per day, normal for a mix of full and part-timers.
**Sensitivity:** Orders per rider per hour (distance, waiting at restaurants) moves it most.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Rain?* Orders rise and speed falls, so demand for riders spikes. 2) *Cost?* Multiply partners by a payout per order.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Sizing on the daily average instead of peak; ignoring part-timers.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Estimate the annual market size for online grocery delivery in a metropolitan city like Bengaluru.
**Asked at:** appears in a Dataford practice set labelled for a Tredence business-analytics round; the company attribution is from a search summary (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Two routes (spend share and order count) that agree, and a clear online-grocery definition.
**Say this in the interview:** "Roughly Rs 4,500 crore a year, from household grocery spend times online share, cross-checked with orders per household."
**Clarify first:** Includes quick commerce and scheduled delivery; groceries only (not restaurant food); gross value in rupees a year; Bengaluru urban, 1.3 crore people.
**Approach:** Top-down by spend share, bottom-up by order count.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Households | 32.5 lakh | 1.3 crore / 4 |
| Monthly grocery spend per household | Rs 9,000 | assumed |
| Online share of grocery spend | 12% | metros are ahead |
| Active online households | 35% | order at least monthly |
| Orders a month per active household | 6 | assumed |
| Average order value | Rs 600 | assumed |
**Calculation:**
1. Top-down: 32.5 lakh x Rs 9,000 x 12 months = Rs 35,100 crore total grocery; x 12% = Rs 4,212 crore.
2. Bottom-up: 32.5 lakh x 35% = 11.4 lakh active households; x 6 orders x Rs 600 x 12 months = Rs 4,914 crore.
3. Middle: about Rs 4,560 crore.
**Result:** about Rs 4,500 crore a year (Rs 2,800 to 5,600 crore if online share is 8% to 16%).
**Sanity check:** Rs 4,500 crore / 32.5 lakh households is about Rs 14,000 per household a year, about Rs 1,150 a month, one top-up order a fortnight. Plausible.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Quick commerce share?* Ask what share of orders are 10-minute delivery. 2) *Growth?* Assume yearly growth rate and project.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using population instead of households; counting restaurants.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Estimate the size of the diaper market in India in rupees per year.
**Asked at:** diaper questions appear in India campus and prep material for guesstimates (unverified); no company-specific report found.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Segmenting by age, usage and price, and recognising that only a share of babies use diapers.
**Say this in the interview:** "Babies under two, times the share using diapers, times diapers a day, times price: about Rs 6,300 crore."
**Clarify first:** Baby diapers only (adult and pants-style counted separately), retail value in rupees, one year, India.
**Approach:** Bottom-up from babies and usage.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**
| Input | Value | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Births a year | 2.4 crore | rough national figure |
| Diaper-wearing age | 2 years | so about 2 birth cohorts |
| Share of babies using disposable diapers | 15% | urban, higher income |
| Diapers per day for a user | 3 | not used all day |
| Price per diaper | Rs 8 | mixed brands |
**Calculation:**
1. Babies: 2.4 crore x 2 = 4.8 crore.
2. Users: 4.8 crore x 15% = 72 lakh.

- TAM (total addressable market): the full yearly spend if every possible customer bought.
- SAM (serviceable available market): the slice of TAM you can serve given product, region and price.
- SOM (serviceable obtainable market): the share of SAM you can actually win in the near term given competitors and capacity.
- A guesstimate such as "size the market for payroll software" normally stops at TAM; the interviewer may then ask you to narrow to SAM and SOM.
**Worked mini-example (illustrative numbers):** TAM for payroll software = Rs 1,000 crore. A start-up serving only small firms in metros reaches 40%: SAM = Rs 400 crore. It can win 5% of that in year 1: SOM = Rs 20 crore. Each layer is a subset of the one before.
**Practice step:** Take any Part 2 question and write TAM, SAM and SOM beside the answer with one filter each.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) Which is used for a revenue target? SOM. 2) Which is largest? TAM. 3) Can SAM grow? Yes, by adding regions, products or channels.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating TAM as revenue you will earn; filters that overlap so SAM is not a true subset; forgetting to say which layer your number is.

### Q51 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Assume an online grocery app has 8 lakh monthly users in a city, 40% order in a month, and an ordering user places 3 orders at Rs 600 each. What is the monthly gross order value in crores?
(These numbers are exercise inputs, not facts.)
**Asked at:** an online-grocery market question is listed on a practice platform as a Tredence business analytics practice item (search summary only, unverified); this version with inline numbers is an exercise.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clean unit handling (lakh, crore) and a funnel chain.
**Say this in the interview:** "Users who order, times orders, times basket value. That is Rs 57.6 crore of gross order value a month."
**Definition in plain words:** Gross order value (GOV) is the total value of all orders placed before discounts, cancellations or returns are removed. Formula: GOV = ordering users x orders per user x average order value.
**Worked answer:**
1. Ordering users = 8 lakh x 40% = 3.2 lakh.
2. Orders = 3.2 lakh x 3 = 9.6 lakh.
3. GOV = 9.6 lakh x Rs 600 = Rs 57.6 crore (1 crore = 100 lakh; 9.6 lakh x Rs 600 = Rs 5,760 lakh = Rs 57.6 crore).

**Result:** 3.2 lakh ordering users, 9.6 lakh orders, Rs 57.6 crore a month.
**Check:** Per monthly user: 57.6 crore / 8 lakh = Rs 720 per user per month, which equals 40% x 3 x 600. Edge test: at 30% ordering, Rs 43.2 crore; at 50%, Rs 72 crore.
**Caveats:** GOV is not revenue (the app keeps only a margin or fee); "3 orders" is an average among orderers; "monthly users" may mean app opens, not buyers.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Annual?* Rs 57.6 crore x 12 = Rs 691 crore, ignoring seasonality. 2) *Which lever matters?* All three multiply, so a 10% gain in any one raises GOV 10%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating 40% as 40 lakh; lakh to crore slips; applying 3 orders to all 8 lakh users.

### Q54 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you adjust a market-size estimate for seasonality, such as festival months?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a method question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you avoid multiplying one month by 12.
**Say this in the interview:** "I split the year into peak and normal months with a seasonal index, then add them, instead of scaling one month by 12."
**Explain simply:**
- A seasonal index is a month's sales divided by the normal month. A festival month at 1.5 means 50% above normal.
- Steps: estimate a normal month; set an index for each month group; annual = normal month x sum of indices.
- If your data is from a festival month, divide by its index to find the normal month first.
- Say what the festival does: more volume, higher basket, or just shifting demand (pull-forward) from the next month.

**Worked mini-example:** Normal month Rs 100 crore; two festival months at 1.5 = 150. Annual = 10 x 100 + 2 x 150 = Rs 1,300 crore. Naive 12 x 150 = 1,800 (38% too high); naive 12 x 100 = 1,200 (about 8% too low). Illustrative numbers.
**Practice step:** Take any monthly sales or attendance series and compute each month divided by the yearly average.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Festival dates move?* Use a festival-based window, not calendar months. 2) *HR example?* Hiring and attendance dips around festivals.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Annualising a peak month; forgetting the post-festival dip; one index for all categories (sweets and cement differ).

### Q83 ⭐ 🟡 How do you use a small real data sample, such as 50 shop surveys, to improve a market-size guesstimate?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a method question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn a small sample into a corrected assumption with a confidence range and fair weights.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would replace my guessed inputs with the sample average, show a range, and weight the shop types to match the real mix. That prevents bias."

**Explain simply:**
- Use the sample to replace one assumption (for example daily sales per shop), not the whole model.
- Report a range, not a single figure: the average plus or minus about two standard errors (a standard error is the spread of the sample divided by the square root of the sample size).
- Weight shop types by how common they are, because surveys over-visit big shops.
**Worked mini-example (made-up exercise inputs):** 50 surveyed shops sell an average of 80 units a day with a spread (standard deviation) of 40.
1. Standard error = 40 / square root of 50 = 5.66.
2. 95% range = 80 plus or minus 1.96 x 5.66 = about 69 to 91.
3. My earlier guess of 100 is outside this range, so I lower it.
4. Shop types: 30 small shops average 50, 15 medium 90, 5 large 230. Simple sample average = (1,500 + 1,350 + 1,150) / 50 = 80, matching step 1.
5. If the real mix is 70% small, 25% medium, 5% large, the weighted average = 0.7 x 50 + 0.25 x 90 + 0.05 x 230 = 69. The simple average of 80 overstates by about 16%.
**Practice step:** Take any guesstimate you made, pick one input, survey ten people or shops and see how far the answer moves.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is 50 enough?* For the average yes, if the shops are chosen fairly; with 200 shops the range halves from plus or minus 11 to plus or minus 5.5. 2) *What if the sample is biased?* Say so, and weight or re-sample.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Trusting a convenience sample; using a simple average when shop sizes differ; reporting one number with no range.

1. Total analyst training = 3.5 lakh x Rs 20,000 = Rs 700 crore.

### Q90 ⭐ 🔴 Estimate how many 10-minute grocery dark stores Hyderabad can support.

Demand says about 100 stores across all platforms; covering the city takes about 100 stores for even one platform. So I say about 100 in total, which means several platforms would each run below break-even."

**Clarify first:** All platforms combined, or one? (All.) The city only, not the whole metro region? (The urban core, about 1 crore people.) Break-even throughput of a store? (Assume 1,000 orders a day.)
**Approach:** Demand-led count, checked by coverage area.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Households | 25 lakh |
| Addressable (income, app use) | 40% = 10 lakh |
| Active quick-commerce households | 5 lakh (half of addressable; the same 20% of households as Q46) |

### Q91 ⭐ 🔴 A client asks you to size the market for a new cold-chain startup for vegetables. How do you structure the estimate and what data would you request?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a structure-first sizing case.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you build a funnel from production to the startup's reachable revenue, and know the data to request.
**Say this in the interview:** "I walk from production to perishables to what cold chain can serve and the price per kg. Illustratively that gives about Rs 1,900 crore for the reachable market, and I list the data I need."

**Clarify first:** Which vegetables? (High-perishable: leafy greens, tomatoes.) Which stage: farm to market, or market to retail? (Farm to city market.) Revenue is a service fee per kg? (Yes.)
**Structure:** Production, marketed share, perishable share, cold-chain penetration today, startup's reachable share, price per kg.
**What I would check, in order:** (1) Production and marketing data by state. (2) Post-harvest loss by crop. (3) Current cold-chain capacity and use. (4) Price per kg that farmers and buyers will pay. (5) Competitor pricing.
**Numeric work (illustrative, all assumed, unverified):**
1. Production 200 million tonnes; marketed 80% = 160 million tonnes.
2. High-perishable 40% = 64 million tonnes.
3. Reachable in the next years 10% = 6.4 million tonnes.
4. Fee Rs 3 per kg: 6.4 million tonnes = 640 crore kg; x Rs 3 = Rs 1,920 crore.
5. Range: reachable 6% to 15% gives Rs 1,150 to 2,880 crore.
**If the data shows...:** Low current penetration but high loss means a strong need; buyers unwilling to pay Rs 3 a kg means the model needs a different payer (retailers or exporters).
**Recommendation:** Start with one cluster and one crop, test willingness to pay, and measure loss reduction and price per kg.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why start with a city cluster?* Density lowers cost per kg. 2) *Which metric proves success?* Wastage cut in percentage points and revenue per kg.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Sizing all agriculture; ignoring who pays; no data request.

### Q92 ⭐ 🔴 You estimated a market at 50,000 crore rupees and the client says the real figure is 10,000 crore. How do you respond and revise?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a reconciliation scenario.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you stay calm, find where the gap comes from, and revise with evidence rather than defend or cave in.
**Say this in the interview:** "A five times gap is large, so I would first align definitions, then compare my drivers with theirs one by one to find which accounts for the difference."

**Clarify first:** What does their number cover (revenue or gross value, which year, which segments)? How was it calculated and from which source? Is my scope the same?
**Structure:** Write both estimates as the same chain (users x share x price) and compare each link.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Definitions: total potential market versus the part that can actually be served; retail value versus company revenue.
2. Units: a lakh versus crore slip would give 100 times, so it cannot explain a 5 times gap.
3. Drivers: for instance a 5 times gap could split as 2 times in users, 1.5 times in share and 1.67 times in price (2 x 1.5 x 1.67 = 5).
4. Source quality for each of their inputs.
**If the data shows...:** One driver explains most of the gap: adopt the better-evidenced value for it. Gap is only definitions: restate both under one definition. Both defensible: present a range, 10,000 to 50,000, with the conditions for each end.
**Numeric work:** 50,000 / 10,000 = 5 times; if the client's users and price are confirmed, I revise my user and price inputs, which brings the estimate to about 10,000 to 15,000 crore.
**Recommendation:** Revise openly, document what changed and why, and propose a data check (for example 20 customer calls) to settle the open driver.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the client is wrong?* Ask for their source politely; the number could be a serviceable market only. 2) *Does it hurt credibility to revise?* No, revising on evidence builds it.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Defending a number; accepting theirs without questions; changing everything at once.

### Q93 ⭐ 🔴 Estimate the market size for an employee wellness app sold to Indian companies with more than 1,000 staff.
HR flavour.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); HR-flavoured gap-fill question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you size a B2B market from number of companies and employees, with price per employee and adoption.
**Say this in the interview:** "Eligible employees times price per employee per year times the share of companies who buy. I get about Rs 270 crore of actual spend and Rs 1,080 crore of full potential."

**Clarify first:** Annual subscription revenue from companies? (Yes.) Priced per employee per year? (Yes.) Which services: mental health, fitness, health checks? (An app bundle.)
**Approach:** Bottom-up B2B.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Companies with over 1,000 staff | 6,000 |
| Average staff each | 3,000 |
| Price per employee per year | Rs 600 (Rs 50 a month) |
| Companies buying | 25% |

**Calculation:**
1. Employees = 6,000 x 3,000 = 1.8 crore.
2. Full potential = 1.8 crore x Rs 600 = Rs 1,080 crore.
3. With 25% adoption: 45 lakh employees x Rs 600 = Rs 270 crore.
**Result:** Potential Rs 1,080 crore; realistic spend about Rs 270 crore a year (range Rs 160 to 760 crore, from 15% adoption at Rs 600 to 35% at Rs 1,200).
**Sanity check:** Rs 600 a year is under Rs 2 a day, a plausible HR benefit cost.
**Sensitivity:** Adoption and price both matter; employee count is the most solid.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What drives adoption?* Employee usage and visible results (for example, lower attrition). 2) *How would you reduce churn?* Show engagement data to the HR head.

### Q94 ⭐ 🔴 Estimate the annual market for background-verification checks on new hires in India.
HR flavour.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); HR-flavoured gap-fill question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you count joiners, the share verified, checks per person, and a blended price.
**Say this in the interview:** "New joiners times share verified times checks per person times price per check. I get about 3.6 crore checks and Rs 900 crore."

**Clarify first:** Verification by a vendor, not in-house? (Yes.) Which checks: identity, address, education, past employment, criminal records? (Yes.) Gig platform onboarding included? (Yes, under blue-collar.)
**Approach:** Two segments, with different check counts.
**Assumptions (all assumed / approximate, unverified):**

| Input | White-collar | Blue-collar and gig |
|---|---|---|
| New joiners a year | 90 lakh | 1.5 crore |
| Share verified | 70% | 35% |
| Checks each | 4 | 2 |

Average price per check: Rs 250 (low for identity checks, higher for criminal and education checks).
**Calculation:**
1. White-collar: 90 lakh x 70% = 63 lakh people x 4 = 2.52 crore checks.
2. Blue-collar: 1.5 crore x 35% = 52.5 lakh x 2 = 1.05 crore checks.
3. Total = 3.57 crore checks.
4. Value = 3.57 crore x Rs 250 = Rs 892 crore.
**Result:** About 3.6 crore checks, about Rs 900 crore a year (range Rs 535 to 1,250 crore, with a price of Rs 150 to 350).
**Sanity check:** 3.57 crore checks over 1.15 crore verified people is about 3.1 checks per person, consistent with the mix. The Rs 250 blended price is dominated by cheap checks.
**Sensitivity:** Price per check is the biggest lever (Rs 150 to Rs 350 gives Rs 535 to Rs 1,250 crore); share verified is the second.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you use the HR data?* Hiring volume by role from HR systems. 2) *Does verification delay joining?* Yes; turnaround time is a key service metric.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming all hires are verified; one price for all checks; ignoring blue-collar and gig volumes.
