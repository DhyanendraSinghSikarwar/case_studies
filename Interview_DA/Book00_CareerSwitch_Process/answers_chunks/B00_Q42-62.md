### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What happens in a live SQL round and how should I behave in it?
**Short answer:** You are given a few tables (or a question on a shared screen) and asked to write queries while the interviewer watches. They are judging how you think and communicate as much as whether the final query runs.
**Why / explain simply:**
- "Live" means you type in real time, often on a shared screen or a coding site. Nobody expects perfect syntax from memory; they expect a sensible approach.
- Typical topics reported: joins, GROUP BY/HAVING, Nth highest salary, duplicates, window functions, CTEs (a CTE is a named sub-query you write first and then use).
- Difficulty varies by company type: basic-to-medium at service MNCs and GCCs, medium-hard at product/startup firms.
**What the evidence says:** SQL appears in about 19 of 20 company profiles (candidate-reported, anonymous, not a statistical sample; IP Section 4). Reported difficulty: basic-medium at service MNCs, medium (windows, CTE) at analytics firms, medium-hard at startups, with one report of "LeetCode hard" at PhonePe (IP Section 3, single-source). Amazon-style processes reportedly open with a shared-screen SQL screen (IP 2.2). Evidence for any one company is thin; ask the recruiter what the round looks like.
**What to do:**
1. Before typing, repeat the question back and ask about the table columns, NULLs and duplicates.
2. Say your plan in one line ("I'll join orders to customers, then group by month").
3. Write a simple version first, run it, then refine.
4. If stuck, say what you do know and ask for a hint; do not go silent.
5. Practise on a shared screen with a friend this week, talking aloud.
**Example:** (illustrative) You are asked for the second-highest salary per department. You say, "I'll rank salaries within each department using DENSE_RANK, then keep rank 2." You write it, test on two rows, and mention that ties would change the answer.
**Say this in the interview:** "Let me confirm the columns and whether there can be duplicates, then I'll start with a simple version and improve it."
**Common mistakes:** Typing silently; skipping clarifying questions; jumping to a clever query that fails; panicking about syntax instead of explaining logic.
**Where this is covered:** Book 2 (SQL); Book 11 Part 9 (when you blank); guide Section 4.

### Q43 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is asked in an Excel or Power BI practical task round?
**Short answer:** You get a small dataset and a business question, and must clean it, calculate something and present a result, using Excel (pivots, formulas) or Power BI (a simple model and a few charts). Time is usually limited.
**Why / explain simply:**
- A "practical task" tests whether you can actually do the work, not recite definitions.
- Excel tasks reported: pivots, rank, lookups and formulas on a case. Power BI questions reported: row-level security, SUM vs SUMX, ALL vs ALLEXCEPT, optimisation (DAX is Power BI's formula language).
- Many firms never run a hands-on task; Excel and Power BI show up less often than SQL in interview reports.
**What the evidence says:** Excel appears in about 5 and Power BI/DAX in about 7 of roughly 20 company profiles (candidate-reported, anonymous; IP Section 4). Examples: Novartis Excel case with pivots, rank and formulas (March 2023 report, single source); Deloitte USI Hyderabad Power BI round (2025-26, search-summary level; IP 2.1). Excel is in 60% and Power BI in 40% of JDs (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points; MS Addendum), so they matter for the job even if less asked in interviews.
**What to do:**
1. Build one end-to-end task: raw HR file, cleaned, pivot, 3 charts, 3-line summary.
2. Time yourself at 60 to 90 minutes.
3. Practise explaining what each chart tells a manager.
4. Learn the DAX terms above if Power BI is in the JD.
5. Ask the recruiter if a task is part of the process.
**Example:** (illustrative) Given an attrition sheet with duplicate employee IDs, you remove duplicates, build a pivot of exits by department and month, and write: "Exits peak in month 3 in Sales."
**Common mistakes:** Beautiful charts with no conclusion; not checking data quality first; leaving cells hard-coded; running out of time on formatting.
**Where this is covered:** Book 1 (Excel); Book 4 (Power BI); guide Section 4.

### Q44 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How important is statistics for a data analyst interview?
**Short answer:** It depends heavily on the employer. Basic statistics is enough for most service and GCC roles, but analytics consulting firms test probability and statistics hard.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Basic means mean, median, standard deviation, correlation, sampling, and what a hypothesis test or p-value is in plain words.
- Analytics firms add Bayes and conditional probability (updating a probability when new information arrives).
- In JDs, statistics is named less often than SQL or Excel, so it rarely decides whether you get shortlisted, but it can decide the interview.
**What the evidence says:** Statistics is named in 23% of JDs (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points), and only 6% in the MIS-focused sub-sample (MS Addendum). In interview reports it appears in about 6 of 20 profiles; Tiger Analytics candidates called probability/stats "the hardest part" (candidate-reported, about 4 sources; IP 2.3, IP Section 4). Service MNCs reportedly ask only basic stats (IP Section 3).
**What to do:**
1. Check the JD and company type: MIS or service role, learn the basics; analytics firm, go deeper.
2. Be able to explain mean vs median, and a hypothesis test, in two sentences each.
3. Practise 5 simple probability questions per week.
4. Do not postpone SQL/Excel for statistics.
**Example:** (illustrative) Asked "Is average salary a good summary?", you say, "Not if a few senior salaries are very high; the median is safer."
**Common mistakes:** Memorising formulas without meaning; ignoring stats entirely before an analytics-firm interview; over-studying it for an MIS role.
**Where this is covered:** Book 5 (Stats); guide Sections 2 and 4.

### Q45 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How should I learn Excel for interviews (which functions and features matter most)?
**Short answer:** Learn a small core deeply: lookups, conditional formulas, pivot tables, data cleaning and basic charts. Excel is the tool most HR professionals already half-know, so it is your fastest win.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Core functions: XLOOKUP or VLOOKUP/INDEX-MATCH, IF, SUMIFS/COUNTIFS, text functions, date functions, and removing duplicates.
- Core features: pivot tables, sorting/filtering, conditional formatting, charts, and Power Query (Excel's built-in data cleaning tool).
- Add VBA (macro scripting) only if MIS roles in your target list ask for it.
**What the evidence says:** Excel is in 60% of JDs (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points) and 72% in the MIS/reporting sub-sample; VBA 13% overall but 19% in MIS (MS Addendum). Google Sheets is 8%. In interviews Excel is reported in about 5 of 20 profiles, e.g. a Novartis case with pivots and rank (candidate-reported; IP Section 4, IP 2.2).
**What to do:**
1. Week 1: lookups and SUMIFS on an attendance file.
2. Week 2: pivot tables and charts on a headcount file.
3. Week 3: clean a messy export (dates, duplicates, spaces).
4. Week 4: one task, start to finish, with a written summary.
5. Check target JDs and add VBA only if requested.
**Example:** (illustrative) Using your own payroll-style sample data (no real employee data), you build a pivot of headcount by grade and location and a lookup that fills manager names.
**Common mistakes:** Learning every function; using only manual formatting; skipping Power Query; practising on tidy tutorial data only.
**Where this is covered:** Book 1 (Excel); guide Section 6.

### Q46 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How much Power BI or Tableau do I need to know?
**Short answer:** For entry roles, you need to build a clean multi-chart dashboard from a cleaned dataset and explain it. Advanced DAX or Tableau calculations are usually later-stage skills.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Basic level: import data, make relationships between tables, build 5 to 8 visuals, add slicers (filters), and publish or screenshot.
- Next level: simple measures (calculated numbers), time comparisons, row-level security.
- Dashboards/reporting are asked for far more than any specific tool name.
**What the evidence says:** Dashboards/reporting/MIS appear in 75% of JDs, Power BI in 40%, Tableau in 33%, DAX in 5% (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points; MS Addendum). Tableau shows up in about 4 interview profiles; Power BI/DAX in about 7 (candidate-reported; IP Section 4). Reported DAX topics: SUM vs SUMX, ALL vs ALLEXCEPT, RLS (IP 2.1).
**What to do:**
1. Pick the tool named most in your target JDs.
2. Build one HR dashboard (hiring funnel or attrition) with a clear title and three takeaways.
3. Learn five basic measures.
4. Be ready to say who the dashboard is for and what decision it helps.
**Example:** (illustrative) An attrition dashboard with exits by month, by department and by tenure band, plus a note: "Highest exits in first year."
**Common mistakes:** Too many visuals; no business message; learning both tools at once; ignoring data modelling.
**Where this is covered:** Book 4 (Power BI); guide Section 2.

### Q47 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Should I learn Power BI or Tableau first?
**Short answer:** Power BI first, for most Hyderabad/Bengaluru entry roles, unless your target JDs clearly say Tableau. Concepts transfer, so the second tool is much quicker.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Power BI appears more often in the JD samples and is free to download for Windows (check Microsoft's current terms).
- Tableau appears more in GCC/finance-type postings.
- Both do the same job: connect data, build charts, share dashboards.
**What the evidence says:** Pooled JDs: Power BI 40%, Tableau 33% (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points). It flips by sample: GCC/MNC sample Tableau 48% vs Power BI 32%; Hyderabad sample Power BI 49% vs Tableau 40%; the first 40-JD sample had Power BI 32% vs Tableau 12% (MS Addendum). Gap is small, so evidence is weak either way. In interviews Power BI is reported in about 7 profiles and Tableau about 4 (candidate-reported; IP Section 4).
**What to do:**
1. List 10 JDs you would actually apply to.
2. Count Power BI vs Tableau mentions.
3. Start with the winner; if tied, choose Power BI.
4. Do not start the second tool until one dashboard is finished.
**Example:** (illustrative) Out of 10 target JDs, 6 say Power BI, 2 say Tableau, 2 neither. You choose Power BI.
**Common mistakes:** Waiting to decide for weeks; paying for a course before checking JDs; assuming one tool shuts you out of the other.
**Where this is covered:** Book 4 (Power BI, including Tableau comparison); guide Section 2.

### Q48 ⭐⭐ 🟢 When should I start learning Python and how deep should I go?
**Short answer:** Start after Excel and SQL are solid, around month 4 of a 6-month plan, and go to pandas basics (loading, cleaning, grouping, simple charts). Deeper Python is for later.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Python is a programming language; pandas is its table-handling library.
- It is useful, but only about a third of JDs ask for it, so it is not the first gate.
- Python is more common in product/startup roles and less in MIS roles.
**What the evidence says:** Python appears in 32% of JDs overall (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points); 16% in the MIS sub-sample, about 40% in Bengaluru and GCC samples (MS Addendum). The first 40-JD sample suggested Python rises in 3+ year roles (impression only; MS Section 3). Python-heavy product/startup analyst roles are the hardest entry point (MS Section 6). In interviews Python is reported in about 10 profiles, mostly basic (candidate-reported; IP Section 4).
**What to do:**
1. Finish a basic SQL project before opening Python.
2. Then spend 4 to 6 weeks on pandas: read CSV, filter, group, merge, plot.
3. Redo one Excel/SQL project in Python.
4. If your targets are MIS roles, keep Python minimal.
**Example:** (illustrative) You rebuild your attrition-by-department pivot with pandas groupby in ten lines and compare the result with Excel.
**Common mistakes:** Starting Python in week 1; spending months on theory; skipping SQL because Python feels more "technical".
**Where this is covered:** Book 3 (Python); guide Section 6.

### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is a realistic 6-month study plan for a non-technical beginner?
**Short answer:** Month 1 Excel, month 2 SQL core, month 3 advanced SQL plus Power BI, month 4 statistics and Python basics, month 5 cases, HR domain and portfolio, month 6 mocks and applications. It assumes steady weekly hours, not a guarantee of a job.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Order follows what JDs ask most: Excel and SQL first, then BI, then Python.
- A daily split is suggested: about 40% SQL/Excel/Power BI, 25% Python/stats, 20% cases/aptitude, 15% soft skills/portfolio (plan guidance, not data).
- Start applying around month 3 for MIS-type roles; this is guidance, not evidence.
**What the evidence says:** Excel 60% and SQL 54% of JDs are the only tools above 50% (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points; MS Addendum). No reliable data found on how long switchers actually take or what share succeed. Blog timelines are commercial and unverified (B0S).
**What to do:**
1. Count your realistic weekly hours.
2. Keep the same order, but stretch months if you have fewer hours.
3. End each month with one small deliverable (month 1 Excel task, month 2 five SQL queries on a dataset, month 3 dashboard).
4. Test yourself monthly and repeat weak topics rather than moving on.
5. Build 2 to 3 projects by month 5 (blog advice, anecdotal).
**Example:** (illustrative) Working 8 hours a week, you take about 9 months for the same plan and start applying for MIS roles at month 4.
**Common mistakes:** Treating six months as a promise; learning in parallel; no projects; waiting until "ready" to apply.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 6; Book 12 (portfolio).

### Q51 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Which is better to learn first, SQL or Excel?
**Short answer:** Excel first if you are a beginner, then SQL soon after. Excel gets you working quickly and is the most common tool in MIS roles; SQL is the most asked skill in interviews.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Excel teaches the thinking (filters, grouping, lookups) with instant visual feedback.
- SQL does the same on larger data, and interviews test it heavily.
- You need both; the order matters less than starting SQL by month 2.
**What the evidence says:** Excel 60% and SQL 54% of JDs (n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points); in MIS sub-sample Excel 72%, SQL 44%; in GCC sub-sample SQL 76%, Excel 76% (MS Addendum). SQL appears in about 19 of 20 interview profiles; Excel in about 5 (candidate-reported, anonymous; IP Section 4). Hence Excel for first jobs, SQL for interviews.
**What to do:**
1. Spend weeks 1 to 4 on Excel and basic maths.
2. Start SQL in week 5; do not wait to finish Excel perfection.
3. If you target product/startup roles, move SQL earlier.
4. Practise both on the same HR-style dataset to see the link.
**Example:** (illustrative) You learn lookups in Excel, then replicate them as a SQL JOIN and notice they are the same idea.
**Common mistakes:** Mastering every Excel feature before SQL; learning SQL by memorising queries; dropping Excel entirely.
**Where this is covered:** Book 1 (Excel); Book 2 (SQL); guide Sections 2 and 6.

### Q52 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Can I learn data analytics while working full time in HR?
**Short answer:** Yes, many people do, but it takes longer than the headline "6 months" timelines. Consistency (a small block every day) matters more than occasional long sessions.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Full-time work means fewer hours, so stretch the plan rather than cram it.
- Your job can become practice: automate a report or analyse a workforce file (with permission).
- Protect rest; burnout ends more switches than a slow schedule.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on completion or success rates for working switchers. Blog timelines are commercial and unverified (B0S). Use your own hours as the basis.
**What to do:**
1. Count free hours honestly (for example 1 hour on weekdays, 3 on weekends).
2. Divide the 6-month plan hours by your weekly hours to get your timeline.
3. Fix one daily slot and a weekly review.
4. Use work tasks, within company data rules, as practice.
5. Reassess monthly; adjust, do not abandon.
**Example:** (illustrative) If the plan needs about 400 hours and you have 10 hours a week, that is 40 weeks, not 26. Plan for 10 months.
**Common mistakes:** Copying someone's full-time timeline; studying only on weekends; breaking data-privacy rules with company data; burning out in week 3.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 6; Book 11 (soft skills, including time and stress).

### Q53 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I use HR datasets (attrition, hiring funnel, attendance) to build portfolio projects?
**Short answer:** Pick a business question, use a public or synthetic HR dataset, analyse it with SQL/Excel/Power BI, and present findings the way you would to an HR head. Never use real employee data from work.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Your HR knowledge helps you ask better questions than a typical fresher.
- A good project has a question, cleaning steps, analysis, chart and recommendation.
- Using HR data tells employers you know the domain, but it does not replace technical skill.
**What the evidence says:** No pure people-analytics role at 0 to 2 years was found in the JD samples; Workday-reporting roles asked 3+ years, though Deutsche Bank's Workday Prism role was tagged Entry (MS Addendum, MS Section 6). So HR projects support HR-tech and general analyst applications. How many projects employers want: no reliable data found (blog advice only, B0S).
**What to do:**
1. Choose one: attrition drivers, hiring funnel drop-off, or absenteeism patterns.
2. Write the question and the decision it supports.
3. Clean, analyse, build a 1-page dashboard and 5-line summary.
4. Publish with a README stating data source and limits.
5. Prepare to explain every step.
**Example:** (illustrative) Hiring funnel project: applications, screened, interviewed, offered, joined. You show the biggest drop is offer to join and suggest follow-up steps.
**Common mistakes:** Real or confidential data; only charts, no recommendation; hiding that the data is synthetic; claiming causes from correlation.
**Where this is covered:** Book 10 Part 1 (People analytics domain); Book 12 (portfolio); guide Section 5.

### Q54 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I explain my HR domain knowledge as a strength in an analyst interview?
**Short answer:** Translate your HR work into data language and show one example where you used numbers to influence a decision. Be honest that you are building the technical side.
**Why / explain simply:**
- You know where HR data comes from (HRIS, payroll, ATS), why it is messy and what managers ask.

2. Prepare one story with a number and a decision.
3. Pair it with a project that shows technical skill.

### Q56 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Are paid data analytics courses with placement guarantees worth the money?

### Q57 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I find out if a bootcamp's placement claims are genuine?

### Q58 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I prepare for the "tell me about yourself" question as a switcher?
**Short answer:** Use a 60 to 90 second story with three parts: what you did in HR, what pulled you towards data and what you have built since, ending with why this role. Keep it honest, specific and forward-looking.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Interviewers use it to judge communication and fit, and it is nearly always asked in HR/behavioural rounds (about 15 of 20 profiles; candidate-reported, anonymous; IP Section 4).
- Switchers lose points by apologising for the switch or reciting a CV.
- A project example proves the switch is real.

1. Write three sentences: past, bridge, present and role.
2. Include one result and one project.
3. Remove anything you cannot back up.
4. Practise aloud until it takes under 90 seconds.
5. Prepare a one-line answer to "why leave HR?".
**Example:** (illustrative) "I spent X years in HR operations reporting to managers on headcount and attrition. I enjoyed the numbers more than the process, so I learnt SQL and Power BI and built an attrition dashboard. I want to do that full time as an analyst."

### Q59 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I answer salary expectation questions in the HR round?
**Short answer:** Know your range before the call, give a researched range tied to the role, and show you are flexible within reason. Do not give one number you cannot defend or reveal you will accept anything.
**Why / explain simply:**
- The question tests whether you did research and whether you will fit the budget.
- Ranges are safer than single numbers.
- Entry pay is wide, so knowing your floor matters before you speak.

Entry MIS/BA mostly Rs 3 to 6 LPA (MS Addendum). Blog figures are secondary and unverified (MS Section 4). HR-to-analyst pay-cut size and what offers switchers actually get: No reliable data found.
**What to do:**
1. Calculate your minimum monthly need, then your target.
2. Check the JD and employer type for pay hints.
3. Prepare a range with your target near the lower middle.
4. If asked early, ask what budget is planned for the role.
5. Practise saying it calmly.
**Example:** (illustrative) Your minimum is Rs 5 LPA and you target Rs 6. You say, "Based on the role and my skills, I am looking at Rs 5.5 to 7 LPA, and I am open to discussing the full package."

### Q60 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What should I say about my current CTC and expected CTC?
**Short answer:** State your current CTC truthfully if asked, with fixed and variable split, and then talk about expected CTC as a range based on the role. Never lie; offers can be verified.
**Why / explain simply:**
- CTC (cost to company) is the total yearly package, not take-home.
- Be clear what is fixed, variable and benefits.
- A switcher may take a smaller increase or even a cut; frame it as moving into a new field, not a failure.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on typical HR-to-analyst hikes or pay cuts, or on how often employers verify CTC. Use only pay data from JDs: mostly Rs 3.5 to 9 LPA, Rs 6 to 10 LPA in upper-end postings (MS Section 4, MS Addendum; verified only in a minority of JDs).
**What to do:**
1. Write your current fixed pay, variable and benefits in a worksheet.
2. Decide your minimum acceptable pay.
3. Run this rule: if offer minus current is a cut, check you can handle it for 12 months.
4. Say a range, not a single number.
5. Keep payslips accurate and ready.
**Example:** (illustrative) Current Rs 8 LPA (Rs 7 fixed, Rs 1 variable). Analyst offer Rs 6 LPA. That is a Rs 2 LPA cut; check monthly budget and growth over two years before accepting.
**Say this in the interview:** "My current CTC is X, with Y fixed and Z variable. I am moving into analytics, so I am looking at roles at X to Y rather than matching my current figure exactly."
**Common mistakes:** Inflating the current CTC; confusing in-hand and CTC; refusing to discuss any number; ignoring variable pay.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2b; Book 11 (negotiation).

### Q61 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I handle a notice period of 60 or 90 days when applying?
**Short answer:** State it honestly, ask early whether the notice period can be negotiated or bought out, and be clear about what you will do. A long notice period is common and not automatically a rejection.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Notice period is the time you must work after resigning, set by your contract.
- Some employers prefer quicker joiners; some wait for the right person.
- Options may include negotiating with your employer, a buy-out, or early release; check your contract first.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on how notice period affects hiring odds in analytics roles. Process timelines also vary widely (about 1 week to 3 months in service-company reports; candidate-reported, anonymous; IP Section 3), so long notice is not necessarily the deciding factor.
**What to do:**
1. Read your contract: duration, buy-out terms, recovery clauses.
2. Ask HR whether early release is possible (only when you are ready).
3. Put your notice in the application form honestly.
4. Prepare a line for the recruiter; offer to start prep tasks or handover plans.
5. Do not promise a date you cannot meet.
**Example:** (illustrative) Notice is 90 days. You tell the recruiter you can usually negotiate down to 60 days with a handover plan, and you will confirm once an offer is in hand.
**Say this in the interview:** "My notice period is 90 days. I will check with my employer on early release once I have an offer, and I will not leave a messy handover."
**Common mistakes:** Hiding the notice period; promising early release without checking; resigning before an offer; badmouthing the current employer.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 3 (timelines); Book 11 (soft skills).

### Q62 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Do I need to resign before I start looking for analytics jobs?
**Short answer:** No. Most people search while employed; it is lower risk, gives you pay continuity and leaves your options open. Resign only if you have an offer or enough savings to cover a long search.
**Why / explain simply:**
- You do not need to quit to learn, build projects or apply.
- Searching while employed removes pressure to take the first poor offer.
- Leaving first makes sense mainly when work leaves no time to prepare, and only with savings.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on how long switcher job searches take or on outcomes of employed versus unemployed searches. Interview timelines in reports range from about 1 week to 3 months for service companies; other types mostly unverified (candidate-reported, anonymous; IP Section 3). Pay in JDs mostly Rs 3.5 to 9 LPA (MS Section 4), so a pay cut is possible.
**What to do:**
1. Count your savings in months of expenses.
2. Rule: keep your job until you have an offer, unless you have savings for at least 6 months and a clear study plan (a personal safety rule, not data).
3. Schedule interviews outside work hours or use leave.
4. Keep your search confidential.
5. Plan notice period and handover once you have an offer.
**Example:** (illustrative) Monthly expenses are Rs 40,000 and savings Rs 2.4 lakh, which is 6 months. You still keep the job and apply on weekends.
**Common mistakes:** Quitting for "focus" without savings; telling colleagues too early; skipping the offer letter check before resigning; underestimating how long the search takes.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 6; Book 11 (soft skills).
