### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you calculate percentage increase and percentage decrease between two numbers, and which value goes in the denominator?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you divide by the starting value and can say whether the change is up or down.
**Say this in the interview:** "Change divided by the original value, times 100. The denominator is always the starting number, whichever direction it moves."
**Answer:** **% change = (New - Old) / Old x 100; the denominator is the OLD (starting) value.** Positive means increase, negative means decrease.
**Explain simply:**
- Step 1: find the change, new minus old. Step 2: divide by the old value, because the question is "how big is the move compared with where we began". Step 3: multiply by 100.
- Percentage increase = (increase / old) x 100. Percentage decrease = (decrease / old) x 100. In both, "old" is the earlier figure.
- Worked example: headcount 200 falls to 150. Change = 50, base = 200, 50/200 = 0.25, so a 25% decrease. Going back from 150 to 200 is a 33.3% increase (50/150), because the base is now 150. The two are different, which is why the base matters.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the old value is zero?* The percentage is undefined; quote the absolute change. 2) *80 to 100, then 100 to 80?* Up 25%, down 20%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing by the new value; dividing by the bigger number; forgetting that "A is x% more than B" uses B as the base.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is 15% of Rs 840?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Mental arithmetic with percentages.
**Say this in the interview:** "10% of 840 is 84, 5% is 42, so 15% is 126."
**Answer:** **Rs 126**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 10% of 840 = 84 (move the decimal one place).
2. 5% is half of that = 42.
3. 15% = 84 + 42 = 126.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Split into 10% plus half of 10%. Why it works: percentages add, so 15% = 10% + 5%.
**Explain simply:**
- "Per cent" means per hundred, so 15% = 15/100 = 0.15.
- 0.15 x 840 = 126.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What is 15% of 1,240?* 124 + 62 = Rs 186. 2) *What is 840 as a percent of 126?* 126/840 = 15%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing 15 x 840 and forgetting to divide by 100 (gives 12,600). Check: 126/840 = 0.15, verified by script.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 On what base are profit percentage, loss percentage and discount percentage each calculated (cost price or marked price)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Knowing which base goes with which term.
**Say this in the interview:** "Profit and loss percentages are on cost price; discount percentage is on marked price, unless the question says otherwise."
**Answer:** **Profit % and loss % use cost price (CP). Discount % uses marked price (MP).**
**Explain simply:**
- Profit % = profit / CP x 100; Loss % = loss / CP x 100. The cost price is what the seller started with, so it is the base for how well or badly the seller did.
- Discount % = discount / MP x 100, because the discount is a cut from the printed (marked) price.
- Worked example: CP Rs 200, MP Rs 300, SP Rs 240. Profit = 40, so 40/200 = 20% profit. Discount = 60, so 60/300 = 20% discount. Same 20%, different bases.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the question says "profit on selling price"?* Then divide by SP (that is margin, see Q25). 2) *Is a discount on CP ever used?* Only if the question states it.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calculating discount on cost price, or profit on selling price, because the SP is the number in front of you.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between cost price, marked price, selling price and discount, and how are they linked by formulas?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clear vocabulary and the formula chain.
**Say this in the interview:** "Cost price is what the shop paid, marked price is the label, discount is the cut from the label, and selling price is what the customer actually pays."
**Answer:** **SP = MP - Discount; Profit = SP - CP (or Loss = CP - SP); Discount % = Discount / MP x 100; Profit % = Profit / CP x 100.**
**Explain simply:**
- Cost price (CP): the price the seller paid to buy or make the item.
- Marked price (MP, also list price): the price printed on the item.
- Discount: the reduction given off MP. Selling price (SP): the price the customer finally pays.
- Chain: CP -> (add markup) -> MP -> (give discount) -> SP -> (compare with CP) -> profit or loss.
- Worked example: CP 800, MP 1,000, discount 10% = 100, so SP = 900. Profit = 900 - 800 = 100, which is 100/800 = 12.5% on cost.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can SP be above MP?* Not with a discount; that would be a surcharge. 2) *If MP = CP x 1.25 and discount is 20%?* SP = CP x 1.25 x 0.8 = CP, no profit, no loss.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Mixing MP with CP; treating MP as what the customer pays.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you find the single equivalent discount for two successive discounts of 20% and 10%?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Combining successive discounts into one.
**Say this in the interview:** "Multiply the remaining fractions: 0.8 x 0.9 = 0.72, so the single equivalent discount is 28%."
**Answer:** **28%**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Take a marked price of 100.
2. After 20%: 80.
3. After 10% on 80: 80 - 8 = 72.
4. Total discount = 100 - 72 = 28, so 28%.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Formula: x + y - xy/100 = 20 + 10 - (20 x 10)/100 = 30 - 2 = 28. Why: the second discount is on a smaller price, so it is worth less than 10% of the original.
**Explain simply:**
- Check: 0.8 x 0.9 = 0.72, so 1 - 0.72 = 0.28.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Three discounts of 10%, 20%, 30%?* 0.9 x 0.8 x 0.7 = 0.504, so 49.6%. 2) *Is a single 30% discount better for the customer?* Yes (30 > 28).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding to 30%.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 The price of an item rises by 25%. By what percentage must a household cut its consumption so that its spending stays the same?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Inverse relation between price and quantity at fixed spend.
**Say this in the interview:** "Price goes to 125% so the household must buy 100/125 = 80% as much, a 20% cut."
**Answer:** **20% reduction**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Take old price 100 and consumption 100 units; spend = 10,000.
2. New price = 125.
3. For the same spend 10,000, units = 10,000/125 = 80.
4. Cut = 100 - 80 = 20 units, so 20%.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Formula: reduction = r/(100 + r) x 100 = 25/125 x 100 = 20%. Why: the base for the cut is the original consumption, but the change is measured against the new, higher price.
**Explain simply:**
- Check: 80 units x 125 = 10,000.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Price falls 20%, by what % can consumption rise to keep spend same?* 20/80 = 25%. 2) *Price up 50%?* Cut 50/150 = 33.3%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 25% (cutting the same percent as the price rise).

### Q18 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Two articles are each sold for Rs 1,200. One is sold at a 20% gain and the other at a 20% loss. What is the overall profit or loss, and what percentage of the total cost price is it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss among the most frequent quant topics at service-company aptitude gates (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Calculating each cost price separately, and knowing that equal % gain and loss on equal SP always means a net loss.
**Say this in the interview:** "The gain article cost 1,000 and the loss article cost 1,500, so total cost is 2,500 against 2,400 received: a loss of Rs 100, which is 4%."
**Answer:** **Overall loss of Rs 100, which is 4% of the total cost price.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Article 1 (20% gain): SP = 1.2 x CP, so CP = 1,200 / 1.2 = 1,000.
2. Article 2 (20% loss): SP = 0.8 x CP, so CP = 1,200 / 0.8 = 1,500.
3. Total CP = 1,000 + 1,500 = 2,500. Total SP = 1,200 + 1,200 = 2,400.
4. Loss = 2,500 - 2,400 = 100. Loss % = 100 / 2,500 x 100 = 4%.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** When equal SP gives x% gain and x% loss, the net loss is always x squared over 100 percent: 20 x 20 / 100 = 4%. Why: the loss article had a bigger cost, so the 20% loss is on a larger base than the 20% gain.
**Check:** Verified by script (exact fractions).
**Explain simply:**
- Gain and loss percentages are on cost price, and the two cost prices differ, so they do not cancel.
- A common wrong shortcut is "20% gain and 20% loss cancel, so no profit or loss".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Each sold at Rs 1,200 with 10% gain and 10% loss?* Loss 1%. 2) *Same CP, 20% gain and 20% loss?* Then they do cancel (no net change).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming break-even; taking 20% of 1,200 for both articles.

### Q22 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 In an election with two candidates, the winner gets 55% of the votes and wins by 2,000 votes. What is the total number of votes cast (assuming every vote is valid)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); percentages and profit-loss are listed as high-frequency quant topics in prep-site material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Turning a vote margin into a percentage gap.
**Say this in the interview:** The loser has 45%, so the gap is 10% of all votes. If 10% is 2,000, the total is 20,000.
**Answer:** **20,000 votes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Winner 55%, so loser = 100% - 55% = 45%.
2. Winning margin = 55% - 45% = 10% of total votes.
3. 10% of total = 2,000, so 1% = 200.
4. Total = 100 x 200 = 20,000.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Margin % = 2 x winner % - 100 = 10%. Then total = 2,000 / 0.10 = 20,000. It works because every vote goes to one of two people.
**Check:** Winner 11,000, loser 9,000; 11,000 - 9,000 = 2,000. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- In a two-way contest the margin is the winner's share minus the loser's share.
- A percentage gap tells you what slice of the total the gap is; divide the actual gap by that slice.

### Q70 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Why is 8% of 25 equal to 25% of 8, and how can this save time in calculations?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss at TCS, Infosys, Wipro and Accenture-type gates (unverified)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Understanding the symmetry of percentages and using it for mental maths.
**Say this in the interview:** x% of y equals y% of x, because both are x times y divided by 100. I flip to whichever is easier.
**Answer:** **Both equal 2, because x% of y = (x x y)/100 = y% of x**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 8% of 25 = (8 x 25) / 100 = 200 / 100 = 2.
2. 25% of 8 = (25 x 8) / 100 = 200 / 100 = 2.
3. Multiplication does not care about order, so the two always match.

- 'Percent' means 'per hundred': x% of y = x/100 x y.

- Worked example: 12% of 50 headcount = 50% of 12 = 6 people.

- What is 36% of 25? 25% of 36 = 9.
- Does the trick work for 'percent more than'? No, only for 'percent of'.

**Check:** Cost 100, MP 200, SP at 40% off = 120; at 20% off = 160; check verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Marked price is found by dividing the selling price by (1 - discount rate).
- The unknown cost does not matter, so any value (100) works.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- Discount 50% on the same item? Break-even (0% profit).
- Discount 10%? 2 x 0.9 - 1 = 80% profit.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking the profit rises from 20% to 40% (just 20 points more); each discount point is worth two profit points because MP is double cost.

### Q80 ⭐ 🔴 At what two successive discounts of equal percentage would a Rs 2,000 product sell for Rs 1,280?

**Answer:** **20% and 20%**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Final / original = 1,280 / 2,000 = 0.64.
2. Two equal discounts d give (1 - d)^2 = 0.64.
3. 1 - d = 0.8, so d = 0.2 = 20%.
4. Check: 2,000 x 0.8 = 1,600, then 1,600 x 0.8 = 1,280.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Recognise 0.64 as 0.8 x 0.8 (8 x 8 = 64).
**Check:** 2,000 x 0.8 = 1,600; 1,600 x 0.8 = 1,280. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Successive discounts multiply, so the net effect is not 2d.
- The single equivalent discount is 36%, not 40%.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- Final price Rs 1,620 from 2,000? 0.81 = 0.9^2, so 10% twice.
- Two equal discounts total 51% off? 0.49 gives 30% each.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 18% (36% / 2 is wrong) or 36%, the net discount rather than each one.

### Q81 ⭐ 🔴 A shopkeeper sells sugar at Rs 40 per kg, which he bought at Rs 36 per kg, but his weighing machine shows 1 kg for only 800 g. What is his actual profit percentage, correct to 2 decimal places?

**Answer:** **38.89%**

1. Cost of the 800 g he actually gives = 0.8 x 36 = Rs 28.80.
2. Revenue = Rs 40 (price per 'kg').
3. Profit = 40 - 28.80 = Rs 11.20.
4. Profit % = 11.20 / 28.80 x 100 = 38.888...%, rounded to 38.89%.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Price factor 40/36 = 1.1111; weight factor 1,000/800 = 1.25; product = 1.3889, so 38.89%.

- Two gains: honest price markup (11.11%) and the 20% shortfall in weight (a 25% gain).
- They multiply: 1.1111 x 1.25 = 1.3889.
**Likely follow-ups:**

### Q82 ⭐ 🔴 The price of a product is first increased by x% and then decreased by x%, resulting in a net 4% decrease from the original. Find x.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list percentages and profit-loss at TCS, Infosys, Wipro and Accenture-type gates (unverified)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting that +x% then -x% equals a loss of x^2/100 percent.
**Say this in the interview:** The net change is minus x squared over 100, so x^2/100 = 4, giving x = 20.
**Answer:** **x = 20**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Take 100. After +x%: 100 + x.
2. After -x% on that: (100 + x)(100 - x)/100 = (10,000 - x^2)/100.
3. Net 4% decrease means result = 96, so 10,000 - x^2 = 9,600.
4. x^2 = 400, so x = 20 (the positive root).
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Memorise: +x% then -x% gives a loss of x^2/100 percent; set it to 4, so x = 20.
**Check:** x = 20: 1.2 x 0.8 = 0.96. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The decrease is taken on a larger base, so the final value is always below the start.
- The negative root -20 is rejected because x is a percentage rise.
**Likely follow-ups:**
- Net 1% decrease? x = 10.
- Net 9% decrease? x = 30.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering x = 4 (assuming the changes cancel to the net figure) or forgetting to take the positive root.
