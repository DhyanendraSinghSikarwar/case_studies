### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Pointing to a man, Meera says, "He is the son of my mother's only brother." How is the man related to Meera?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); blood relations appear in prep-site topic lists for Infosys, Accenture and TCS NQT (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn a spoken relation into a family tree step by step.
**Say this in the interview:** "Meera's mother's brother is her maternal uncle. His son is her cousin, and since the question says 'son', he is a male cousin. Answer: cousin brother."
**Answer:** **The man is Meera's cousin (a male cousin, "cousin brother").**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Start with Meera. Her mother is one step up.
2. The mother's only brother is Meera's maternal uncle (mama).
3. The uncle's son is the child of Meera's uncle, so he is Meera's first cousin.
4. "Son" tells us he is male.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Read the clue backwards from the pointed person: he is son of (mother's brother of me). Mother's brother = uncle, uncle's child = cousin. "Only" brother just removes doubt about which uncle.
**Check:** Reverse it: Meera is the daughter of the man's father's sister, so she is his cousin. Consistent.
**Explain simply:**
- A cousin is a child of your parent's brother or sister.
- Words like "son" or "daughter" fix gender, so use them to name the answer precisely.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if it were "mother's only sister's son"?* Still a cousin (maternal aunt's son). 2) *Can the answer be "nephew"?* No, nephew is a sibling's son.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "uncle" or "brother"; the uncle is the father of the man, not the man himself.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A is the sister of B, C is the mother of B, D is the father of C, and E is the mother of D. How is A related to D?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); chain-style blood relations are a standard item in the Infosys/Accenture topic lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Following a chain of generations without losing a link.
**Say this in the interview:** "A and B are siblings, so C is also A's mother. D is C's father, so D is A's maternal grandfather. A is a girl, so A is D's granddaughter."
**Answer:** **A is D's granddaughter (D's daughter's daughter).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A is B's sister, so A and B share the same parents.
2. C is B's mother, so C is also A's mother.
3. D is C's father, so D is A's mother's father, the maternal grandfather.
4. A is female (sister), so from D's side she is a granddaughter.
5. E (D's mother) is not needed for the question; it is a distractor.
**Check:** Tree: E, then D, then C, then A and B. A is two generations below D. Correct.
**Explain simply:**
- Siblings share parents, so any clue about B's parents also holds for A.
- Generations: parent is one step, grandparent is two.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How is A related to E?* Great-granddaughter. 2) *How is B related to D?* Grandchild; gender of B is not given.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calling A the "daughter" of D, or reading the extra clue E as relevant.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 P is the brother of Q, Q is the sister of R, and R is the father of S, who is a girl. How is S related to P?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic seen in prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting that siblings of a parent are uncles or aunts.
**Say this in the interview:** "P, Q and R are all siblings. R is S's father, so P is S's father's brother, which makes P her uncle. S is P's niece."
**Answer:** **S is P's niece (P is her uncle).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. P is Q's brother and Q is R's sister, so P, Q and R are one set of siblings.
2. R is the father of S, so S is the child of P's sibling.
3. A brother's daughter is a niece, so S is P's niece.
**Check:** Reverse: P is the paternal uncle of S. Both readings agree.
**Explain simply:**
- Your sibling's child is your niece (girl) or nephew (boy).
- Gender of R as "father" is given, so we know it is the paternal side.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How is Q related to S?* Paternal aunt. 2) *If S were a boy?* Nephew.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "cousin" or "daughter"; forgetting that R, P, Q are siblings.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Introducing a woman, a man says, "Her mother is the only daughter of my mother." How is the woman related to the man?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a classic form in prep-site blood-relation sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Decoding "only daughter of my mother" correctly.
**Say this in the interview:** "The only daughter of my mother is my sister. So her mother is my sister, and she is my sister's daughter, my niece."
**Answer:** **The woman is the man's niece.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. "My mother's only daughter" is a daughter of the speaker's own mother, so she is the speaker's sister (he is a man, so he is not that daughter).
2. The woman's mother is therefore the speaker's sister.
3. The sister's daughter is the speaker's niece.
**Check:** From the woman's side: her mother's brother is her maternal uncle (mama), so the man is her uncle. Consistent.
**Explain simply:**
- "My mother's only daughter" is the usual puzzle phrase for "my sister".
- The speaker's gender (a man) matters: it rules out the speaker being the daughter.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the speaker were a woman?* The "only daughter" could be herself, so the woman would be her daughter. 2) *Who is the woman's mother's mother to the man?* His own mother.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "daughter" (forgetting the speaker is male) or "cousin".

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A is the brother of B, C is the father of A, D is the brother of C, and E is the son of D. How is E related to B?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic in prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising cousins and not over-claiming gender.
**Say this in the interview:** "A and B are siblings, so C is B's father. D is C's brother, so D is B's uncle, and D's son E is B's cousin."
**Answer:** **E is B's cousin (a male cousin; B's gender is not given).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A and B are siblings, so C (father of A) is also B's father.
2. D is C's brother, so D is B's paternal uncle.
3. E is D's son, so E is the son of B's uncle: B's cousin.
4. E is a son, so male. B could be a boy or a girl, so we cannot say "cousin brother of B" safely.
**Check:** Reverse: B is the child of E's father's brother, so B is E's cousin. Matches.
**Explain simply:**
- Children of two brothers are first cousins.
- Say "cousin" when the other person's gender is not known.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How is D related to A?* Paternal uncle. 2) *How is C related to E?* Paternal uncle.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing "brother" or "nephew".

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you solve a blood-relation question quickly: which symbols do you use for gender and marriage, how do you build the family tree, and what do you do when a clue does not give the gender?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the family-tree method is the standard approach in prep-site guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a repeatable method and are careful about what the clues do not say.
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw a small tree: a square or plus sign for a male, a circle or minus sign for a female, a double line for a married couple, a single line down for parent to child, and a horizontal line for siblings. I place the pointed person last, read backwards, and I never assume gender that is not given."
**Answer:** **Use symbols, build the tree one clue at a time starting from the speaker, and keep unknown gender unknown.**
**Explain simply:**
- Symbols: male = square (+), female = circle (-), couple = "=" between them, children hang below a vertical line, siblings sit side by side.
- Build the tree: start with the most definite clue (the speaker or a named parent), add one clue at a time, and place new people above or below by generation.
- Read "A is the X of B" as: A to B. Cross-check by reading it back from B to A.
- Unknown gender: write "?" and give a gender-neutral answer such as cousin, sibling, spouse, parent or child. Gender is fixed only by words like son, daughter, brother, father, mother, wife.
- Worked mini-example: "A is B's sister; B is C's father." Tree: C is a child of B, A is B's sister, so A is C's paternal aunt (A is female, so aunt).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Your cousin's gender is unknown; what do you call the cousin?* "Cousin". 2) *A is B's brother; B is C's mother; C's gender unknown?* A is C's maternal uncle.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming gender from a name, mixing up paternal and maternal sides, or reading the clue from the wrong end.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Statements: All employees are analysts. All analysts are graduates. Conclusions: I. All employees are graduates. II. All graduates are employees. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); syllogisms are listed for Infosys, Cognizant and Wipro in prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Chaining two "All" statements and not reversing them.
**Say this in the interview:** "Employees sit inside analysts and analysts sit inside graduates, so employees sit inside graduates: I follows. The reverse, II, does not follow."
**Answer:** **Only conclusion I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Draw three circles: Employees inside Analysts inside Graduates.
2. Conclusion I: the Employees circle is fully inside Graduates. It follows.
3. Conclusion II: Graduates is the biggest circle; some graduates may lie outside Employees. It does not follow.
**Check:** Example: 10 employees, 30 analysts, 100 graduates fits both statements, yet not all graduates are employees.
**Explain simply:**
- "All A are B" means circle A is wholly inside circle B.
- Two such links chain: All A are B, All B are C gives All A are C.
- A statement does not work backwards: "All A are B" does not mean "All B are A".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some graduates are analysts" follow?* Yes, if analysts exist (all analysts are graduates). 2) *Does "Some graduates are employees"?* Yes, the same way.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "both follow" by reversing the statement.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Statements: Some managers are engineers. All engineers are analysts. Conclusions: I. Some managers are analysts. II. All analysts are managers. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic in prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Combining a "Some" with an "All".
**Say this in the interview:** "Those managers who are engineers are also analysts, so some managers are analysts: I follows. Nothing says every analyst is a manager, so II does not."
**Answer:** **Only conclusion I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Draw Engineers inside Analysts.
2. Managers overlaps Engineers (some managers are engineers). That overlap area is inside Analysts too.
3. So Managers and Analysts share at least that overlap: "Some managers are analysts" is true.
4. Analysts is a larger circle that may have members outside Managers, so "All analysts are managers" is not forced.
**Check:** Example: managers {1,2,3}, engineers {1}, analysts {1,4,5}. Both statements hold; I is true, II is false.
**Explain simply:**
- Some + All (the All points onward) gives Some.
- "Some A are B" and "All B are C" gives "Some A are C".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the first statement was "All managers are engineers"?* Then all managers are analysts. 2) *Does "Some analysts are managers" follow?* Yes (the reverse of "some" is allowed).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating "some" as "all".

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the four standard statement forms in syllogism questions (All, No, Some, Some not), and how is each drawn in a Venn diagram?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the Venn method is the standard in prep guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know the building blocks before attempting any syllogism.
**Say this in the interview:** "All A are B: A circle inside B. No A is B: two separate circles. Some A are B: the circles overlap. Some A are not B: part of A lies outside B."
**Answer:** **Four forms: All (inside), No (separate), Some (overlap), Some not (part outside).**
**Explain simply:**
- All A are B: circle A fully inside circle B. Example: all HR executives are employees.
- No A is B: two circles that do not touch. Example: no intern is a director.
- Some A are B: the circles overlap, at least one common member. "Some" means "at least one, maybe all". Example: some employees are analysts.
- Some A are not B: at least one member of A is outside B. Example: some employees are not managers.
- Valid reverses: "No A is B" gives "No B is A"; "Some A are B" gives "Some B are A". "All A are B" does not give "All B are A" (only "Some B are A", if A is non-empty).
- Mini-example: All pens are books; some books are bags. Pens are inside Books; Bags overlap Books, but whether Bags touch Pens is open.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some A are not B" follow from "No A is B"?* Yes, if A has members. 2) *Opposite of "All A are B"?* "Some A are not B".
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading "some" as "only some"; reversing "All".

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Five colleagues A, B, C, D and E sit in a row facing north. A sits at the extreme left end and E at the extreme right end. D sits immediately to the left of E. B does not sit next to A. Who sits in the middle?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); linear seating is listed in Accenture and Cognizant prep sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Placing fixed clues first, then eliminating.
**Say this in the interview:** "A is seat 1, E is seat 5, D is seat 4. B cannot be in seat 2, so B is seat 3 and C is seat 2. The middle is B."
**Answer:** **B sits in the middle.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Seats 1 to 5 from the left. A = 1, E = 5.
2. D is immediately left of E, so D = 4.
3. Left: seats 2 and 3 for B and C.
4. B is not next to A, so B is not seat 2. B = 3, C = 2.
5. Final row: A, C, B, D, E.
**Check:** Brute force over all 120 orders gives exactly one valid arrangement (A C B D E); the answer is unique.
**Explain simply:**
- Fixed positions first (ends, "immediately next to"), negative clues last.
- Facing north: left of the row is the speaker's left too, so no flipping.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who sits next to A?* C. 2) *If B could sit next to A?* Two arrangements, so the middle would not be unique.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Ignoring the "does not sit next to" clue and picking C.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you approach a seating-arrangement question: how do you handle linear versus circular tables, left and right, and which clues do you place first?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); seating rated medium-hard in one prep-site view of TCS NQT (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A calm, ordered method for the most time-consuming reasoning type.
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw the shape first, place the definite clues (fixed seat, 'opposite', 'immediately next to'), then the relative ones, then use the negative clues to eliminate. I fix left and right using the facing direction."
**Answer:** **Draw the layout, place the sure clues first, then eliminate with the negative clues, and fix the left-right convention up front.**
**Explain simply:**
- Linear row facing north: your left is the left of the page. If they face south, left and right flip, so write the facing direction.
- Circular table facing the centre: your right-hand side is the anticlockwise direction, your left is clockwise. If they face outward, it reverses.
- Place first: exact positions (ends, "third from the left"), then "opposite" pairs (in a table of 2n people, opposite means n seats apart), then "immediately" clues, then "second to the right" clues, and last the "not next to" clues.
- If two cases remain after the sure clues, write both and let later clues kill one.
- Mini-example: 6 people at a round table: if A is at seat 1, D opposite is seat 4. "B second to the right of A" is two steps anticlockwise.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the question gives no fixed seat?* Fix any one person; a circle has no starting point. 2) *When do you skip?* If it needs many cases and time is gone (see Q47).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Mixing up left and right in circles; placing a "not next to" clue too early.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a certain code, MANAGER is written as NBOBHFS. How is ANALYST written in that code?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); coding-decoding appears in Infosys and Wipro lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Finding a constant shift in the alphabet.
**Say this in the interview:** "M goes to N, A to B, N to O: every letter moves one step forward. ANALYST becomes BOBMZTU."
**Answer:** **BOBMZTU**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Compare letters: M to N (+1), A to B (+1), N to O (+1), A to B, G to H, E to F, R to S. The rule is +1 for every letter.
2. Apply to ANALYST: A to B, N to O, A to B, L to M, Y to Z, S to T, T to U.
3. Result: B O B M Z T U.
**Check:** Verified by script: shifting MANAGER by +1 reproduces NBOBHFS, and the same function gives BOBMZTU.
**Explain simply:**
- A "shift" code adds or subtracts the same number from each letter's position.
- Check one or two letters first; if the shift differs, test a position pattern.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *CODE with +2?* EQFG. 2) *What if Z needs +1?* It wraps to A.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Shifting only part of the word, or going backwards.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a certain code, TABLE is written as GZYOV. How is CHAIR written in that code?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reverse-alphabet codes are a common pattern (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting the "mirror" alphabet (A to Z, B to Y).
**Say this in the interview:** "T becomes G, A becomes Z, B becomes Y: each letter is replaced by its opposite from the other end. CHAIR becomes XSZRI."
**Answer:** **XSZRI**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A to Z, B to Y shows the reverse alphabet. Check: T (20th) to G (7th); 20 + 7 = 27. L (12) to O (15): 27. E (5) to V (22): 27. Every pair adds to 27.
2. Opposite of C (3) is 24 = X. H (8) is 19 = S. A (1) is 26 = Z. I (9) is 18 = R. R (18) is 9 = I.
3. Result: X S Z R I.
**Check:** Verified by script: mirroring TABLE gives GZYOV.
**Explain simply:**
- Reverse alphabet: a letter at position p goes to 27 minus p.
- Quick test: if the first two letters of a code are A to Z and B to Y, it is this pattern.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Code for DATA?* WZGZ. 2) *What is the opposite of M?* N (13 and 14).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 26 instead of 27 minus position.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you identify the rule in a coding-decoding question: what are the common patterns (shift, reverse alphabet, position value, substitution)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); coding-decoding appears in Infosys and Wipro lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A systematic way to test hypotheses quickly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I compare the first word letter by letter, write the position numbers (A=1 to Z=26), look at the difference, then test shift, reverse, or a word-level substitution on the next word."
**Answer:** **Test in order: constant shift, reverse alphabet, position value, then word substitution.**
**Explain simply:**
- Shift: every letter moves the same number. MANAGER to NBOBHFS is +1 each.
- Reverse alphabet: letter to the opposite end (pairs add to 27). TABLE to GZYOV.
- Position value: the word becomes numbers (A=1...). SQL = 19 + 17 + 12 = 48.
- Alternating or growing shifts: +1, +2, +3 along the word, or +1, -1, +1 ...
- Substitution (word codes): find the common word across sentences. "good sales team" and "team is good" share two codes; common words show which code is which.
- Mini-example: CAT to DBU. C to D, A to B, T to U: +1 shift. Apply to DOG: EPH.
- Tip: if the answer is a different length from the question, check for a reversal of the word first.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the first letter fits +1 but the second does not?* Try a changing shift. 2) *Code with no letter pattern?* Likely word substitution.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Stopping after one letter fits; forgetting to wrap Z to A.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A man walks 8 km south and then 6 km west. How far is he from his starting point?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); direction sense is listed for Wipro (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising a right angle and using Pythagoras.
**Say this in the interview:** "South and west are at right angles, so the distance is the hypotenuse: root of 64 plus 36 = 10 km."
**Answer:** **10 km (he is south-west of the start).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. South then west makes a right angle, with legs 8 and 6.
2. Distance squared = 8 squared + 6 squared = 64 + 36 = 100.
3. Distance = root of 100 = 10 km.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 6-8-10 is the 3-4-5 triple doubled. Spot it and skip the arithmetic.
**Check:** 6 squared + 8 squared = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10 squared.
**Explain simply:**
- Pythagoras: for a right angle, hypotenuse squared = sum of the squares of the two legs.
- Common triples: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *5 km east then 12 km north?* 13 km. 2) *Direction from start to end?* South-west.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding 8 + 6 = 14 km.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Ravi walks 5 km north, turns right and walks 3 km, then turns right again and walks 5 km. How far is he from the starting point and in which direction?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); direction sense appears in Wipro and similar lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Tracking turns, and cancelling equal opposite legs.
**Say this in the interview:** "North 5, right turn means east 3, another right turn means south 5. The north and south legs cancel, so he is 3 km east of the start."
**Answer:** **3 km, due East of the starting point.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Facing north, a right turn faces east. Walk 3 km east.
2. Facing east, a right turn faces south. Walk 5 km south.
3. Net north-south: +5 - 5 = 0. Net east-west: 3 km east.
4. He ends 3 km east of the start.
**Check:** Coordinates: (0,0) to (0,5) to (3,5) to (3,0). The distance from the origin is 3, along the east axis.
**Explain simply:**
- Right turns go in the order N, E, S, W; left turns go the other way.
- Use coordinates: north is +y, east is +x.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the last leg were 9 km?* Distance = root of 9 + 16 = 5 km (3 east, 4 south: south-east). 2) *Left turn first?* He would end 3 km west.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 13 km by adding legs, or "south-east".

### Q17 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a class of 40 students, Priya's rank from the top is 12. What is her rank from the bottom?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ranking items appear in standard reasoning sets (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** The "plus one" in rank problems.
**Say this in the interview:** "Rank from bottom = total minus rank from top plus 1: 40 - 12 + 1 = 29."
**Answer:** **29th from the bottom.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 11 students are above Priya (ranks 1 to 11).
2. Students below her: 40 - 12 = 28.
3. Her rank from the bottom is 28 + 1 = 29.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Bottom rank = total - top rank + 1.
**Check:** Top rank + bottom rank - 1 = 12 + 29 - 1 = 40. Matches.
**Explain simply:**
- Rank counts the person too, so the two ranks overlap by one (the person herself).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Rank 5 from bottom in 40?* 36th from the top. 2) *Class size from 12 and 29?* 12 + 29 - 1 = 40.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 28 (forgetting the +1).

### Q18 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Arun ranks 7th from the top and 28th from the bottom in a class. How many students are in the class?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ranking items appear in standard reasoning sets (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Subtracting the double-counted person.
**Say this in the interview:** "Total = top rank + bottom rank - 1 = 7 + 28 - 1 = 34."
**Answer:** **34 students.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Students above Arun: 6. Students below Arun: 27.
2. Add Arun himself: 6 + 27 + 1 = 34.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Total = top + bottom - 1.
**Check:** From the top, 34 - 7 + 1 = 28 from the bottom. Matches.
**Explain simply:**
- Adding the two ranks counts Arun twice, so take one off.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *15th from top, 20th from bottom?* 34. 2) *What if the ranks were in a row from left and right?* Same formula.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 35 (7 + 28).

### Q19 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 P is taller than Q, Q is taller than R, S is taller than P but shorter than T. Who is the tallest and who is the shortest?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ordering items are standard in reasoning sets (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Merging partial orders into one chain.
**Say this in the interview:** "Chain them: T > S > P > Q > R. So T is tallest and R is shortest."
**Answer:** **Tallest: T. Shortest: R.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. P > Q > R (first two clues).
2. S is taller than P and shorter than T: T > S > P.
3. Join: T > S > P > Q > R.
**Check:** Brute force over all 120 orders gives only T, S, P, Q, R; the answer is unique.
**Explain simply:**
- Write the clues as "greater than" arrows and link equal names into a single line.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who is third tallest?* P. 2) *Who is the middle person?* P.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Naming S as tallest because it is bigger than P.

### Q20 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 If A = 1, B = 2, ... Z = 26, what is the sum of the letter values of the word SQL?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); alphabet position items are standard (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Alphabet positions at speed.
**Say this in the interview:** "S is 19, Q is 17, L is 12. 19 + 17 + 12 = 48."
**Answer:** **48**
**Step-by-step working:**

### Q24 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Statements: All pens are books. Some books are bags. No bag is a box. Conclusions: I. Some books are not boxes. II. Some pens are bags. III. No pen is a box. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic listed in TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture and Cognizant prep pages (prep-site claim, unverified)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Syllogism: accept only what is true in every possible picture (Venn diagram thinking).
**Say this in the interview:** "Only conclusion I follows. Some books are bags, and no bag is a box, so those books are not boxes. The pen-bag link and the pen-box link are not forced."
**Answer:** **Only I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. All pens are books: the Pen circle sits fully inside Book.
2. Some books are bags: Book and Bag overlap.
3. No bag is a box: Bag and Box are completely apart.
4. Conclusion I (Some books are not boxes): the books that are bags are not boxes. These books exist, so some books are not boxes. Follows.
5. Conclusion II (Some pens are bags): the bag-books might be outside the Pen circle. Not forced. Does not follow.
6. Conclusion III (No pen is a box): a pen could be a box-book that is not a bag, since only Bag and Box are separated. Not forced. Does not follow.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** A conclusion follows only if you cannot draw any valid picture that breaks it. Draw one picture where it fails: Pen overlapping Box (outside Bag) breaks III; Pen away from Bag breaks II.
**Check:** Tested against all possible set-combinations by script: only I holds in every case.
**Explain simply:**
- "Some" means at least one. It does not say which group.
- "Follows" means true in every possible picture, not just in the picture you drew first.
**Likely follow-ups:** If the statement were "All books are bags", would II follow? Yes: pens are books, books are bags, so some pens are bags (given pens exist).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing III because pens "look" far from boxes. Two groups both linked to a third need not be linked to each other.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What formula gives the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand of a clock at H hours and M minutes?

4. Gap = (30H + 0.5M) - 6M = 30H - 5.5M; take the absolute value.

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How many odd days are there in 100 years, and what does an odd day mean?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Calendar basics: odd days as the leftover after full weeks.
**Say this in the interview:** "An odd day is a day left over after dividing the days by 7. 100 years has 5 odd days."
**Answer:** **5 odd days. An odd day is the remainder when the number of days is divided by 7.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 100 years contain 24 leap years (1900 is not a leap year, so the century has 24, not 25) and 76 ordinary years.
2. Days = 76 x 365 + 24 x 366 = 27,740 + 8,784 = 36,524.
3. 36,524 / 7 = 5217 weeks and 5 days remaining (7 x 5217 = 36,519).
4. So 5 odd days.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** An ordinary year has 1 odd day (365 = 52 weeks + 1), a leap year 2. 76 x 1 + 24 x 2 = 124; 124 mod 7 = 5.
**Check:** Verified by script. The standard rule: 100 years = 5, 200 = 3, 300 = 1, 400 = 0 odd days.
**Explain simply:**
- Weeks repeat every 7 days, so only the leftover days shift the weekday.
- If today is Monday, after 5 odd days it is Saturday.
**Likely follow-ups:** How many odd days in 400 years? 0 (400 years is an exact number of weeks, 20,871 weeks).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Counting 25 leap years in 100 years (gives 6), forgetting the century year is not leap.

### Q46 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the most common traps in syllogism questions (for example, treating "some" as "all", or ignoring the "either-or" case)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); syllogisms appear in reasoning sections of many service-company tests per prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you reason only from the given statements and know the formal rules.
**Say this in the interview:** "I use only what the statements say, treat 'some' as 'at least one, maybe all', and check for the either-or pair before saying neither follows."
**Answer:** **Main traps: (1) "some" read as "all"; (2) "some" read as "some not"; (3) using real-world knowledge; (4) wrongly reversing "All A are B"; (5) missing the either-or case; (6) treating "no" as "some not" or the reverse.**
**Explain simply:**
- "Some" means at least one and possibly all. "Some doctors are teachers" does not say that some doctors are not teachers.
- "All A are B" does not mean "All B are A". All HR managers are employees; not all employees are HR managers.
- Use the "possible diagram" method: draw all the possible Venn diagrams. A conclusion follows only if it holds in every diagram.
- Either-or: if neither conclusion follows alone, but they are contradictory (for example "All X are Y" and "Some X are not Y", or "Some X are Y" and "No X is Y"), then "either I or II follows". Both must involve the same subject and predicate.
**Worked mini-example:** Statements: Some doctors are teachers; All teachers are writers. "All doctors are writers" may be true or false, and "Some doctors are not writers" may be true or false, yet exactly one of them must be true: either I or II follows (see Q55).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "No A is B" imply "No B is A"?* Yes. 2) *Does "Some A are B" imply "Some B are A"?* Yes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "neither" when the pair is complementary; accepting "some A are not B" from "some A are B".

### Q48 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between deductive and inductive reasoning, with one workplace example each?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand certainty versus probability in reasoning.
**Say this in the interview:** "Deduction goes from a general rule to a certain conclusion; induction goes from observed cases to a likely general rule."
**Answer:** **Deductive: general to specific, conclusion is certain if the premises are true. Inductive: specific to general, conclusion is only probable.**
**Explain simply:**
- Deductive example: "All employees must complete the safety training. Asha is an employee. So Asha must complete it." If the first two are true, the third cannot be false.
- Inductive example: "The last five hires from campus X performed well, so campus X likely produces good hires." Useful, but a sixth hire could disappoint.
- Deduction preserves truth; induction extends knowledge but carries risk.
- In data work: hypothesis testing applies a general rule (deductive flavour); spotting a trend in samples is inductive.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which is used in syllogisms?* Deductive. 2) *Which is used when forecasting from past sales?* Inductive.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking "deductive" means "stronger person" or mixing them up; calling a valid-looking argument with false premises "certain".

### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In a survey of 100 employees, 60 use Excel, 45 use SQL and 20 use neither. How many use both?

**Say this in the interview:** "80 use at least one tool. 60 + 45 = 105 counts the overlap twice, so both = 105 - 80 = 25."
**Answer:** **25 employees**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Use at least one tool = 100 - 20 = 80.
2. Excel + SQL = 60 + 45 = 105.
3. The extra 105 - 80 = 25 is the people counted twice, i.e. those who use both.

### Q54 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statement: P > Q >= R = S < T. Conclusions: I. P > S. II. T > Q. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); inequality-coding items are a standard reasoning topic.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Linking symbols through a chain, and knowing when there is no link.
**Say this in the interview:** "P > Q >= R = S gives P > S, so I follows. T is only known to be above S; its link to Q is not fixed, so II does not follow. Only I."
**Answer:** **Only conclusion I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. P > Q and Q >= R and R = S, so P > Q >= S, hence P > S. I is true.
2. T > S and Q >= S: both Q and T are above S (or Q equals S), but there is no direct or indirect comparison between T and Q.
3. Example where II fails: S = 1, R = 1, Q = 3, P = 4, T = 2. Here T < Q.
4. Example where II holds: Q = 2, T = 5. So II is sometimes true, which means it does not follow definitely.
**Check:** Tried all value combinations 0-4 by script: I always true; II true in some and false in others.
**Explain simply:**
- A conclusion follows only if it holds in every case.
- Both Q and T are greater than S, but that does not rank them against each other (two "greater than" arrows into the same point).
- Neither I nor II is the exact opposite of the other (II is T > Q; the opposite would be T <= Q), so the either-or rule does not apply here.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "P > R" follow?* Yes: P > Q >= R. 2) *Does "Q > S" follow?* No: Q >= S only, they could be equal.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming T is the largest because it is written last; treating "greater than or equal" as strictly greater.

### Q55 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statements: Some doctors are teachers. All teachers are writers. Conclusions: I. All doctors are writers. II. Some doctors are not writers. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** The either-or case, where two conclusions contradict each other and neither is certain alone.
**Say this in the interview:** "Neither follows on its own, but they are exact opposites, so one of them must be true: either I or II follows."
**Answer:** **Either I or II follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Some doctors are teachers, and all teachers are writers, so those doctors are writers. That much is certain.
2. Other doctors (not teachers) are not mentioned: they could be writers or not.
3. I (All doctors are writers) is possible but not certain; II (Some doctors are not writers) is possible but not certain.
4. I and II are contradictory: if I is false then II is true, and if I is true then II is false. One of them must be true.
5. Hence "either I or II".
**Check:** Checked by script over all small models: every model makes exactly one of I and II true, and each is false in some model.
**Explain simply:**
- Either-or rule: apply only when the two conclusions share the same subject and predicate, are individually not sure, and together cover all possibilities (All X are Y versus Some X are not Y).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some writers are doctors" follow?* Yes (the doctors who are teachers are writers). 2) *Does "Some teachers are doctors" follow?* Yes (reverse of "some").
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "neither" (the most common wrong answer) or picking I because it sounds natural.

### Q56 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statement: The company has decided to cut travel expenses by 20% this year. Assumptions: I. Travel is a meaningful part of the company's costs. II. Employees will stop travelling completely. Which assumption(s) are implicit?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); critical-reasoning items of this type are listed in Accenture prep material (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Telling a hidden belief the statement depends on from an over-reach.
**Say this in the interview:** "The decision only makes sense if travel is a real cost, so I is implicit. A 20% cut does not mean no travel at all, so II is not."
**Answer:** **Only assumption I is implicit.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Ask: what must be true for this decision to be reasonable?
2. If travel were a tiny cost, cutting it would not be worth announcing. So I is assumed.
3. A 20% cut means 80% of the spend continues, so travel continues. II contradicts the statement.
**Explain simply:**
- An assumption is an unstated belief the statement needs in order to make sense.
- Test: if the assumption were false, would the decision look pointless? If yes, it is implicit.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is "the cut can be achieved without hurting business" implicit?* Reasonably yes, since the company expects it to work. 2) *Would "video calls are a possible substitute" be implicit?* Not necessarily; it is one way, not required.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing absolute claims ("completely", "all", "never"); mistaking a consequence for an assumption.

### Q57 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Call-centre attrition fell from 30% to 25% after the pay hike, so the pay hike caused the fall." Which fact would weaken this argument most? (a) Attrition also fell from 28% to 24% in a branch that got no pay hike. (b) The pay hike was 10%. (c) The call centre has 400 employees. (d) Attrition is measured yearly.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Cause versus coincidence: spotting that a comparison group explains the change.
**Say this in the interview:** "Option (a) shows attrition fell even where there was no pay hike, so something else probably explains it. That weakens the claim the most."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. The argument says: pay hike came first, attrition fell, so the hike caused it.
2. A weakening fact shows the fall would have happened anyway or had another cause.
3. (a): a branch with no pay hike also saw a fall (28% to 24%), so the fall is not unique to the pay hike. It points to a common factor (for example a weak job market).
4. (b), (c), (d) are facts about size, amount and measurement; they say nothing about cause.
**Explain simply:**
- This is the "correlation is not causation" idea: two things happening together does not prove one caused the other.
- A control group (no pay hike) is the cleanest test. This is the same logic behind A/B tests in analytics.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would strengthen it?* A no-hike branch where attrition stayed at 30%. 2) *What if the no-hike branch fell from 28% to 20%?* Then it fell even more without a hike, which weakens it still further.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because the numbers look relevant; ignoring the no-hike branch.

### Q58 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Facing north, Riya turns 90 degrees clockwise, then 180 degrees anticlockwise, then 45 degrees clockwise. Which direction is she facing now?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); direction sense is listed among reasoning topics for Wipro in prep-site material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Tracking net rotation, with clockwise as positive and anticlockwise as negative.
**Say this in the interview:** "Net turn is +90 - 180 + 45 = -45 degrees, so 45 degrees anticlockwise from north: north-west."
**Answer:** **North-west**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Start: North (0 degrees).
2. 90 clockwise: East.
3. 180 anticlockwise: from East, half-turn to West.
4. 45 clockwise: from West, turn towards North by 45 degrees: North-west.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Add clockwise, subtract anticlockwise: 90 - 180 + 45 = -45. Negative means 45 degrees anticlockwise from north, i.e. North-west.
**Check:** Worked by script using 90-degree steps (N, E, S, W) and the net angle -45 degrees, which is North-west.
**Explain simply:**
- Clockwise is the direction a clock hand moves (north to east to south to west).
- Directions in between: NE, SE, SW, NW sit 45 degrees from the main four.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Facing south, turn 135 degrees anticlockwise?* Anticlockwise from south goes to east first (90), then 45 more to north-east: North-east. 2) *If the last turn were 45 anticlockwise?* -135 net, so South-west.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating anticlockwise as clockwise (90 + 180 + 45 = 315 degrees happens to land on North-west here, but the method is wrong and fails on other numbers); or ending at South-west by turning the wrong way on the last step.

### Q59 ⭐⭐ 🟡 One morning after sunrise, Vikas stood facing a pole and the shadow of the pole fell to his right. Which direction was he facing?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Shadow rule: morning shadows fall west, evening shadows fall east.
**Say this in the interview:** "In the morning the sun is in the east, so shadows fall to the west. The shadow is on his right, so west is his right, which means he faces south."
**Answer:** **South**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Morning: the sun is in the east, so the shadow points west.
2. The shadow is to Vikas's right, so his right-hand side is west.
3. If your right hand points west, you face south (facing north, right is east; facing south, right is west).
**Check:** Facing south, east is on your left and west on your right. Consistent.
**Explain simply:**
- The shadow always points away from the sun.
- Right hand sits 90 degrees clockwise from the face direction: south then west.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If it were evening?* Shadow points east, so he faces north. 2) *Morning and the shadow falls behind him?* Behind him is west, so he faces east.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying the shadow falls east in the morning.

### Q60 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Asha, Bala, Chitra and Dev each use a different one of Excel, SQL, Python and Power BI. Asha does not use Excel or SQL. Bala does not use Python or Power BI. Chitra does not use Excel or Power BI. Dev does not use SQL, Python or Excel. Who uses Python?

**Step-by-step working:**

### Q61 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statements: All laptops are devices. No device is a fruit. Conclusions: I. No laptop is a fruit. II. Some fruits are not laptops. Assuming every named group has at least one member, which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can chain "all" and "no" statements and spot the extra assumption in conclusion II.
**Say this in the interview:** "Laptops sit inside devices, and devices sit completely outside fruits, so no laptop is a fruit. Since fruits exist and none of them is a device, some fruits are certainly not laptops. Both follow."
**Answer:** **Both I and II follow.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Draw a small circle (laptops) inside a bigger circle (devices). Fruits is a third circle that does not touch the devices circle.
2. Conclusion I: laptops are inside devices, devices do not touch fruits, so laptops do not touch fruits. "No laptop is a fruit" follows.
3. Conclusion II: the question tells us fruits has at least one member. That fruit is outside devices, so it is outside laptops too. "Some fruits are not laptops" follows.
**Check:** verified by script (every small "world" that fits the two statements and has all three groups non-empty satisfies both conclusions).
**Explain simply:**
- "All X are Y" means the X circle is fully inside the Y circle. "No Y is Z" means the circles do not overlap.
- A "No" statement always flips freely: "No laptop is a fruit" and "No fruit is a laptop" say the same thing.
- "Some ... are not ..." needs at least one member to exist. The question supplies that assumption; without it conclusion II would not be safe.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if fruits could be empty?* Then II fails (there might be no fruits at all) and only I follows. 2) *Would "Some devices are not laptops" follow?* No; devices could be exactly the laptops.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Rejecting II because "nothing was said about fruits and laptops together"; it follows through the device link. Also forgetting that the existence assumption is what makes "some ... not" valid.

### Q62 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You have a 3-litre jug and a 5-litre jug with no markings and unlimited water. How do you measure exactly 4 litres?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Systematic trial of fill, pour and empty moves.
**Say this in the interview:** "Fill the 5, pour into the 3 leaving 2, empty the 3, move the 2 across, refill the 5 and top up the 3; 4 litres remain in the 5-litre jug."
**Answer:** **Yes, 4 litres, in 6 steps (shown below).**
**Step-by-step working:** (5-litre jug, 3-litre jug)
1. Fill the 5-litre jug: (5, 0).
2. Pour 5 into 3 until full: (2, 3).
3. Empty the 3-litre jug: (2, 0).
4. Pour the 2 litres into the 3-litre jug: (0, 2).
5. Fill the 5-litre jug again: (5, 2).
6. Pour from 5 into 3 until it is full; it needs only 1 litre: (4, 3). The 5-litre jug now holds exactly 4 litres.
**Check:** verified by script (a shortest-path search over all jug states finds 4 litres in 6 moves, and no shorter route exists). Hand check: 5 - 3 = 2 left, 2 + 3 = 5 total moved, then 5 - 1 = 4.
**Explain simply:**
- With jugs of 3 and 5 (no common factor except 1) you can make any whole number up to 5, because each pour moves you by 3 or by 5 modulo each other.
- Think of it as "5 minus 1": you want the 5-litre jug short of full by 1, and 3 minus 2 gives that 1.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How to measure 1 litre?* After step 5 you hold (5, 2); pouring 1 litre into the 3-litre jug leaves 4, and emptying both then pouring the 4 into the 3 leaves 1. 2) *Can you measure 4 with 6 and 9 litre jugs?* No, 4 is not a multiple of 3.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Wasting steps by emptying the wrong jug; forgetting that the leftover after a pour is the "difference" you are collecting.

### Q63 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You have 8 identical-looking balls and one is heavier. Using a balance scale, what is the minimum number of weighings needed to find the heavy ball, and how?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting into three groups (not two) because a balance has three outcomes.
**Say this in the interview:** "Two weighings. Weigh 3 against 3: if they tip, the heavy ball is in the lower group; if equal, it is among the 2 left out. Then one more weighing of at most 3 balls finds it."
**Answer:** **2 weighings.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Split the 8 balls into groups of 3, 3 and 2.
2. Weighing 1: put 3 on each pan. Lower pan means the heavy ball is among those 3. Balanced means it is among the 2 not weighed.
3. Weighing 2 if you have 3 balls: weigh one against another. Lower pan is heavy; balanced means the third ball is heavy.
4. Weighing 2 if you have 2 balls: weigh one against the other; the lower pan is heavy.
5. One weighing is not enough: it has only 3 outcomes, and 8 balls need 8 distinct answers.
**Check:** verified by script (a search over all weighings shows 2 works for 8 balls and 1 does not).
**Explain simply:**
- Each weighing has 3 outcomes (left down, right down, balanced), so k weighings can separate up to 3 to the power k balls: 1 weighing handles 3, 2 handle 9.
- 8 is at most 9, so 2 weighings suffice; the splitting should be as even as possible in threes.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many balls can 3 weighings handle (one heavy)?* 27. 2) *What if you split 4 vs 4 first?* You need 2 more weighings (4 balls need 2), so 3 in total: not optimal.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Splitting 4 and 4 (answer 3); forgetting the leftover group, which the balanced outcome points to.

### Q64 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Which year after 2026 will have exactly the same calendar as 2026?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Odd days and leap years.
**Say this in the interview:** "A normal year shifts the weekday of 1 January by 1, a leap year by 2. I add shifts until the total is a multiple of 7, and 2026 is not a leap year, so the match is 2037."
**Answer:** **2037**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A year of 365 days is 52 weeks and 1 odd day, so the next year starts 1 weekday later. A leap year (366 days) has 2 odd days.
2. Start: 2026 begins on Thursday (and is not a leap year).
3. Go year by year: 2027 Fri, 2028 Sat (leap), 2029 Mon (2028 gave 2 odd days), 2030 Tue, 2031 Wed, 2032 Thu (leap year), 2033 Sat, 2034 Sun, 2035 Mon, 2036 Tue, 2037 Thu.
4. 2037 starts on Thursday again. Both 2026 and 2037 are non-leap years, so the whole calendar matches.
5. Total shift 2026 to 2037: 11 years + 3 leap years (2028, 2032, 2036) = 14 odd days = 0 after dividing by 7.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Count years + number of leap years in between; stop when the sum is a multiple of 7. It is 11 + 3 = 14.
**Check:** verified by script (first year after 2026 that starts on Thursday and is non-leap is 2037).
**Explain simply:**
- "Odd days" are the days left over after removing whole weeks; only they move the weekday.
- The calendar repeats only if the first weekday matches AND the leap-year status matches.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Next same calendar after 2024 (a leap year)?* 2052. 2) *Same calendar as 2027?* 2038 (shift 11 years + 3 leap years).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking 2032 or 2033 because the first weekday matches once, without checking leap status; forgetting 2028, 2032, 2036 are leap years.

### Q65 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Of 200 candidates, 100 know Excel, 80 know SQL and 60 know Python. 30 know Excel and SQL, 25 know SQL and Python, 20 know Excel and Python, and 10 know all three. How many know at least one of the three?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Three-set inclusion-exclusion, and not being distracted by the 200.
**Say this in the interview:** "Add the three sets, subtract each pair, add back the triple overlap: 240 minus 75 plus 10 is 175."
**Answer:** **175 candidates know at least one of the three.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Add the singles: 100 + 80 + 60 = 240 (anyone who knows two skills was counted twice, and anyone who knows all three, three times).
2. Subtract the pairs: 30 + 25 + 20 = 75, giving 240 - 75 = 165. Now the all-three group has been subtracted three times, which removed it entirely.
3. Add back the triple group once: 165 + 10 = 175.
4. So 175 know at least one; the other 200 - 175 = 25 know none.
**Check:** Count the exact groups: all three 10; only Excel+SQL 20, only SQL+Python 15, only Excel+Python 10; only Excel 100-20-10-10 = 60... check: Excel only = 100 - 30 - 20 + 10 = 60; SQL only = 80 - 30 - 25 + 10 = 35; Python only = 60 - 25 - 20 + 10 = 25. Total 10 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 60 + 35 + 25 = 175. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Formula: |A or B or C| = A + B + C - (AB + BC + AC) + ABC.
- The "30 know Excel and SQL" figures include the 10 who know all three.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many know exactly one?* 60 + 35 + 25 = 120. 2) *How many know exactly two?* 20 + 15 + 10 = 45.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to add back the 10; treating "30 know Excel and SQL" as "exactly" two skills.

### Q66 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How many times do the hour hand and the minute hand of a clock overlap in 12 hours?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative-speed reasoning on a clock.
**Say this in the interview:** "The minute hand laps the hour hand 11 times in 12 hours, but the 11th overlap is exactly at 12 again, so it is 11 overlaps."
**Answer:** **11 times**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. The minute hand makes 12 full turns in 12 hours; the hour hand makes 1.
2. The minute hand gains 11 laps on the hour hand, and each lap-gain is one overlap: 11 overlaps.
3. They overlap every 12/11 hours (65 minutes 27 seconds roughly, i.e. 65 5/11 minutes): at 12:00, about 1:05:27, 2:10:54, ... , 10:54:33.
4. The 11th overlap would be at 12 hours, which is the 12:00 that starts the next cycle, so the count in one full cycle is 11, not 12.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** In 12 hours: 11. In 24 hours: 22.
**Check:** verified by script (overlap times 720k/11 minutes for k = 0 to 10 lie within 12 hours, k = 11 equals 12:00 again).
**Explain simply:**
- The minute hand moves 6 degrees per minute, the hour hand 0.5 degrees per minute, so the minute hand gains 5.5 degrees per minute, and 360 / 5.5 = 65 5/11 minutes per overlap.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many times in a day (24 h)?* 22. 2) *How many times are the hands opposite in 12 hours?* 11.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 12 (counting 12:00 twice); thinking they overlap once per hour.

### Q67 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A is twice as old as B. Ten years ago A was three times as old as B. What are their present ages?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Forming two equations from words.
**Say this in the interview:** "A = 2B and A - 10 = 3(B - 10). Substituting gives B = 20, A = 40."
**Answer:** **A is 40 years and B is 20 years.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let B's age be B, so A's age is 2B.
2. Ten years ago A was 2B - 10 and B was B - 10, and A was three times B: 2B - 10 = 3(B - 10).
3. Expand: 2B - 10 = 3B - 30, so B = 20.
4. Then A = 2 x 20 = 40.
**Check:** Ten years ago they were 30 and 10: 30 is three times 10. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Use one letter for the younger person and write the older in terms of it; then restate the second condition 10 years back.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When will A be 1.5 times B?* The gap stays 20, so A = 1.5B means 0.5B = 20, B = 40: in 20 years (A = 60). 2) *Change to "five years ago A was 3 times B"?* 2B - 5 = 3B - 15 gives B = 10, A = 20.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting 10 from only one person; solving "ten years from now" by mistake (that would give a different answer).

### Q68 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Three people A, B and C say: A: "B is lying." B: "C is lying." C: "A and B are both lying." Who is telling the truth?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Testing each assumption of who is truthful and spotting contradictions.
**Say this in the interview:** "I test each person as the truth-teller. If A is truthful, B is lying, so C is truthful, which contradicts A (C says A is lying). If B is truthful, C is lying and A is lying, which fits. Only B tells the truth."
**Answer:** **Only B is telling the truth.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Suppose A is truthful: then B is lying. B says "C is lying", which must be false, so C is truthful. But C says A is lying, which contradicts A being truthful. Impossible.
2. Suppose B is truthful: then C is lying (B's claim) and A is lying (A said B is lying, which is false). Check C: C says "A and B are both lying"; B is truthful so this is false, consistent with C lying. Possible.
3. Suppose C is truthful: then A and B both lie. A lies, so B is truthful; this contradicts B lying. Impossible.
4. Only the second case works, and it is the only consistent scenario.
**Check:** verified by script (all 8 truth/lie combinations tested; only B truthful survives).
**Explain simply:**
- "Lying" means the statement is false, so a liar's statement must be checked as false, not "the opposite of everything".
- If a case leads to a contradiction, that case is dead; keep going until one survives.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many truth/lie cases are there for 3 people?* 8 (2 x 2 x 2); listing them all is a safe method. 2) *Why is it safe to test cases?* Because each case either fits all three statements or breaks one.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming "B is lying" means B says only false things about everything; stopping after finding one consistent case without checking others (here there is exactly one).

### Q69 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Six candidates A, B, C, D, E and F are interviewed on six consecutive days, Monday to Saturday, one per day. C is interviewed on Wednesday. A is interviewed two days before C. B is interviewed the day after F and after C. D is not interviewed on Saturday. E is interviewed before D. On which day is B interviewed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Placing fixed clues first, then eliminating.
**Say this in the interview:** "C is Wednesday, A is Monday, B comes the day after F and later than C, so B is Thursday, Friday or Saturday; testing the rest leaves one arrangement, with B on Saturday."
**Answer:** **Saturday**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. C is on Wednesday (day 3). A is two days before C: Monday.
2. B is after C, so B is on Thu, Fri or Sat; F is the day before B.
3. D is not Saturday, and E is before D.
4. Days left for B, D, E, F: Tue, Thu, Fri, Sat. The pair (F, B) must be consecutive days: only (Thu, Fri) or (Fri, Sat), since Tue is not next to a later free day.
5. If F = Thu and B = Fri, the free days are Tue and Sat for D and E; D cannot be Saturday so D = Tue, but E must be before D (Monday or earlier, taken). Fails.
6. If F = Fri and B = Sat, the free days are Tue and Thu; E before D gives E = Tue, D = Thu. Valid.
7. Final order: Mon A, Tue E, Wed C, Thu D, Fri F, Sat B.
**Check:** verified by script (all 720 orders tested; exactly one satisfies every clue). Clue read as "the day right after F, and later than C"; if it were also "the day right after C", it would clash with C and F having to be the same day, so the sensible reading is the first.
**Explain simply:**
- Fix known days first, then use "consecutive" clues as a block (F then B) to cut the possibilities.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who is on Friday?* F. 2) *Which day is E?* Tuesday.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading the B clue as "day after C" (impossible with F); forgetting E must be before D.

### Q70 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Statements: Some A are B. All B are C. No C is D. Some D are E. Conclusions: I. Some A are C. II. No B is D. III. Some E are not C. IV. Some A are D. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified). (syllogism items listed in TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture and Cognizant syllabi, unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Four-statement syllogism chains and the difference between "must follow" and "might be true".
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw circles. Some A inside B, B inside C, so some A are C (I follows). B inside C and C does not touch D, so no B is D (II follows). Some D are E, and D has no overlap with C, so those E are not C (III follows). A and D are never forced to meet (IV does not follow)."
**Answer:** **I, II and III follow; IV does not.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Some A are B, and all B are C: the A that are B are also C. So **I (some A are C) follows**.
2. All B are C, and no C is D: so nothing in B can be D. **II (no B is D) follows**.
3. Some D are E. Those D are not C (no C is D), so some E are not C. **III follows**.
4. Some A are B means A touches B, and B is inside C, which is away from D. Other A members might touch D, but nothing forces it. **IV does not follow**.
**Check:** verified by script (545 small worlds satisfying all four statements; I, II and III hold in all of them, IV fails in 225).
**Explain simply:**
- "Follows" means true in every possible picture, not just one.
- "Some A are B" plus "all B are C" gives a "some" conclusion (some A are C); "all" plus "no" gives "no".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some E are D" follow?* Yes (reverse of "some D are E"). 2) *Does "Some C are A" follow?* Yes (reverse of I).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Marking IV true because A and B overlap and "everything is connected"; missing that III follows through the "No C is D" link.

### Q71 ⭐ 🟢 Why do companies such as Mu Sigma, Fractal and service MNCs test logical reasoning for analyst roles, and how does it connect to day-to-day data work?
**Asked at:** Mu Sigma (muAPT includes logical reasoning, puzzles, series, syllogism, seating) and Fractal (Reasoning Ability section reported in older candidate reports); service MNC online tests also include reasoning (prep-site and candidate reports, partly unverified, per research/Book06_Part07_sources.md).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand why the skill matters, not just the puzzle method.
**Say this in the interview:** "Reasoning tests are a fast, fair proxy for structured thinking: breaking a messy situation into clear conditions, checking each, and not jumping to conclusions. That is the daily habit in data work, from validating a dashboard to choosing the right filter."
**Answer:** **They are testing structured, step-by-step thinking under time pressure, which is what analysis needs.**
**Explain simply:**
- Conditions and constraints: a seating puzzle is like a data-validation rule; many clues must all be true at once, just as a report must satisfy every filter.
- Conclusions vs assumptions: syllogism and "assumption" questions train you to separate what the data says from what you hope it says.
- Spotting hidden patterns: series and coding-decoding are tiny pattern-finding exercises, like noticing a seasonal spike.
- Cause vs correlation: weaken-the-argument questions resemble asking "did the pay hike actually cause the drop in attrition?"
- Fresh graduates have little work history, so a standard test is an easy, cheap, comparable filter for thousands of applicants.
**Worked mini-example (HR flavour):** Attrition fell after a pay hike. A reasoning-minded analyst asks, "Did it also fall in teams that got no hike?" That is the same move as Q57 above.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you think the test predicts job performance?* Partly: it checks accuracy and speed, not business knowledge, so interviews and cases cover the rest. 2) *How do you prepare?* Practise a few puzzle types daily, check answers, and note the method.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "to filter people out" without linking to real work; claiming company-specific facts you cannot support.

### Q72 ⭐ 🟡 On an island, knights always tell the truth and knaves always lie. A says, "We are both knaves." What are A and B?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Testing the two possibilities for A against his own statement.
**Say this in the interview:** "If A were a knight, he would be telling the truth that both are knaves, which contradicts him being a knight. So A is a knave; then his statement is false, so not both are knaves, and since A is, B must be a knight."
**Answer:** **A is a knave and B is a knight.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Suppose A is a knight (truthful). Then "we are both knaves" must be true, so A is a knave. Contradiction.
2. So A is a knave (liar). His statement must be false.
3. The statement is false if it is not true that both are knaves. A is a knave, so B must not be a knave: B is a knight.
**Check:** verified by script (all 4 knight/knave combinations tried; only A knave, B knight is consistent).
**Explain simply:**
- A liar's sentence is false; "both are knaves" is false whenever at least one is a knight.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If A says "at least one of us is a knave"?* A is a knight, B a knave. 2) *If A says "We are both knights"?* A is a knave (then B could be either); not enough alone.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Concluding B is a knave because "A says so"; liars' statements must be flipped, not trusted.

### Q73 ⭐ 🟡 Three switches outside a closed room control three bulbs inside. You can enter the room only once. How do you find which switch controls which bulb?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Using a second property (heat) when only one observation (light) is available.
**Say this in the interview:** "I turn switch 1 on for a few minutes, then off, turn switch 2 on, and walk in. The lit bulb is switch 2, the warm unlit bulb is switch 1, the cold unlit bulb is switch 3."
**Answer:** **Use a bulb's warmth as the second clue (steps below).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Turn switch 1 on and leave it on for about 10 minutes, so its bulb heats up.
2. Turn switch 1 off and turn switch 2 on.
3. Enter the room (the only time). The bulb that is on belongs to switch 2.
4. Touch the two unlit bulbs: the warm one belongs to switch 1; the cold one belongs to switch 3.
**Explain simply:**
- One look gives only two states (on or off), which cannot separate three bulbs. Warmth adds a third state (warm and off).
- A modern LED may not get hot enough; the puzzle assumes old-style filament bulbs.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the bulbs are LEDs?* Leave the room door ajar, or use another memory (e.g., a bulb that has flickered), but the classic answer needs heat. 2) *Can you do it with two bulbs?* Yes: turn one on and enter.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Trying only on/off combinations, which cannot separate three bulbs in one visit.

### Q74 ⭐ 🟡 You have two ropes that each burn for exactly 60 minutes but burn unevenly. How do you measure exactly 45 minutes?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Using both ends of a rope to halve the burn time.
**Say this in the interview:** "Light rope 1 at both ends and rope 2 at one end. Rope 1 finishes at 30 minutes; then light the other end of rope 2, which has 30 minutes of burn left and now finishes in 15. Total 45."
**Answer:** **45 minutes (steps below).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. At time 0, light both ends of rope 1 and one end of rope 2.
2. Rope 1 burns from both ends, so it burns out in 30 minutes however unevenly (the two flames always burn exactly one rope's worth in total).
3. At 30 minutes, rope 2 has 30 minutes of burning left. Light its other end now.
4. Two flames on 30 minutes' worth of rope finish in 15 minutes: that is at 45 minutes.
**Explain simply:**
- Lighting both ends doubles the speed: the same total burning needs half the time, even if the rope burns unevenly.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Measure 15 minutes?* It is the last part of the same plan: from the moment you light the second end of rope 2 (at 30 minutes) until it finishes. 2) *Measure 30 minutes?* Light one rope at both ends.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming half the rope length equals half the time; the rope burns unevenly, so only the total burn matters.

### Q75 ⭐ 🟡 A snail climbs 3 m up a 10 m well each day and slips back 2 m each night. On which day does it reach the top?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Not applying the average blindly; the last day has no slip-back.
**Say this in the interview:** "Net gain is 1 m per day, but on the final day the snail reaches the top before slipping. After 7 days it is at 7 m; on day 8 it climbs 3 m to 10 m and is out. Answer: day 8, not day 10."
**Answer:** **Day 8**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Each full day and night gives a net 3 - 2 = 1 m.
2. After 7 days (and nights) the snail is at 7 m.
3. On day 8, it climbs 3 m during the day: 7 + 3 = 10 m, the top of the well. It is out before the night.
4. So it reaches the top on day 8.
**Check:** verified by script (day-by-day simulation reaches 10 m on day 8).
**Explain simply:**
- Use the formula: days = (height - day climb) / net gain + 1 = (10 - 3) / 1 + 1 = 8.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Well 20 m, climb 5, slip 3?* (20 - 5) / 2 = 7.5, round up to 8, plus 1 = day 9. 2) *What is its position at the end of day 7's night?* 7 m.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering day 10 by dividing 10 m by the net 1 m; forgetting that the slip does not happen once the snail is out.

### Q76 ⭐ 🟡 Three boxes are labelled Apples, Oranges and Mixed, and every label is wrong. You may pick one fruit from one box. Which box do you pick from, and how does that fix all labels?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Using the "all labels are wrong" fact to cut possibilities.
**Say this in the interview:** "Pick from the box labelled Mixed. It cannot really be mixed, so whatever fruit I pull out tells me its true content, and then the other two labels follow by elimination."
**Answer:** **Pick from the box labelled "Mixed".**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Every label is wrong, so the box labelled "Mixed" is actually all apples or all oranges.
2. Pick one fruit from it. Say it is an apple: this box is "Apples".
3. The box labelled "Oranges" cannot be oranges (label wrong), and "Apples" is now taken, so it must be "Mixed".
4. The box labelled "Apples" is then "Oranges".
5. (If the fruit is an orange, swap the roles: Mixed-labelled is Oranges, Oranges-labelled is Apples, Apples-labelled is Mixed.)
**Check:** hand check: with every label wrong there are only 2 possible arrangements (Apples-box is Oranges, Oranges-box is Mixed, Mixed-box is Apples, or the reverse); one fruit from the Mixed-labelled box tells which. Verified by script that exactly 2 arrangements exist.
**Explain simply:**
- Only the Mixed-labelled box tells you everything; a pick from the Apples-labelled box could be oranges or "mixed", which is ambiguous.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you pick from the box labelled Apples and get an orange?* It could be Oranges or Mixed: still ambiguous. 2) *How many wrong-label arrangements are there?* 2.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking from the Apples or Oranges box; forgetting that "Mixed" is the one label you can trust to be wrong in a useful way.

### Q77 ⭐ 🔴 Four people take 1, 2, 5 and 10 minutes to cross a bridge at night with one torch. At most two cross at a time at the slower person's speed. What is the minimum total time for all four to cross?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Not letting the fastest person escort everyone; sending the two slowest together.
**Say this in the interview:** "Send the 1 and 2 across, bring the 1 back, send the 5 and 10 together, bring the 2 back, then send the 1 and 2 across. Total 2 + 1 + 10 + 2 + 2 = 17 minutes."
**Answer:** **17 minutes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 1 and 2 cross together: 2 minutes (now 5 and 10 on the start side, torch on the far side).
2. 1 walks back with the torch: 1 minute.
3. 5 and 10 cross together: 10 minutes (the slowest pair crosses only once).
4. 2 walks back with the torch: 2 minutes.
5. 1 and 2 cross together again: 2 minutes.
6. Total: 2 + 1 + 10 + 2 + 2 = 17 minutes.
**Check:** verified by script (a shortest-path search over all states gives 17, and the "1 escorts everyone" plan takes 19).
**Explain simply:**
- The two slowest walkers waste the least time if they cross together; the 2 is the helper who returns the torch.
- The other plan (the 1 escorts everyone) is 2 + 1 + 5 + 1 + 10 = 19.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Times 1, 3, 6, 8?* 3 + 1 + 8 + 3 + 3 = 18, versus escort plan 3 + 1 + 6 + 1 + 8 = 19, so 18. 2) *Is 17 provable minimal?* Yes by the search.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 19 using the 1 as an escort; forgetting that the crossing time equals the slower person.

### Q78 ⭐ 🔴 Using two identical eggs in a 100-storey building, what is the minimum number of drops needed in the worst case to find the lowest floor from which an egg breaks?
**Asked at:** Mu Sigma (the 2 eggs and 100 floors puzzle is listed in a FACE Prep Mu Sigma guide, a prep-site report cited in research/interview_processes.md; unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Balancing the cost of a step: the first egg's drops against the second egg's walk-up.
**Say this in the interview:** "Drop from floors 14, 27, 39, 50 and so on, each step one floor smaller than the last, so the worst case is always 14. Total 14 drops."
**Answer:** **14 drops in the worst case.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let n be the worst-case number of drops. The first egg is dropped first from floor n. If it breaks, the second egg checks floors 1 to n - 1 one by one: total n drops.
2. If it does not break, the next step is only n - 1 floors higher (floor n + n - 1), because one drop has already been used: this keeps the worst case at n everywhere.
3. The floors covered: n + (n - 1) + ... + 1 = n(n + 1)/2. We need this to be at least 100.
4. n = 13 gives 91 (too few); n = 14 gives 105 (enough). So n = 14.
5. Drop sequence for the first egg: floors 14, 27, 39, 50, 60, 69, 77, 84, 90, 95, 99, 100.
**Check:** verified by script (dynamic programming over eggs and floors gives 14 for 100 floors and 2 eggs).
**Explain simply:**
- With one egg you must walk up one floor at a time. With two you can take big jumps first, then walk only inside the one gap.
- Equal jumps (10, 20, 30...) give a worst case of 19 drops (10 + 9). Shrinking jumps equalise the worst case.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *3 eggs, 100 floors?* 9 drops (verified by script). 2) *Jumps of 10?* Worst case 19.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 10 (jumps of 10) or 19; forgetting to reduce the jump after each non-break.

### Q79 ⭐ 🔴 You have 25 horses and a track that races 5 at a time, with no stopwatch. What is the minimum number of races needed to find the 3 fastest horses?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Pruning candidates by elimination rather than racing everyone against everyone.
**Say this in the interview:** "Five heat races, one race of the five winners, then one race among the 5 horses that could still be in the top 3. Seven races."
**Answer:** **7 races.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Races 1 to 5: split the 25 horses into 5 groups of 5 and race each group. Label each group's horses 1st to 5th (A1 to A5, B1 to B5, ... E1 to E5).
2. Race 6: race the 5 group winners (A1, B1, C1, D1, E1). Suppose the order is A1 > B1 > C1 > D1 > E1, so A1 is the fastest overall (rank 1).
3. Eliminate: D1 and E1 and their groups cannot be in the top 3 (D1 and E1 already have 3 or 4 faster horses). Also C2, C3, B3 (each has at least 3 faster horses above them) and anyone in the bottom of a group.
4. Candidates left for places 2 and 3: A2, A3, B1, B2, C1 (A1 is already first).
5. Race 7: race those 5. The first two finishers are 2nd and 3rd fastest overall.
**Check:** the count is the standard result for this puzzle (not proved by script here); the elimination in step 3 was checked by reasoning: 5 candidates, one race, 2 places. Each place needs a race of its own group, so fewer than 7 cannot cover all horses.
**Explain simply:**
- Each race gives only an order, not a time, so you can only compare horses inside the same race; you must use the results to chain comparisons.
- The key trick is that a horse is out as soon as 3 horses are known to be faster.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Only the fastest?* 6 races. 2) *Fastest 2?* 7 races.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Racing all combos; including A1 in the last race (already known as the fastest); keeping too many candidates.

### Q80 ⭐ 🔴 You have 12 identical-looking balls, one of which has a different weight (heavier or lighter, unknown). Using a balance scale at most 3 times, how do you find the odd ball and whether it is heavier or lighter?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting into groups of 4 and planning follow-up weighings in advance.
**Say this in the interview:** "Weigh 4 against 4. If balanced, the odd ball is in the last 4; if not, I use the tipped side to split into 'maybe heavy' and 'maybe light' groups, and two more weighings finish it."
**Answer:** **Possible in 3 weighings (full plan below).**
**Step-by-step working:** Label the balls 1 to 12.
1. **Weighing 1:** 1, 2, 3, 4 against 5, 6, 7, 8.
2. **If balanced:** the odd ball is among 9, 10, 11, 12 and 1 to 8 are good.
 - Weighing 2: 9, 10, 11 against 1, 2, 3 (known good). If balanced, the odd ball is 12; weigh 12 against 1 for heavier or lighter (the third weighing).
 - If 9, 10, 11 is heavier: the odd ball is one of them and heavy; weigh 9 against 10: heavier one is the odd ball; balanced means it is 11. If 9, 10, 11 is lighter, do the same for a light ball.
3. **If not balanced** (say 1 to 4 is heavier): either one of 1 to 4 is heavy, or one of 5 to 8 is light; 9 to 12 are good.
 - Weighing 2: 1, 2, 5 against 3, 6, 9.
 - Left down: 1 heavy, 2 heavy or 6 light; weigh 1 against 2: the heavier is odd; balanced means 6 is light.
 - Right down: 3 heavy or 5 light; weigh 3 against 9: heavier means 3 is heavy; balanced means 5 is light.
 - Balanced: 4 heavy, 7 light or 8 light; weigh 7 against 8: the lighter is odd; balanced means 4 is heavy.
 - If 1 to 4 is lighter, swap "heavy" and "light" throughout.
**Check:** verified by script (after the 4 versus 4 weighing, each of the three outcomes can be resolved in the 2 remaining weighings, using known-good balls where needed).
**Explain simply:**
- There are 12 balls x 2 possibilities (heavier or lighter) = 24 cases; 3 weighings give 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 outcomes, so 24 fits, just barely.
- The trick is that a failed weighing converts unknown balls into "good" reference balls for the next step.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *With 13 balls?* If you only need to find the ball (not whether it is heavier or lighter), 13 is the most 3 weighings can handle; 12 is the most if you must also say heavier or lighter. 2) *What if you knew it was heavier?* 27 balls in 3 weighings.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Starting with 6 against 6 (cannot resolve in 2 more weighings); forgetting to use the known-good balls as reference.
