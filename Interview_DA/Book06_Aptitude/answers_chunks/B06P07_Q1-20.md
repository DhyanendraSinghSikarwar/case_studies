### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Pointing to a man, Meera says, "He is the son of my mother's only brother." How is the man related to Meera?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); blood relations appear in prep-site topic lists for Infosys, Accenture and TCS NQT (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn a spoken relation into a family tree step by step.
**Say this in the interview:** "Meera's mother's brother is her maternal uncle. His son is her cousin, and since the question says 'son', he is a male cousin. Answer: cousin brother."
**Answer:** **The man is Meera's cousin (a male cousin, "cousin brother").**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Start with Meera. Her mother is one step up.
2. The mother's only brother is Meera's maternal uncle (mama).
3. The uncle's son is the child of Meera's uncle, so he is Meera's first cousin.
4. "Son" tells us he is male.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Read the clue backwards from the pointed person: he is son of (mother's brother of me). Mother's brother = uncle, uncle's child = cousin. "Only" brother just removes doubt about which uncle.
**Check:** Reverse it: Meera is the daughter of the man's father's sister, so she is his cousin. Consistent.
**Explain simply:**
- A cousin is a child of your parent's brother or sister.
- Words like "son" or "daughter" fix gender, so use them to name the answer precisely.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if it were "mother's only sister's son"?* Still a cousin (maternal aunt's son). 2) *Can the answer be "nephew"?* No, nephew is a sibling's son.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "uncle" or "brother"; the uncle is the father of the man, not the man himself.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A is the sister of B, C is the mother of B, D is the father of C, and E is the mother of D. How is A related to D?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); chain-style blood relations are a standard item in the Infosys/Accenture topic lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Following a chain of generations without losing a link.
**Say this in the interview:** "A and B are siblings, so C is also A's mother. D is C's father, so D is A's maternal grandfather. A is a girl, so A is D's granddaughter."
**Answer:** **A is D's granddaughter (D's daughter's daughter).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A is B's sister, so A and B share the same parents.
2. C is B's mother, so C is also A's mother.
3. D is C's father, so D is A's mother's father, the maternal grandfather.
4. A is female (sister), so from D's side she is a granddaughter.
5. E (D's mother) is not needed for the question; it is a distractor.
**Check:** Tree: E, then D, then C, then A and B. A is two generations below D. Correct.
**Explain simply:**
- Siblings share parents, so any clue about B's parents also holds for A.
- Generations: parent is one step, grandparent is two.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How is A related to E?* Great-granddaughter. 2) *How is B related to D?* Grandchild; gender of B is not given.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Calling A the "daughter" of D, or reading the extra clue E as relevant.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 P is the brother of Q, Q is the sister of R, R is the father of S, who is a girl. How is S related to P?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic seen in prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting that siblings of a parent are uncles or aunts.
**Say this in the interview:** "P, Q and R are all siblings. R is S's father, so P is S's father's brother, which makes P her uncle. S is P's niece."
**Answer:** **S is P's niece (P is her uncle).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. P is Q's brother and Q is R's sister, so P, Q and R are one set of siblings.
2. R is the father of S, so S is the child of P's sibling.
3. A brother's daughter is a niece, so S is P's niece.
**Check:** Reverse: P is the paternal uncle of S. Both readings agree.
**Explain simply:**
- Your sibling's child is your niece (girl) or nephew (boy).
- Gender of R as "father" is given, so we know it is the paternal side.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How is Q related to S?* Paternal aunt. 2) *If S were a boy?* Nephew.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "cousin" or "daughter"; forgetting that R, P, Q are siblings.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Introducing a woman, a man says, "Her mother is the only daughter of my mother." How is the woman related to the man?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a classic form in prep-site blood-relation sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Decoding "only daughter of my mother" correctly.
**Say this in the interview:** "The only daughter of my mother is my sister. So her mother is my sister, and she is my sister's daughter, my niece."
**Answer:** **The woman is the man's niece.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. "My mother's only daughter" is a daughter of the speaker's own mother, so she is the speaker's sister (he is a man, so he is not that daughter).
2. The woman's mother is therefore the speaker's sister.
3. The sister's daughter is the speaker's niece.
**Check:** From the woman's side: her mother's brother is her maternal uncle (mama), so the man is her uncle. Consistent.
**Explain simply:**
- "My mother's only daughter" is the usual puzzle phrase for "my sister".
- The speaker's gender (a man) matters: it rules out the speaker being the daughter.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the speaker were a woman?* The "only daughter" could be herself, so the woman would be her daughter. 2) *Who is the woman's mother's mother to the man?* His own mother.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering "daughter" (forgetting the speaker is male) or "cousin".

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A is the brother of B, C is the father of A, D is the brother of C, and E is the son of D. How is E related to B?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic in prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising cousins and not over-claiming gender.
**Say this in the interview:** "A and B are siblings, so C is B's father. D is C's brother, so D is B's uncle, and D's son E is B's cousin."
**Answer:** **E is B's cousin (a male cousin; B's gender is not given).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A and B are siblings, so C (father of A) is also B's father.
2. D is C's brother, so D is B's paternal uncle.
3. E is D's son, so E is the son of B's uncle: B's cousin.
4. E is a son, so male. B could be a boy or a girl, so we cannot say "cousin brother of B" safely.
**Check:** Reverse: B is the child of E's father's brother, so B is E's cousin. Matches.
**Explain simply:**
- Children of two brothers are first cousins.
- Say "cousin" when the other person's gender is not known.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How is D related to A?* Paternal uncle. 2) *How is C related to E?* Paternal uncle.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing "brother" or "nephew".

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you solve a blood-relation question quickly: which symbols do you use for gender and marriage, how do you build the family tree, and what do you do when a clue does not give the gender?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the family-tree method is the standard approach in prep-site guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have a repeatable method and are careful about what the clues do not say.
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw a small tree: a square or plus sign for a male, a circle or minus sign for a female, a double line for a married couple, a single line down for parent to child, and a horizontal line for siblings. I place the pointed person last, read backwards, and I never assume gender that is not given."
**Answer:** **Use symbols, build the tree one clue at a time starting from the speaker, and keep unknown gender unknown.**
**Explain simply:**
- Symbols: male = square (+), female = circle (-), couple = "=" between them, children hang below a vertical line, siblings sit side by side.
- Build the tree: start with the most definite clue (the speaker or a named parent), add one clue at a time, and place new people above or below by generation.
- Read "A is the X of B" as: A to B. Cross-check by reading it back from B to A.
- Unknown gender: write "?" and give a gender-neutral answer such as cousin, sibling, spouse, parent or child. Gender is fixed only by words like son, daughter, brother, father, mother, wife.
- Worked mini-example: "A is B's sister; B is C's father." Tree: C is a child of B, A is B's sister, so A is C's paternal aunt (A is female, so aunt).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Your cousin's gender is unknown; what do you call the cousin?* "Cousin". 2) *A is B's brother; B is C's mother; C's gender unknown?* A is C's maternal uncle.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming gender from a name, mixing up paternal and maternal sides, or reading the clue from the wrong end.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Statements: All employees are analysts. All analysts are graduates. Conclusions: I. All employees are graduates. II. All graduates are employees. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); syllogisms are listed for Infosys, Cognizant and Wipro in prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Chaining two "All" statements and not reversing them.
**Say this in the interview:** "Employees sit inside analysts and analysts sit inside graduates, so employees sit inside graduates: I follows. The reverse, II, does not follow."
**Answer:** **Only conclusion I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Draw three circles: Employees inside Analysts inside Graduates.
2. Conclusion I: the Employees circle is fully inside Graduates. It follows.
3. Conclusion II: Graduates is the biggest circle; some graduates may lie outside Employees. It does not follow.
**Check:** Example: 10 employees, 30 analysts, 100 graduates fits both statements, yet not all graduates are employees.
**Explain simply:**
- "All A are B" means circle A is wholly inside circle B.
- Two such links chain: All A are B, All B are C gives All A are C.
- A statement does not work backwards: "All A are B" does not mean "All B are A".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some graduates are analysts" follow?* Yes, if analysts exist (all analysts are graduates). 2) *Does "Some graduates are employees"?* Yes, the same way.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "both follow" by reversing the statement.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Statements: Some managers are engineers. All engineers are analysts. Conclusions: I. Some managers are analysts. II. All analysts are managers. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); topic in prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Combining a "Some" with an "All".
**Say this in the interview:** "Those managers who are engineers are also analysts, so some managers are analysts: I follows. Nothing says every analyst is a manager, so II does not."
**Answer:** **Only conclusion I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Draw Engineers inside Analysts.
2. Managers overlaps Engineers (some managers are engineers). That overlap area is inside Analysts too.
3. So Managers and Analysts share at least that overlap: "Some managers are analysts" is true.
4. Analysts is a larger circle that may have members outside Managers, so "All analysts are managers" is not forced.
**Check:** Example: managers {1,2,3}, engineers {1}, analysts {1,4,5}. Both statements hold; I is true, II is false.
**Explain simply:**
- Some + All (the All points onward) gives Some.
- "Some A are B" and "All B are C" gives "Some A are C".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the first statement was "All managers are engineers"?* Then all managers are analysts. 2) *Does "Some analysts are managers" follow?* Yes (the reverse of "some" is allowed).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating "some" as "all".

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the four standard statement forms in syllogism questions (All, No, Some, Some not), and how is each drawn in a Venn diagram?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the Venn method is the standard in prep guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know the building blocks before attempting any syllogism.
**Say this in the interview:** "All A are B: A circle inside B. No A is B: two separate circles. Some A are B: the circles overlap. Some A are not B: part of A lies outside B."
**Answer:** **Four forms: All (inside), No (separate), Some (overlap), Some not (part outside).**
**Explain simply:**
- All A are B: circle A fully inside circle B. Example: all HR executives are employees.
- No A is B: two circles that do not touch. Example: no intern is a director.
- Some A are B: the circles overlap, at least one common member. "Some" means "at least one, maybe all". Example: some employees are analysts.
- Some A are not B: at least one member of A is outside B. Example: some employees are not managers.
- Valid reverses: "No A is B" gives "No B is A"; "Some A are B" gives "Some B are A". "All A are B" does not give "All B are A" (only "Some B are A", if A is non-empty).
- Mini-example: All pens are books; some books are bags. Pens are inside Books; Bags overlap Books, but whether Bags touch Pens is open.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some A are not B" follow from "No A is B"?* Yes, if A has members. 2) *Opposite of "All A are B"?* "Some A are not B".
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading "some" as "only some"; reversing "All".

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Five colleagues A, B, C, D and E sit in a row facing north. A sits at the extreme left end and E at the extreme right end. D sits immediately to the left of E. B does not sit next to A. Who sits in the middle?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); linear seating is listed in Accenture and Cognizant prep sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Placing fixed clues first, then eliminating.
**Say this in the interview:** "A is seat 1, E is seat 5, D is seat 4. B cannot be in seat 2, so B is seat 3 and C is seat 2. The middle is B."
**Answer:** **B sits in the middle.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Seats 1 to 5 from the left. A = 1, E = 5.
2. D is immediately left of E, so D = 4.
3. Left: seats 2 and 3 for B and C.
4. B is not next to A, so B is not seat 2. B = 3, C = 2.
5. Final row: A, C, B, D, E.
**Check:** Brute force over all 120 orders gives exactly one valid arrangement (A C B D E); the answer is unique.
**Explain simply:**
- Fixed positions first (ends, "immediately next to"), negative clues last.
- Facing north: left of the row is the speaker's left too, so no flipping.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who sits next to A?* C. 2) *If B could sit next to A?* Two arrangements, so the middle would not be unique.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Ignoring the "does not sit next to" clue and picking C.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you approach a seating-arrangement question: how do you handle linear versus circular tables, left and right, and which clues do you place first?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); seating rated medium-hard in one prep-site view of TCS NQT (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A calm, ordered method for the most time-consuming reasoning type.
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw the shape first, place the definite clues (fixed seat, 'opposite', 'immediately next to'), then the relative ones, then use the negative clues to eliminate. I fix left and right using the facing direction."
**Answer:** **Draw the layout, place the sure clues first, then eliminate with the negative clues, and fix the left-right convention up front.**
**Explain simply:**
- Linear row facing north: your left is the left of the page. If they face south, left and right flip, so write the facing direction.
- Circular table facing the centre: your right-hand side is the anticlockwise direction, your left is clockwise. If they face outward, it reverses.
- Place first: exact positions (ends, "third from the left"), then "opposite" pairs (in a table of 2n people, opposite means n seats apart), then "immediately" clues, then "second to the right" clues, and last the "not next to" clues.
- If two cases remain after the sure clues, write both and let later clues kill one.
- Mini-example: 6 people at a round table: if A is at seat 1, D opposite is seat 4. "B second to the right of A" is two steps anticlockwise.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the question gives no fixed seat?* Fix any one person; a circle has no starting point. 2) *When do you skip?* If it needs many cases and time is gone (see Q47).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Mixing up left and right in circles; placing a "not next to" clue too early.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a certain code, MANAGER is written as NBOBHFS. How is ANALYST written in that code?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); coding-decoding appears in Infosys and Wipro lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Finding a constant shift in the alphabet.
**Say this in the interview:** "M goes to N, A to B, N to O: every letter moves one step forward. ANALYST becomes BOBMZTU."
**Answer:** **BOBMZTU**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Compare letters: M to N (+1), A to B (+1), N to O (+1), A to B, G to H, E to F, R to S. The rule is +1 for every letter.
2. Apply to ANALYST: A to B, N to O, A to B, L to M, Y to Z, S to T, T to U.
3. Result: B O B M Z T U.
**Check:** Verified by script: shifting MANAGER by +1 reproduces NBOBHFS, and the same function gives BOBMZTU.
**Explain simply:**
- A "shift" code adds or subtracts the same number from each letter's position.
- Check one or two letters first; if the shift differs, test a position pattern.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *CODE with +2?* EQFG. 2) *What if Z needs +1?* It wraps to A.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Shifting only part of the word, or going backwards.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a certain code, TABLE is written as GZYOV. How is CHAIR written in that code?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reverse-alphabet codes are a common pattern (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting the "mirror" alphabet (A to Z, B to Y).
**Say this in the interview:** "T becomes G, A becomes Z, B becomes Y: each letter is replaced by its opposite from the other end. CHAIR becomes XSZRI."
**Answer:** **XSZRI**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A to Z, B to Y shows the reverse alphabet. Check: T (20th) to G (7th); 20 + 7 = 27. L (12) to O (15): 27. E (5) to V (22): 27. Every pair adds to 27.
2. Opposite of C (3) is 24 = X. H (8) is 19 = S. A (1) is 26 = Z. I (9) is 18 = R. R (18) is 9 = I.
3. Result: X S Z R I.
**Check:** Verified by script: mirroring TABLE gives GZYOV.
**Explain simply:**
- Reverse alphabet: a letter at position p goes to 27 minus p.
- Quick test: if the first two letters of a code are A to Z and B to Y, it is this pattern.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Code for DATA?* WZGZ. 2) *What is the opposite of M?* N (13 and 14).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 26 instead of 27 minus position.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you identify the rule in a coding-decoding question: what are the common patterns (shift, reverse alphabet, position value, substitution)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); coding-decoding appears in Infosys and Wipro lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A systematic way to test hypotheses quickly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I compare the first word letter by letter, write the position numbers (A=1 to Z=26), look at the difference, then test shift, reverse, or a word-level substitution on the next word."
**Answer:** **Test in order: constant shift, reverse alphabet, position value, then word substitution.**
**Explain simply:**
- Shift: every letter moves the same number. MANAGER to NBOBHFS is +1 each.
- Reverse alphabet: letter to the opposite end (pairs add to 27). TABLE to GZYOV.
- Position value: the word becomes numbers (A=1...). SQL = 19 + 17 + 12 = 48.
- Alternating or growing shifts: +1, +2, +3 along the word, or +1, -1, +1 ...
- Substitution (word codes): find the common word across sentences. "good sales team" and "team is good" share two codes; common words show which code is which.
- Mini-example: CAT to DBU. C to D, A to B, T to U: +1 shift. Apply to DOG: EPH.
- Tip: if the answer is a different length from the question, check for a reversal of the word first.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the first letter fits +1 but the second does not?* Try a changing shift. 2) *Code with no letter pattern?* Likely word substitution.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Stopping after one letter fits; forgetting to wrap Z to A.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A man walks 8 km south and then 6 km west. How far is he from his starting point?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); direction sense is listed for Wipro (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Recognising a right angle and using Pythagoras.
**Say this in the interview:** "South and west are at right angles, so the distance is the hypotenuse: root of 64 plus 36 = 10 km."
**Answer:** **10 km (he is south-west of the start).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. South then west makes a right angle, with legs 8 and 6.
2. Distance squared = 8 squared + 6 squared = 64 + 36 = 100.
3. Distance = root of 100 = 10 km.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 6-8-10 is the 3-4-5 triple doubled. Spot it and skip the arithmetic.
**Check:** 6 squared + 8 squared = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10 squared.
**Explain simply:**
- Pythagoras: for a right angle, hypotenuse squared = sum of the squares of the two legs.
- Common triples: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *5 km east then 12 km north?* 13 km. 2) *Direction from start to end?* South-west.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding 8 + 6 = 14 km.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Ravi walks 5 km north, turns right and walks 3 km, then turns right again and walks 5 km. How far is he from the starting point and in which direction?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); direction sense appears in Wipro and similar lists (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Tracking turns, and cancelling equal opposite legs.
**Say this in the interview:** "North 5, right turn means east 3, another right turn means south 5. The north and south legs cancel, so he is 3 km east of the start."
**Answer:** **3 km, due East of the starting point.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Facing north, a right turn faces east. Walk 3 km east.
2. Facing east, a right turn faces south. Walk 5 km south.
3. Net north-south: +5 - 5 = 0. Net east-west: 3 km east.
4. He ends 3 km east of the start.
**Check:** Coordinates: (0,0) to (0,5) to (3,5) to (3,0). The distance from the origin is 3, along the east axis.
**Explain simply:**
- Right turns go in the order N, E, S, W; left turns go the other way.
- Use coordinates: north is +y, east is +x.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the last leg were 9 km?* Distance = root of 9 + 16 = 5 km (3 east, 4 south: south-east). 2) *Left turn first?* He would end 3 km west.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 13 km by adding legs, or "south-east".

### Q17 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In a class of 40 students, Priya's rank from the top is 12. What is her rank from the bottom?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ranking items appear in standard reasoning sets (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** The "plus one" in rank problems.
**Say this in the interview:** "Rank from bottom = total minus rank from top plus 1: 40 - 12 + 1 = 29."
**Answer:** **29th from the bottom.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 11 students are above Priya (ranks 1 to 11).
2. Students below her: 40 - 12 = 28.
3. Her rank from the bottom is 28 + 1 = 29.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Bottom rank = total - top rank + 1.
**Check:** Top rank + bottom rank - 1 = 12 + 29 - 1 = 40. Matches.
**Explain simply:**
- Rank counts the person too, so the two ranks overlap by one (the person herself).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Rank 5 from bottom in 40?* 36th from the top. 2) *Class size from 12 and 29?* 12 + 29 - 1 = 40.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 28 (forgetting the +1).

### Q18 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Arun ranks 7th from the top and 28th from the bottom in a class. How many students are in the class?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ranking items appear in standard reasoning sets (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Subtracting the double-counted person.
**Say this in the interview:** "Total = top rank + bottom rank - 1 = 7 + 28 - 1 = 34."
**Answer:** **34 students.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Students above Arun: 6. Students below Arun: 27.
2. Add Arun himself: 6 + 27 + 1 = 34.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Total = top + bottom - 1.
**Check:** From the top, 34 - 7 + 1 = 28 from the bottom. Matches.
**Explain simply:**
- Adding the two ranks counts Arun twice, so take one off.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *15th from top, 20th from bottom?* 34. 2) *What if the ranks were in a row from left and right?* Same formula.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 35 (7 + 28).

### Q19 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 P is taller than Q, Q is taller than R, S is taller than P but shorter than T. Who is the tallest and who is the shortest?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ordering items are standard in reasoning sets (prep sites, unverified).

**Answer:** **Tallest: T. Shortest: R.**

- Write the clues as "greater than" arrows and link equal names into a single line.

### Q20 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 If A = 1, B = 2, ... Z = 26, what is the sum of the letter values of the word SQL?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); alphabet position items are standard (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Alphabet positions at speed.
**Say this in the interview:** "S is 19, Q is 17, L is 12. 19 + 17 + 12 = 48."
**Answer:** **48**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. S is the 19th letter: 19. (Count back from Z: Z=26, Y=25, X=24, W=23, V=22, U=21, T=20, S=19.)
2. Q is the 17th letter: 17. (P is 16, so Q is 17.)
