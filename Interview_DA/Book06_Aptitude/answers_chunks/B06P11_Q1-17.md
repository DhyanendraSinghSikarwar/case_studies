### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Choose the correct option: "The list of employees eligible for the bonus ___ on the notice board." (a) are displayed (b) is displayed (c) were displayed (d) have been displayed. Which is correct and why?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); sentence-correction and fill-in-the-blank grammar is the focus reported by prep sites for Accenture-style communication tests (format unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Subject-verb agreement when a plural noun sits between the subject and the verb.
**Say this in the interview:** "The subject is 'list', which is singular, so the verb is singular: 'is displayed'. 'Employees' is only part of the describing phrase."
**Answer:** **(b) is displayed**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Find the real subject: "The list" (the head noun).
2. "of employees eligible for the bonus" is a describing phrase; it does not decide the verb.
3. "List" is singular, so the verb must be singular: "is".
4. Options (a), (c), (d) all use plural verbs ("are", "were", "have been"), so they fail.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Cross out everything from "of" up to the verb: "The list ___ displayed". Now "is" is obvious.
**Check:** Corrected sentence: "The list of employees eligible for the bonus is displayed on the notice board."
**Explain simply:**
- Rule: the verb agrees with the head noun, not the noun nearest to it.
- Phrases starting with "of", "with", "along with" do not change the subject's number.
**Likely follow-ups:** "The members of the committee ___ (is/are) meeting." Answer: "are", because "members" is the subject here.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "are" because "employees" sits right before the verb (the test setter expects this).

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Spot the error in the sentence: "Neither of the two candidates have submitted their documents on time." Rewrite it correctly and name the grammar rule.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); error-spotting is a reported grammar task type (format unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Agreement with "neither", a singular pronoun.
**Say this in the interview:** "'Neither' means 'not one of the two', so it takes a singular verb: 'has submitted'."
**Answer:** **"Neither of the two candidates has submitted his or her documents on time."** (Rule: "neither", "either", "each", "one" take a singular verb.)
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Subject = "Neither" (one thing, not the plural "candidates").
2. Singular subject, so "have" becomes "has".
3. The pronoun that refers back to it should also be singular: "his or her". Modern usage also accepts singular "their" ("has submitted their documents"), and many tests accept it; the verb "has" is the key fix.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Replace "Neither of the two candidates" with "Neither" or "Nobody": "Neither has submitted". The verb fixes itself.
**Check:** "Each of the candidates has..." follows the same pattern.
**Explain simply:**
- Words like each, either, neither, everyone, someone are singular even though they point to a group.
- The "of the candidates" part is only extra detail.
**Likely follow-ups:** "Neither the manager nor the clerks ___ present." Answer: "were" (with neither...nor the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "clerks").
**Common mistakes / traps:** Fixing only "their" and leaving "have"; or treating "candidates" as the subject.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Fill in the blanks with the correct prepositions: "The manager is responsible ___ payroll accuracy, and she is very good ___ analysing attendance data, but she is not satisfied ___ the current report."
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); fill-in-the-blank prepositions are a reported grammar task (format unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Fixed adjective + preposition pairs.
**Say this in the interview:** "responsible for, good at, satisfied with."
**Answer:** **for, at, with** : "The manager is responsible for payroll accuracy, and she is very good at analysing attendance data, but she is not satisfied with the current report."
**Step-by-step working:**
1. "Responsible" is followed by "for" (a duty or thing you answer for); "responsible to" is used for the person you report to.

**Explain simply:**
- Many adjectives need one particular preposition; there is no rule, so memorise common pairs.
**Likely follow-ups:** "She is responsible ___ the HR head." Answer: "to" (the person you report to).
**Common mistakes / traps:** "good in" (common in Indian English); "satisfied from".

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Correct the sentence and name the tense rule: "He has joined the company in 2022 and is working here since then."
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); tense correction is a standard sentence-correction item (format unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Present perfect vs simple past, and "since" with a continuing action.
**Say this in the interview:** "A finished event with a fixed past year takes simple past; an action that started in the past and continues takes present perfect continuous with 'since'."
**Answer:** **"He joined the company in 2022 and has been working here since then."**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. "in 2022" is a fixed, finished time, so use simple past: "joined" (not "has joined").
2. "since then" links a past start to now, so use present perfect continuous: "has been working" (not "is working").
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Fixed past time word (yesterday, in 2022, last year) means simple past. "Since" or "for" with a still-continuing action means perfect tense.
**Check:** "Joined in 2022" and "has been working since" are both correct together; no tense clash remains.
**Explain simply:**
- Present perfect (has/have + 3rd form) cannot be used with a specific past date.
- "Since" gives the starting point; "for" gives the length (for three years).
**Likely follow-ups:** "She is working here for five years." Answer: "She has been working here for five years."
**Common mistakes / traps:** Keeping "has joined in 2022"; using plain "is working" with "since".

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Fill in the correct articles (a / an / the / no article): "She is ___ honest analyst who works in ___ MIS team and sends ___ report to ___ HR Head every Monday."
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); articles are a standard grammar topic (format unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Article choice by sound (not spelling) and specific vs general reference.
**Say this in the interview:** "An honest, in the MIS team, the report to the HR Head."
**Answer:** **an, the, the, the** : "She is an honest analyst who works in the MIS team and sends the report to the HR Head every Monday."
**Step-by-step working:**
1. "honest" starts with a vowel sound (the h is silent), so "an".
2. "the MIS team": one particular team that both people know, so "the".
3. "the report": the known weekly report. (Context allows "a report" if it means some report each week; "the" is the safer pick for a known routine report.)
4. "the HR Head": one specific, known post, so "the".

**Check:** Say it aloud: "an honest" (sounds like "on-est").
**Explain simply:**
- "A" before consonant sounds, "an" before vowel sounds (an hour, an MBA, a university).
- "The" points to something specific both speaker and listener know.
**Likely follow-ups:** "___ European candidate" Answer: "A" (starts with a "yoo" sound).
**Common mistakes / traps:** "a honest"; "a HR Head" (HR is said "aitch-ar", vowel sound, so if an article were needed it would be "an HR"). Dropping "the" before a specific post.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 In SQL, what is the difference between WHERE and HAVING? Which clause would you use to list departments having more than 10 employees from the table employees(emp_id, dept, salary), and why?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); core SQL appears in candidate reports of analyst online tests and the SQL-heavy analyst gate (unverified, snippet-level sources).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Order of operations: filter rows first, filter groups after GROUP BY.
**Say this in the interview:** "WHERE filters individual rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after aggregation. A count per department is a group result, so I use GROUP BY dept with HAVING COUNT(*) > 10."
**Answer:** **HAVING** (with GROUP BY): `SELECT dept, COUNT(*) FROM employees GROUP BY dept HAVING COUNT(*) > 10;`
**Step-by-step working:**
1. WHERE runs first and sees single rows; it cannot use COUNT/SUM/AVG.
2. GROUP BY then puts rows into one bucket per department.
3. HAVING runs on each bucket, so it can test COUNT(*) > 10.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Is the condition on a raw column? WHERE. On an aggregate (count, sum, avg)? HAVING.
**Check (run on Python sqlite3, small data: threshold changed to 2 because there are only 8 sample rows):**
```sql
-- employees: Sales x3, HR x2, Ops x3
SELECT dept, COUNT(*) AS n FROM employees GROUP BY dept HAVING COUNT(*) > 2;
-- Output: ('Ops', 3), ('Sales', 3)

-- WHERE and HAVING together: only salary > 25 rows are counted, then groups with 2 or more kept
SELECT dept, COUNT(*) FROM employees WHERE salary > 25 GROUP BY dept HAVING COUNT(*) >= 2 ORDER BY dept;
-- Output: ('HR', 2), ('Sales', 3)
```
**Explain simply:**
- Think of WHERE as a gatekeeper for rows and HAVING as a gatekeeper for finished groups.
- Both can appear in the same query; WHERE cuts the data down first.
**Likely follow-ups:** "Departments with average salary above 50,000?" `GROUP BY dept HAVING AVG(salary) > 50000`.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing `WHERE COUNT(*) > 10` (error); forgetting GROUP BY.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between a PRIMARY KEY, a FOREIGN KEY and a UNIQUE key in a database table? Can a primary key contain NULL, and can a table have more than one UNIQUE key?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); DBMS fundamentals are reported in some online tests (Tiger Analytics via one institutional note; Accenture disputed).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Constraint basics and NULL handling.
**Say this in the interview:** "A primary key uniquely identifies each row and cannot be NULL; only one per table. A foreign key points to another table's key to link the tables. A unique key stops duplicates but allows NULL, and a table can have many."
**Answer:** **Primary key: unique and NOT NULL, only one per table. Foreign key: links to a key in another table, can repeat and can be NULL. Unique key: no duplicates, NULL allowed (standard SQL), many per table. So: primary key cannot be NULL; yes, a table can have several unique keys.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Primary key = the row's ID card (employee ID).
2. Foreign key = a reference to that ID card in another table (employee's dept_id points to the department table); it enforces that you cannot refer to a department that does not exist.
3. Unique key = a rule "no two rows share this value" (for example official email).
**Check (sqlite3):**
```sql
CREATE TABLE dept(dept_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, dname TEXT);
CREATE TABLE emp(emp_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, email TEXT UNIQUE, dept_id INT REFERENCES dept(dept_id));
INSERT INTO emp VALUES(1,'b@x.com',1);   -- duplicate emp_id -> FAIL: UNIQUE constraint failed: emp.emp_id
INSERT INTO emp VALUES(2,'a@x.com',1);   -- duplicate email -> FAIL: UNIQUE constraint failed: emp.email
INSERT INTO emp VALUES(3,'c@x.com',99);  -- no dept 99 -> FAIL: FOREIGN KEY constraint failed
INSERT INTO emp VALUES(4,NULL,1);        -- OK (NULL in unique column)
INSERT INTO emp VALUES(5,NULL,1);        -- OK (a second NULL is also allowed)
```
(Run with foreign keys switched on; first row inserted earlier was (1,'a@x.com',1).)
**Explain simply:**
- Primary key identifies, foreign key connects, unique key prevents duplicates.
- Database note: SQL Server allows only one NULL in a unique column; MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle and SQLite allow many.
**Likely follow-ups:** "Composite key?" A key made of two or more columns together, like (order_id, product_id).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying a primary key may be NULL; saying a foreign key must be unique.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A table employees has 5 rows. The column bonus has the values 1000, NULL, 2000, NULL, 3000. What do SELECT COUNT(*), SELECT COUNT(bonus) and SELECT AVG(bonus) return?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); NULL handling is a staple of SQL online tests.
**What the interviewer is checking:** That aggregates ignore NULL, except COUNT(*).
**Say this in the interview:** "COUNT(*) counts all rows: 5. COUNT(bonus) counts only non-NULL: 3. AVG(bonus) ignores NULLs: 6000 divided by 3 is 2000."
**Answer:** **COUNT(*) = 5; COUNT(bonus) = 3; AVG(bonus) = 2000**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. COUNT(*) counts rows regardless of content: 5.
2. COUNT(bonus) skips the two NULLs: 1000, 2000, 3000 gives 3.
3. AVG sums non-NULL values: 1000 + 2000 + 3000 = 6000, and divides by the count of non-NULL values, 3: 6000 / 3 = 2000.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Pretend NULL rows do not exist for the column. The trap average would be 6000 / 5 = 1200.
**Check (sqlite3, real output):**
```sql
SELECT COUNT(*), COUNT(bonus), AVG(bonus), SUM(bonus) FROM e8;
-- Output: 5, 3, 2000.0, 6000
```
**Explain simply:**
- NULL means "unknown or missing", not zero. Aggregate functions skip it.
- To treat missing bonus as 0, use `AVG(COALESCE(bonus,0))` = 6000 / 5 = 1200.
**Likely follow-ups:** "AVG with NULL treated as 0?" 1200.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 1200 for AVG; answering 5 for COUNT(bonus).

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Two tables are given. Table A(id): 1, 2, 3. Table B(id): 2, 3, 4. How many rows and which ids are returned by (i) A INNER JOIN B ON A.id = B.id, (ii) A LEFT JOIN B ON A.id = B.id (show A.id and B.id), (iii) A FULL OUTER JOIN B ON A.id = B.id?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a joins item appears in a candidate report of Accenture 2026 on-campus DBMS/SQL (LeetCode discuss, snippet-level, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Matching vs non-matching rows in each join type.
**Say this in the interview:** "Inner keeps only matches: 2 rows. Left keeps all of A: 3 rows with NULL for id 1. Full outer keeps all from both: 4 rows."
**Answer:** **(i) 2 rows: (2,2), (3,3). (ii) 3 rows: (1,NULL), (2,2), (3,3). (iii) 4 rows: (1,NULL), (2,2), (3,3), (NULL,4).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Matching ids are 2 and 3.
2. Inner join returns only these.
3. Left join adds the A-only row, id 1, with B.id NULL.
4. Full outer join also adds the B-only row, id 4, with A.id NULL.
**Check (sqlite3 3.53, real output):**
```sql
SELECT A.id, B.id FROM A INNER JOIN B ON A.id=B.id;      -- (2,2),(3,3)
SELECT A.id, B.id FROM A LEFT JOIN B ON A.id=B.id;       -- (1,None),(2,2),(3,3)
SELECT A.id, B.id FROM A FULL OUTER JOIN B ON A.id=B.id; -- (1,None),(2,2),(3,3),(None,4)
```
(Old SQLite before 3.39 and MySQL lack FULL OUTER JOIN; emulate with LEFT JOIN UNION RIGHT JOIN.)
**Explain simply:**
- Think of two overlapping circles: inner = overlap; left = whole left circle; full = both circles.
**Likely follow-ups:** "Right join?" (2,2),(3,3),(NULL,4).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Counting 3 rows for inner; forgetting NULL fill.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 The table employees(emp_id, name, manager_id) has 4 rows with manager_id values 101, NULL, 102, NULL. What does SELECT * FROM employees WHERE manager_id = NULL return, and what is the correct query to find employees with no manager? Why does the first query behave that way?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); NULL comparison is a classic SQL test item.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Three-valued logic: comparing with NULL gives "unknown", not true.
**Say this in the interview:** "`= NULL` returns zero rows because NULL means unknown and nothing equals unknown. The correct test is `IS NULL`."
**Answer:** **The first query returns 0 rows (empty). Correct query: `SELECT * FROM employees WHERE manager_id IS NULL;` which returns the 2 rows with NULL.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. `manager_id = NULL` evaluates to UNKNOWN for every row (even when manager_id is NULL).
2. WHERE keeps only rows that evaluate to TRUE, so none pass.
3. `IS NULL` is the special test that returns TRUE for missing values.
**Check (sqlite3, real output):**
```sql
SELECT * FROM m WHERE manager_id = NULL;      -- []  (no rows)
SELECT * FROM m WHERE manager_id IS NULL;     -- (2,'Ravi',None), (4,'Kiran',None)
SELECT * FROM m WHERE manager_id IS NOT NULL; -- (1,'Asha',101), (3,'Meena',102)
```
**Explain simply:**
- NULL = "value not known". Is an unknown equal to another unknown? Can't say, so SQL says UNKNOWN.
- Same for `<>`, `>`, `<`: use IS NULL / IS NOT NULL.
**Likely follow-ups:** "`WHERE manager_id <> 101` includes NULL rows?" No, they are UNKNOWN and are dropped.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing `= NULL` and assuming it works; expecting an error (there is none, just an empty result).

1. Object "the monthly attrition report" moves to the front as the subject.
2. "prepared" (past) becomes "was prepared".
3. Second verb "sent it" also becomes passive; since the subject is the same, write only "sent" (was is understood), and "it" is dropped.
4. The doer "the analyst" goes after "by". Optionally add the second doer: "...and was sent to the HR head by the analyst" (acceptable).
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Formula: Object + be (matching tense) + past participle + by + doer.
**Check:** Switch back: "The analyst prepared the report and sent it" matches the original meaning.
**Explain simply:**
- Passive focuses on the thing done, not the doer; good when the doer is unknown or unimportant.
**Likely follow-ups:** Passive of "The team will review the data." "The data will be reviewed by the team."
**Common mistakes / traps:** Keeping "it" ("was sent it"); using the wrong tense of "be".
