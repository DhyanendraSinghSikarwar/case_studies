3. Q_new = (4/5) Q_old, so quantity falls by 1/5 = 20%.

**Fast method (30 seconds):** If an increase is 1/n, the offsetting decrease is 1/(n+1). Here n = 4 so 1/5 = 20%. Another example: 20% = 1/5 rise needs 1/6 = 16.67% fall.
**Check:** price 100 becomes 125; with 100 units spent Rs 10,000; new consumption 80 units x Rs 125 = Rs 10,000. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Expenditure is price multiplied by quantity.
- A rise and fall of the same percent do not cancel because they are on different bases.
- The fall is smaller in percent than the rise because it is computed on the bigger new number.

**Likely follow-ups:** (1) Price falls 20%; by how much can consumption rise to keep spend same? 25%. (2) Price rises 50%? Fall 1/3 = 33.33%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** answering 25% (assuming symmetry).

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mock Set B, Q4 (target 90 s): Two fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum of the numbers is 8?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a basic probability question.
**What the interviewer is checking:** counting the favourable outcomes systematically out of 36.
**Say this in the interview:** "Pairs adding to 8 are (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2): five out of 36, so 5/36."
**Answer:** **5/36 (about 13.9%)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Total outcomes = 6 x 6 = 36.
2. Favourable: (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2) = 5 outcomes.
3. Probability = 5 / 36 = 0.1389, about 13.9%.

**Fast method (30 seconds):** The sum 7 is the most likely (6 ways). Sums go down by one way on each side: 8 has 5 ways, 9 has 4, and so on.
**Check:** the number of ways for sums 2 to 12 is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 totalling 36; the 7th term is 6 and the next is 5. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Probability = favourable outcomes / total equally likely outcomes.
- (2,6) and (6,2) are different outcomes on two dice, so both count.
- (4,4) counts once only.

**Likely follow-ups:** (1) Probability of sum 7? 6/36 = 1/6. (2) Sum at least 10? 6/36 = 1/6.
**Common mistakes / traps:** counting (4,4) twice, or (2,6) only once; dividing by 11 (the possible sums are not equally likely).

### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Mock Set B, Q6 (target 60 s): Find the HCF and the LCM of 36 and 48.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); basic number system.
**What the interviewer is checking:** prime factorisation and the HCF x LCM = product identity.
**Say this in the interview:** "36 = 2 squared x 3 squared and 48 = 2 to the 4 x 3. HCF takes the smaller powers: 4 x 3 = 12. LCM takes the larger: 16 x 9 = 144."
**Answer:** **HCF = 12; LCM = 144**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Prime factors: 36 = 2 x 2 x 3 x 3 = 2^2 x 3^2; 48 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 3 = 2^4 x 3.
2. HCF (highest common factor, the biggest number dividing both) uses the lowest power of each prime: 2^2 x 3 = 12.
3. LCM (lowest common multiple, the smallest number both divide) uses the highest power: 2^4 x 3^2 = 16 x 9 = 144.

**Fast method (30 seconds):** Subtract: 48 - 36 = 12, and 12 divides both, so HCF = 12. Then LCM = (36 x 48) / 12 = 1,728 / 12 = 144.
**Check:** 12 x 144 = 1,728 = 36 x 48. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- HCF x LCM = product of the two numbers (true for two numbers only).
- HCF is used for splitting into equal groups (for example, 36 and 48 employees into equal teams: largest team size 12).
- LCM is used for "when will they coincide" (for example, two shift patterns repeating every 36 and 48 days align every 144 days).

**Likely follow-ups:** (1) HCF and LCM of 24 and 60? 12 and 120. (2) Smallest number of equal teams if 36 and 48 staff are split with no mixing? 3 + 4 = 7 teams of 12.
**Common mistakes / traps:** mixing up HCF and LCM; picking the highest powers for HCF; using the product identity for three numbers.
