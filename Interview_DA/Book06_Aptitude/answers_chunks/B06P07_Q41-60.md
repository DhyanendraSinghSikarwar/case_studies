### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A clock strikes once at 1 o'clock, twice at 2 o'clock, and so on up to 12 times at 12 o'clock. How many times does it strike in 12 hours?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can spot a simple series (1 + 2 + ... + 12) and sum it quickly.
**Say this in the interview:** "The strikes are 1, 2, 3 up to 12, so I add the first 12 whole numbers using n(n+1)/2, which gives 78."
**Answer:** **78 strikes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Strikes at each hour: 1, 2, 3, ..., 12.
2. Pair the ends: 1 + 12 = 13, 2 + 11 = 13, and so on. There are 6 such pairs.
3. Total = 6 x 13 = 78.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** n(n+1)/2 = 12 x 13 / 2 = 78. Why it works: writing the list forwards and backwards gives n pairs each summing to n+1, which counts everything twice.
**Check:** 1+2+3+4+5+6 = 21 and 7+8+9+10+11+12 = 57; 21 + 57 = 78. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A "series" is a list of numbers that follows a pattern; here each term is one more than the last.
- The sum of the first n counting numbers is n(n+1)/2.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many strikes in 24 hours?* The day repeats the pattern twice: 2 x 78 = 156. 2) *If it also strikes once at every half hour?* Add 12 half-hour strikes: 78 + 12 = 90.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 12 x 12 = 144 or 12 x 6.5 = 78 by guesswork without seeing the pattern; or counting only 11 hours' worth.

### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟢 If today is Wednesday, what day of the week will it be 100 days from today?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Calendar arithmetic with "odd days" (the leftover days after removing whole weeks).
**Say this in the interview:** "A week has 7 days, so I only care about the remainder of 100 divided by 7. That is 2, so two days after Wednesday: Friday."
**Answer:** **Friday**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 100 divided by 7 = 14 weeks (98 days) with remainder 2.
2. 14 whole weeks bring us back to Wednesday.
3. Move the 2 leftover days forward: Thursday, then Friday.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Take the remainder of the days divided by 7 and count that many days ahead. Why it works: every 7 days the weekday repeats.
**Check:** 98 days later is Wednesday (14 x 7); two more days gives Friday. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The remainder after dividing by 7 is called the "odd days".
- Weekdays in order: Mon, Tue, Wed, Thu, Fri, Sat, Sun, then it repeats.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What day was it 100 days ago?* Go back 2 days: Monday. 2) *What day after 365 days?* 365 mod 7 = 1, so Thursday.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 100 / 7 = 14 and counting 14 days ahead, or counting the starting day itself as day 1.

### Q46 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the most common traps in syllogism questions (for example, treating "some" as "all", or ignoring the "either-or" case)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); syllogisms appear in reasoning sections of many service-company tests per prep-site lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you reason only from the given statements and know the formal rules.
**Say this in the interview:** "I use only what the statements say, treat 'some' as 'at least one, maybe all', and check for the either-or pair before saying neither follows."
**Answer:** **Main traps: (1) "some" read as "all"; (2) "some" read as "some not"; (3) using real-world knowledge; (4) wrongly reversing "All A are B"; (5) missing the either-or case; (6) treating "no" as "some not" or the reverse.**
**Explain simply:**
- "Some" means at least one and possibly all. "Some doctors are teachers" does not say that some doctors are not teachers.
- "All A are B" does not mean "All B are A". All HR managers are employees; not all employees are HR managers.
- Use the "possible diagram" method: draw all the possible Venn diagrams. A conclusion follows only if it holds in every diagram.
- Either-or: if neither conclusion follows alone, but they are contradictory (for example "All X are Y" and "Some X are not Y", or "Some X are Y" and "No X is Y"), then "either I or II follows". Both must involve the same subject and predicate.
**Worked mini-example:** Statements: Some doctors are teachers; All teachers are writers. "All doctors are writers" may be true or false, and "Some doctors are not writers" may be true or false, yet exactly one of them must be true: either I or II follows (see Q55).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "No A is B" imply "No B is A"?* Yes. 2) *Does "Some A are B" imply "Some B are A"?* Yes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "neither" when the pair is complementary; accepting "some A are not B" from "some A are B".

### Q47 ⭐⭐ 🟢 You have 20 questions in 20 minutes in a reasoning section. How do you manage time, and when do you skip a question?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the 20 questions in 20 minutes format is the question's own example, not a reported test pattern (test timings conflict across prep sites).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Test strategy: pacing, prioritising, and not sinking time into one item.
**Say this in the interview:** "That is one minute a question on average. I do the quick ones first, mark long puzzles and come back, and skip anything where I have spent about 90 seconds without progress."
**Answer:** **Plan: pass 1 (about 12 minutes) for all easy and medium items; pass 2 (about 6 minutes) for the marked ones; last 2 minutes to guess and review. Skip when stuck beyond about 90 seconds, or when a puzzle has many clues.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Budget: 20 minutes / 20 questions = 1 minute each. Easy items (series, ranking, simple syllogisms) take 30-40 seconds, which buys time for harder ones.
2. Pass 1: answer what you can see in under a minute; mark the rest.
3. Pass 2: attempt marked items, shortest first.
4. Final 2 minutes: fill every blank (if there is no negative marking, always guess; if there is, guess only after eliminating options).
**Explain simply:**
- Each question usually carries the same marks, so the cheap ones are the best value.
- Skipping is a planned move, not failure.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if there is negative marking?* Guess only when you can remove at least two options. 2) *Which types to attempt first?* Ranking, direction sense, simple syllogisms.

### Q48 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between deductive and inductive reasoning, with one workplace example each?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand certainty versus probability in reasoning.
**Say this in the interview:** "Deduction goes from a general rule to a certain conclusion; induction goes from observed cases to a likely general rule."
**Answer:** **Deductive: general to specific, conclusion is certain if the premises are true. Inductive: specific to general, conclusion is only probable.**
**Explain simply:**
- Deductive example: "All employees must complete the safety training. Asha is an employee. So Asha must complete it." If the first two are true, the third cannot be false.
- Inductive example: "The last five hires from campus X performed well, so campus X likely produces good hires." Useful, but a sixth hire could disappoint.
- Deduction preserves truth; induction extends knowledge but carries risk.
- In data work: hypothesis testing applies a general rule (deductive flavour); spotting a trend in samples is inductive.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which is used in syllogisms?* Deductive. 2) *Which is used when forecasting from past sales?* Inductive.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking "deductive" means "stronger person" or mixing them up; calling a valid-looking argument with false premises "certain".

### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In a survey of 100 employees, 60 use Excel, 45 use SQL and 20 use neither. How many use both?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** The two-set overlap formula (inclusion-exclusion).
**Say this in the interview:** "80 use at least one tool. 60 + 45 = 105 counts the overlap twice, so both = 105 - 80 = 25."
**Answer:** **25 employees**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Use at least one tool = 100 - 20 = 80.
2. Excel + SQL = 60 + 45 = 105.
3. The extra 105 - 80 = 25 is the people counted twice, i.e. those who use both.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Both = (A + B) - (Total - Neither). Why: adding A and B double counts the overlap.
**Check:** Only Excel = 35, only SQL = 20, both = 25, neither = 20: 35 + 20 + 25 + 20 = 100. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Union = A + B - Both.
- "Neither" sits outside both circles in a Venn diagram.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many use only Excel?* 60 - 25 = 35. 2) *If 30 used neither?* 105 - 70 = 35 use both.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Computing 60 + 45 - 100 = 5 (forgetting the 20 who use neither).

### Q50 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A 3 x 3 x 3 cube is painted on all outer faces and then cut into 27 equal small cubes. How many small cubes have exactly two faces painted?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spatial counting by position: corner, edge, face, centre.
**Say this in the interview:** "Two painted faces means the edge cubes that are not corners. A cube has 12 edges, each has 1 middle cube, so 12."
**Answer:** **12 cubes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Corner cubes: 8, each with 3 painted faces.
2. Edge cubes (middle of an edge): 12 edges x 1 = 12, each with 2 painted faces.
3. Face-centre cubes: 6 faces x 1 = 6, each with 1 painted face.
4. Hidden centre cube: 1, no paint.
5. Check the total: 8 + 12 + 6 + 1 = 27.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** For an n x n x n cube: two-faces = 12 x (n - 2). For n = 3: 12 x 1 = 12.
**Check:** Counted by script (coordinates with exactly one middle value): 12; parts add to 27.
**Explain simply:**
- Cubes touching 3 outer faces are corners; 2 faces are edges; 1 face are face centres.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *For a 4 x 4 x 4 cube?* 12 x 2 = 24. 2) *How many with no paint in a 4 x 4 x 4?* (4-2)^3 = 8.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 8 (counting corners) or 24 (two cubes per edge, as in the 4 x 4 x 4 case).

### Q51 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand at 3:25?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** The clock-angle formula and that the hour hand keeps moving.
**Say this in the interview:** "The minute hand is at 25 x 6 = 150 degrees. The hour hand is at 3 x 30 + 25 x 0.5 = 102.5 degrees. The gap is 47.5 degrees."
**Answer:** **47.5 degrees**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Minute hand moves 360 / 60 = 6 degrees per minute: 25 x 6 = 150 degrees from 12.
2. Hour hand moves 30 degrees per hour, 0.5 degrees per minute: 3 x 30 + 25 x 0.5 = 90 + 12.5 = 102.5 degrees.
3. Difference = 150 - 102.5 = 47.5 degrees.

- The clock face is 360 degrees; each hour mark is 30 degrees apart.
- The hour hand creeps forward as minutes pass, so it is not at exactly 90 degrees at 3:25.

### Q52 ⭐⭐ 🟡 If 1 January 2026 is a Thursday, what day of the week is 15 August 2026?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Counting days across months and using odd days.
**Say this in the interview:** "15 August is day 227 of the year, so it is 226 days after 1 January. 226 leaves remainder 2 on dividing by 7, so Thursday plus 2 is Saturday."
**Answer:** **Saturday**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Days before August (2026 is not a leap year): Jan 31 + Feb 28 + Mar 31 + Apr 30 + May 31 + Jun 30 + Jul 31 = 212.
2. 15 August is day 212 + 15 = 227 of the year.
3. Days after 1 Jan = 227 - 1 = 226.
4. 226 / 7 = 32 weeks (224 days), remainder 2.
5. Thursday + 2 = Saturday.
**Check:** Verified by script: 15 August 2026 is a Saturday (226 days after 1 January).
**Explain simply:**
- Whole weeks do not change the weekday; only the leftover days (odd days) do.
- 2026 is not a leap year (not divisible by 4), so February has 28 days.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What day is 1 January 2027?* 365 mod 7 = 1, so Friday. 2) *What day is 26 January 2026?* 25 days later, 25 mod 7 = 4, so Monday.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 227 instead of 226 (off by one) which gives Sunday; using 29 days in February.

### Q53 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Of 120 candidates, 70 cleared the aptitude test, 50 cleared the technical test and 30 cleared both. How many cleared neither?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Inclusion-exclusion for two sets.

**Answer:** **30 candidates**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Cleared at least one = 70 + 50 - 30 = 90 (subtract the overlap once because it was counted twice).
2. Cleared neither = 120 - 90 = 30.

### Q54 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statement: P > Q >= R = S < T. Conclusions: I. P > S. II. T > Q. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); inequality-coding items are a standard reasoning topic.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Linking symbols through a chain, and knowing when there is no link.
**Say this in the interview:** "P > Q >= R = S gives P > S, so I follows. T is only known to be above S; its link to Q is not fixed, so II does not follow. Only I."
**Answer:** **Only conclusion I follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. P > Q and Q >= R and R = S, so P > Q >= S, hence P > S. I is true.
2. T > S and Q >= S: both Q and T are above S (or Q equals S), but there is no direct or indirect comparison between T and Q.
3. Example where II fails: S = 1, R = 1, Q = 3, P = 4, T = 2. Here T < Q.
4. Example where II holds: Q = 2, T = 5. So II is sometimes true, which means it does not follow definitely.
**Check:** Tried all value combinations 0-4 by script: I always true; II true in some and false in others.
**Explain simply:**
- A conclusion follows only if it holds in every case.
- Both Q and T are greater than S, but that does not rank them against each other (two "greater than" arrows into the same point).
- Neither I nor II is the exact opposite of the other (II is T > Q; the opposite would be T <= Q), so the either-or rule does not apply here.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "P > R" follow?* Yes: P > Q >= R. 2) *Does "Q > S" follow?* No: Q >= S only, they could be equal.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming T is the largest because it is written last; treating "greater than or equal" as strictly greater.

### Q55 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statements: Some doctors are teachers. All teachers are writers. Conclusions: I. All doctors are writers. II. Some doctors are not writers. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** The either-or case, where two conclusions contradict each other and neither is certain alone.
**Say this in the interview:** "Neither follows on its own, but they are exact opposites, so one of them must be true: either I or II follows."
**Answer:** **Either I or II follows.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Some doctors are teachers, and all teachers are writers, so those doctors are writers. That much is certain.
2. Other doctors (not teachers) are not mentioned: they could be writers or not.
3. I (All doctors are writers) is possible but not certain; II (Some doctors are not writers) is possible but not certain.
4. I and II are contradictory: if I is false then II is true, and if I is true then II is false. One of them must be true.
5. Hence "either I or II".
**Check:** Checked by script over all small models: every model makes exactly one of I and II true, and each is false in some model.
**Explain simply:**
- Either-or rule: apply only when the two conclusions share the same subject and predicate, are individually not sure, and together cover all possibilities (All X are Y versus Some X are not Y).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some writers are doctors" follow?* Yes (the doctors who are teachers are writers). 2) *Does "Some teachers are doctors" follow?* Yes (reverse of "some").
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking "neither" (the most common wrong answer) or picking I because it sounds natural.

### Q56 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statement: The company has decided to cut travel expenses by 20% this year. Assumptions: I. Travel is a meaningful part of the company's costs. II. Employees will stop travelling completely. Which assumption(s) are implicit?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); critical-reasoning items of this type are listed in Accenture prep material (prep-site claim, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Telling a hidden belief the statement depends on from an over-reach.
**Say this in the interview:** "The decision only makes sense if travel is a real cost, so I is implicit. A 20% cut does not mean no travel at all, so II is not."
**Answer:** **Only assumption I is implicit.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Ask: what must be true for this decision to be reasonable?
2. If travel were a tiny cost, cutting it would not be worth announcing. So I is assumed.
3. A 20% cut means 80% of the spend continues, so travel continues. II contradicts the statement.
**Explain simply:**
- An assumption is an unstated belief the statement needs in order to make sense.
- Test: if the assumption were false, would the decision look pointless? If yes, it is implicit.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is "the cut can be achieved without hurting business" implicit?* Reasonably yes, since the company expects it to work. 2) *Would "video calls are a possible substitute" be implicit?* Not necessarily; it is one way, not required.

### Q57 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Argument: "Call-centre attrition fell from 30% to 25% after the pay hike, so the pay hike caused the fall." Which fact would weaken this argument most? (a) Attrition also fell from 28% to 24% in a branch that got no pay hike. (b) The pay hike was 10%. (c) The call centre has 400 employees. (d) Attrition is measured yearly.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found)
**What the interviewer is checking:** Cause versus coincidence: spotting that a comparison group explains the change.
**Say this in the interview:** "Option (a) shows attrition fell even where there was no pay hike, so something else probably explains it. That weakens the claim the most."
**Answer:** **(a)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. The argument says: pay hike came first, attrition fell, so the hike caused it.
2. A weakening fact shows the fall would have happened anyway or had another cause.
3. (a): a branch with no pay hike also saw a fall (28% to 24%), so the fall is not unique to the pay hike. It points to a common factor (for example a weak job market).
4. (b), (c), (d) are facts about size, amount and measurement; they say nothing about cause.
**Explain simply:**
- This is the "correlation is not causation" idea: two things happening together does not prove one caused the other.
- A control group (no pay hike) is the cleanest test. This is the same logic behind A/B tests in analytics.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would strengthen it?* A no-hike branch where attrition stayed at 30%. 2) *What if the no-hike branch fell from 28% to 20%?* Then it fell even more without a hike, which weakens it still further.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking (b) because the numbers look relevant; ignoring the no-hike branch.

### Q58 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Facing north, Riya turns 90 degrees clockwise, then 180 degrees anticlockwise, then 45 degrees clockwise. Which direction is she facing now?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); direction sense is listed among reasoning topics for Wipro in prep-site material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Tracking net rotation, with clockwise as positive and anticlockwise as negative.
**Say this in the interview:** "Net turn is +90 - 180 + 45 = -45 degrees, so 45 degrees anticlockwise from north: north-west."
**Answer:** **North-west**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Start: North (0 degrees).
2. 90 clockwise: East.
3. 180 anticlockwise: from East, half-turn to West.
4. 45 clockwise: from West, turn towards North by 45 degrees: North-west.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Add clockwise, subtract anticlockwise: 90 - 180 + 45 = -45. Negative means 45 degrees anticlockwise from north, i.e. North-west.
**Check:** Worked by script using 90-degree steps (N, E, S, W) and the net angle -45 degrees, which is North-west.
**Explain simply:**
- Clockwise is the direction a clock hand moves (north to east to south to west).
- Directions in between: NE, SE, SW, NW sit 45 degrees from the main four.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Facing south, turn 135 degrees anticlockwise?* Anticlockwise from south goes to east first (90), then 45 more to north-east: North-east. 2) *If the last turn were 45 anticlockwise?* -135 net, so South-west.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating anticlockwise as clockwise (90 + 180 + 45 = 315 degrees happens to land on North-west here, but the method is wrong and fails on other numbers); or ending at South-west by turning the wrong way on the last step.

### Q60 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Asha, Bala, Chitra and Dev each use a different one of Excel, SQL, Python and Power BI. Asha does not use Excel or SQL. Bala does not use Python or Power BI. Chitra does not use Excel or Power BI. Dev does not use SQL, Python or Excel. Who uses Python?
