### Q23 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A and B can finish a job in 6 and 12 days respectively. They work together and are paid Rs 9,000 in total, shared in proportion to the work each does. What is A's share?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); wage-split items are a gap-fill pattern in the Part 3 sources file.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know that pay follows work done, and that work done follows daily rate (efficiency).
**Say this in the interview:** "They work for the same number of days, so work done is in the ratio of their daily rates. A does 1/6 a day, B does 1/12, which is 2 : 1, so A gets 2/3 of Rs 9,000."
**Answer:** **A's share = Rs 6,000** (B gets Rs 3,000).
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A's daily work = 1/6; B's daily work = 1/12 = 0.5/6.
2. Ratio of A : B = 1/6 : 1/12 = 2 : 1.
3. Total parts = 2 + 1 = 3.
4. A's share = 2/3 x 9,000 = Rs 6,000.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Pay is shared in the inverse ratio of the days taken (6 : 12 flips to 12 : 6 = 2 : 1). The person who finishes faster does more of the work in any shared time, so earns more.
**Check:** B = 1/3 x 9,000 = 3,000; 6,000 + 3,000 = 9,000. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Efficiency (work per day) = 1 / days taken.
- Both work the same number of days together, so the work each does is in the ratio of efficiencies.
- Money is split in that same ratio, like splitting a team bonus by contribution.
**Likely follow-ups:** If B had 18 days instead of 12, A : B = 18 : 6 = 3 : 1, so A gets Rs 6,750.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Splitting in the ratio of days (6 : 12 = 1 : 2) gives A Rs 3,000, which is the trap option. Faster worker must get more, not less.

### Q24 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why is the average speed over two equal distances not the arithmetic mean of the two speeds? Which mean is correct, and when is the simple average correct?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a return-trip average-speed item style appears in a GfG Wipro mock item (practice site, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand that average speed is total distance divided by total time, not the mean of the speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "Average speed is total distance over total time. Over equal distances you spend more time at the slower speed, so the slower speed counts more, and the correct mean is the harmonic mean, 2ab/(a+b). The simple average is right only when the times are equal."
**Answer:** **The harmonic mean, 2ab/(a+b), is correct for equal distances; the simple average (a+b)/2 is correct only for equal times.**
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed = total distance / total time. Always start from this.
- With equal distances, the slow leg takes longer, so you are "stuck" at the slow speed for more of the trip. That drags the average below the simple mean.
- Harmonic mean (a way of averaging that weights slow speeds more) for two speeds: 2ab / (a + b).
- With equal times (say 1 hour at a, then 1 hour at b), distances are a and b over 2 hours, so the average is (a + b)/2, the simple mean.
**Worked mini-example:** Go 60 km at 30 km/h and back 60 km at 60 km/h. Times: 2 h and 1 h, total 3 h for 120 km, so 40 km/h. The simple mean (45) is wrong. Formula: 2 x 30 x 60 / 90 = 40. Same answer.
**Likely follow-ups:** Drive 1 hour at 30 and 1 hour at 60: average = 45 km/h (equal times). For three equal distances: 3 / (1/a + 1/b + 1/c).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking the arithmetic mean (45) because it "looks right". It is always larger than the true average when the speeds differ.

### Q25 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you solve a time-and-work problem quickly using the LCM of the days as the total units of work? Show the method on a job that A does in 12 days and B in 18 days.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the LCM/units method is standard prep content on time-and-work practice sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can avoid messy fractions by choosing a convenient size for the whole job.
**Say this in the interview:** "I take the LCM of the days as the total units of work. Then each person's daily output is a whole number, I add them, and divide the total by that. For 12 and 18, LCM is 36 units, A does 3 a day, B does 2, together 5, so 36/5 = 7.2 days."
**Answer:** **A and B together take 7.2 days (7 1/5 days).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM (smallest number both 12 and 18 divide into) of 12 and 18 = 36. Call the whole job 36 units.
2. A's daily output = 36 / 12 = 3 units.
3. B's daily output = 36 / 18 = 2 units.
4. Together = 3 + 2 = 5 units per day.
5. Days = 36 / 5 = 7.2.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Same as above; it replaces 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 with whole numbers. Any total works (36, 72, 360) because the answer is a ratio; the LCM just keeps numbers small.
**Check:** Fraction method: 1/12 + 1/18 = 3/36 + 2/36 = 5/36 per day, so 36/5 = 7.2 days. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The "units" are invented. We only need the total work to be a number both days divide into cleanly.
- Units per day = efficiency; the faster worker has the larger number.
- Works for part-time stints too: days worked x units per day = units done.
**Likely follow-ups:** A works 4 days alone first: 4 x 3 = 12 units done, 24 left, together 24/5 = 4.8 more days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 12 x 18 = 216 as the total also works but is slower; picking a total that one of the days does not divide into gives fractions and errors.

### Q26 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A can do a job in 24 days and B in 36 days. They work on alternate days, starting with A. In how many days is the job completed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); alternate-day work is listed as a variant in the sources file (2-star pattern, judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you handle a repeating cycle and the leftover work at the end, instead of dividing by an average.
**Say this in the interview:** "Take 72 units of work: A does 3 a day, B does 2. A two-day cycle gives 5 units. After 14 cycles (28 days) 70 units are done, and A finishes the last 2 units on day 29."
**Answer:** **29 days** (precisely 28 2/3 days, since A needs only 2/3 of the last day).
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM of 24 and 36 = 72 units.
2. A does 72 / 24 = 3 units a day; B does 72 / 36 = 2 units a day.
3. One cycle (A day + B day) = 5 units in 2 days.
4. 14 cycles = 28 days, 14 x 5 = 70 units done.
5. 2 units remain. Day 29 is A's turn and A does 3 units, so A needs 2/3 of a day.
6. Total = 28 + 2/3 days, counted as 29 days.
**Check:** After day 28: 14 x 3 + 14 x 2 = 70; 70 + 2 = 72. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Alternate-day problems repeat in a pattern; find the work done in one full cycle first.
- Divide total by cycle work to see how many full cycles fit (here 72 / 5 = 14.4, so 14 full cycles).
- Always check who works on the leftover day. Here it is A, who is faster.
**Likely follow-ups:** If B started first: after 28 days 70 units, B (2 per day) does the last 2 units on day 29, a full day, so 29 days exactly.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 72 / 5 = 14.4 cycles = 28.8 days and answering 29 by luck, or answering 28.8; or using the average of 24 and 36. The leftover day depends on who starts.

### Q27 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A 200 m long train passes a man running in the opposite direction at 6 km/h in 12 seconds. What is the speed of the train in km/h?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); trains with relative motion are reported as TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro topics on prep sites (counts unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative speed in opposite directions and the km/h to m/s conversion.
**Say this in the interview:** "The train covers its own length, 200 m, in 12 seconds relative to the man. That relative speed is 200/12 m/s, which is 60 km/h. Since he runs towards the train, the speeds add, so the train is 60 minus 6 = 54 km/h."
**Answer:** **54 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Distance covered = length of train = 200 m (the man is a point).
2. Relative speed = 200 / 12 = 16.67 m/s.
3. Convert to km/h: multiply by 18/5 (= 3.6): 16.67 x 3.6 = 60 km/h.
4. Opposite directions: train + man = 60, so train = 60 - 6 = 54 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** m/s to km/h is x 3.6. 200/12 x 3.6 = 60. Subtract the man's 6.
**Check:** Train 54 km/h = 15 m/s; man 6 km/h = 1.67 m/s; together 16.67 m/s; 200 / 16.67 = 12 s. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Opposite directions: speeds add. Same direction: speeds subtract.
- Passing a person or pole means covering only the train's own length.
- 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h because 3,600 seconds in an hour and 1,000 m in a km.
**Likely follow-ups:** If the man ran in the same direction at 6 km/h, relative speed 60 means train = 66 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding 6 instead of subtracting (66 km/h); forgetting to convert m/s to km/h (16.67); wrong direction logic.

### Q28 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A boat goes 30 km upstream and 44 km downstream in 10 hours. It goes 40 km upstream and 55 km downstream in 13 hours. Find the speed of the boat in still water and the speed of the stream.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); boats and streams is listed among TSD topics on prep sites.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Setting up two equations in the upstream and downstream speeds and solving cleanly.
**Say this in the interview:** "Let upstream speed be u and downstream speed be d. Two equations: 30/u + 44/d = 10 and 40/u + 55/d = 13. Solving gives u = 5 and d = 11, so still water is 8 km/h and stream is 3 km/h."
**Answer:** **Boat in still water = 8 km/h; stream = 3 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Upstream speed u = boat - stream; downstream speed d = boat + stream.
2. Equation 1: 30/u + 44/d = 10. Equation 2: 40/u + 55/d = 13.
3. Make the 1/u parts equal: multiply Eq 1 by 4 and Eq 2 by 3: 120/u + 176/d = 40 and 120/u + 165/d = 39.
4. Subtract: 11/d = 1, so d = 11 km/h.
5. Put back in Eq 1: 30/u + 44/11 = 10, so 30/u = 6, u = 5 km/h.
6. Still water = (11 + 5)/2 = 8 km/h; stream = (11 - 5)/2 = 3 km/h.
**Check:** 30/5 + 44/11 = 6 + 4 = 10; 40/5 + 55/11 = 8 + 5 = 13. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Stream helps you downstream (add) and slows you upstream (subtract).
- Still water speed = average of the two; stream = half the difference.
- Solving for time-like quantities (1/u, 1/d) is like solving any two simple equations.
**Likely follow-ups:** With stream 3 and boat 8, time for 55 km downstream = 55 / 11 = 5 hours.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 8 + 3 for upstream (swapping the signs); arithmetic slips when eliminating; forgetting that u and d are the unknowns, not the boat and stream.

### Q29 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A and B together can do a job in 10 days, B and C in 15 days, and A and C in 12 days. How many days do all three take working together?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); pair-wise time-and-work is a standard prep pattern (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Adding pair rates and halving, because each person is counted twice.
**Say this in the interview:** "Add the three pair rates: 1/10 + 1/15 + 1/12 = 1/4. That counts everyone twice, so A + B + C together is 1/8 per day, which means 8 days."
**Answer:** **8 days.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM of 10, 15, 12 = 60 units of work.
2. A + B = 60 / 10 = 6 units a day; B + C = 60 / 15 = 4; A + C = 60 / 12 = 5.
3. Add: 2(A + B + C) = 6 + 4 + 5 = 15, so A + B + C = 7.5 units a day.
4. Days = 60 / 7.5 = 8.
**Check:** A = 7.5 - 4 = 3.5, B = 7.5 - 5 = 2.5, C = 7.5 - 6 = 1.5; A + B = 6, B + C = 4, A + C = 5, all match. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Each person appears in two of the three pairs, so adding pairs doubles the total; halve it.
- Once you have the three-person rate, subtract a pair to get the missing person alone.
**Likely follow-ups:** How long does C alone take? 60 / 1.5 = 40 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to halve (giving 60/15 = 4 days); adding days 10 + 15 + 12.

### Q30 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Convert 25 m/s into km/h.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); unit conversion is the entry step of TSD items.
**What the interviewer is checking:** The 18/5 conversion factor.
**Say this in the interview:** "Multiply m/s by 18/5, which is 3.6. 25 times 3.6 is 90 km/h."
**Answer:** **90 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 25 m/s = 25 m in 1 second.
2. In one hour (3,600 s): 25 x 3,600 = 90,000 m.
3. 90,000 m = 90 km, so 90 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** x 3.6 (or x 18/5). To go back, km/h to m/s, x 5/18. Why: 3,600 seconds per hour divided by 1,000 metres per km.
**Check:** 90 km/h x 5/18 = 25 m/s. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Bigger unit (km/h) gives a bigger number from m/s, so multiply.
- 18/5 is just 3,600/1,000 in lowest terms.
**Likely follow-ups:** 72 km/h in m/s = 72 x 5/18 = 20 m/s.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing instead of multiplying, or using 5/18 in the wrong direction.

### Q31 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A tap fills a tank in 6 hours. Because of a leak at the bottom it takes 7 hours. How long would the leak alone take to empty the full tank?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a leak is the "negative work" variant of pipes and cisterns (Careers360, snippet).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Treating the leak as a negative rate.
**Say this in the interview:** "Tap fills 1/6 per hour, net rate with the leak is 1/7. The leak removes the difference: 1/6 minus 1/7 is 1/42 per hour, so 42 hours."
**Answer:** **42 hours.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Tap alone: 1/6 of the tank per hour.
2. With leak: 1/7 per hour.
3. Leak rate = 1/6 - 1/7 = 7/42 - 6/42 = 1/42 per hour.
4. Time to empty a full tank = 42 hours.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Use the formula (6 x 7) / (7 - 6) = 42.
**Check:** In 42 hours the tap adds 7 tanks and the net is 6 tanks, so the leak removed 1 tank. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Fill rate minus drain rate equals the net rate.
- Rate = 1 / time, so time = 1 / rate.
**Likely follow-ups:** Tap 4 h, with leak 5 h: leak alone = 20 h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 1 hour (7 - 6); the answer is much longer than the delay because the leak is slow.

### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the formulas for relative speed when two objects move in the same direction and in opposite directions? Give one business-day example of each.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); relative motion is a reported TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro theme on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know when to subtract and when to add speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "Same direction: relative speed is the difference of the speeds. Opposite directions: it is the sum. Relative speed is how fast the gap between them changes."
**Answer:** **Same direction: S1 - S2 (larger minus smaller). Opposite directions: S1 + S2.**
**Explain simply:**
- Relative speed means the speed at which the distance between two moving things shrinks or grows.
- Same direction: the faster one gains only the extra speed, so subtract.
- Opposite direction: they close in from both sides, so add.
**Worked mini-example:**
- Same direction: a delivery bike at 30 km/h is 10 km behind a courier van doing 20 km/h on the same road. Closing speed is 30 - 20 = 10 km/h, so it catches up in 1 hour.
- Opposite direction: an HR manager leaves the office at 40 km/h and a candidate leaves the venue 120 km away at 20 km/h, heading towards each other. Closing speed 60 km/h, so they meet in 120 / 60 = 2 hours.
**Likely follow-ups:** For trains, add the lengths when crossing each other; for a pole or a person, use only the train's length.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting for opposite directions or adding for the same direction; using the wrong distance (gap vs. lengths).

### Q35 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A boat goes 20 km downstream in 2 hours and returns the same 20 km upstream in 4 hours. Find the speed of the boat in still water and of the stream.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); boats and streams recur in TSD prep lists.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Downstream and upstream speed, then averaging and halving the difference.
**Say this in the interview:** "Downstream speed is 20/2 = 10 km/h, upstream is 20/4 = 5 km/h. Still water is the average, 7.5 km/h, and stream is half the difference, 2.5 km/h."
**Answer:** **Boat = 7.5 km/h; stream = 2.5 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Downstream speed = 20 / 2 = 10 km/h (boat + stream).
2. Upstream speed = 20 / 4 = 5 km/h (boat - stream).
3. Boat = (10 + 5) / 2 = 7.5 km/h.
4. Stream = (10 - 5) / 2 = 2.5 km/h.

**Likely follow-ups:** Stream 2.5, boat 7.5: 30 km upstream takes 30 / 5 = 6 hours.

### Q36 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A tank has capacity 1,200 litres. An inlet pipe adds 25 litres per minute and an outlet removes 15 litres per minute. If both are open on an empty tank, how many minutes does it take to fill?

### Q37 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In Excel you have a delivery log with distance and time for each trip. How would you compute the correct average speed for all trips, and why is AVERAGE of the speed column wrong?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a gap-fill question for the analyst audience, not from a source.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you apply "total distance / total time" in a spreadsheet and know why a plain average misleads.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would use =SUM(distance)/SUM(time), which is total distance over total time. AVERAGE of the speed column treats a 5-minute trip and a 5-hour trip as equal, so it is wrong."
**Answer:** **=SUM(Distance_range) / SUM(Time_range)** (with time in hours).
**Explain simply:**
- Each trip's speed is distance / time. A plain average gives every trip the same weight, whatever its length or duration.
- The correct overall speed is a weighted average: the weights are the times.
- If distances are equal, it matches the harmonic mean; if times are equal, it matches the simple average.
**Worked mini-example:** Trip 1: 60 km in 2 h (30 km/h). Trip 2: 60 km in 1 h (60 km/h). AVERAGE of speeds = 45. Correct: 120 / 3 = 40 km/h.
Excel: if distance is in B2:B3 and time (hours) in C2:C3, use `=SUM(B2:B3)/SUM(C2:C3)` for 40. Alternative: `=SUMPRODUCT(speed, time)/SUM(time)` gives the same.
**Likely follow-ups:** If time is in minutes? Divide by 60 first, or multiply the result by 60 for km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using AVERAGE on the speed column; mixing minutes with hours.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A can do a job in 20 days and B in 30 days. A works alone for 5 days and then B joins. How many days from the start does the job take to finish?

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A team of 10 analysts clears 500 tickets in 8 days working 8 hours a day. How many days would 8 analysts need to clear 500 tickets working 10 hours a day?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); an HR/analyst-flavoured gap-fill in the sources file.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Man-hours as the constant measure, with workers and hours both inverse to days.

**Answer:** **8 days.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Total effort = 10 analysts x 8 days x 8 hours = 640 analyst-hours.
2. Tickets are the same (500), so the effort is the same.
3. New daily effort = 8 x 10 = 80 analyst-hours.
4. Days = 640 / 80 = 8.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Fewer analysts (10 to 8) but longer hours (8 to 10): 10 x 8 = 80 = 8 x 10, so the time stays at 8 days.
**Check:** 8 x 10 x 8 = 640. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Same tickets means same total work, so workers x hours x days stays constant.
- If the tickets changed, scale the effort in proportion.
**Likely follow-ups:** For 750 tickets, effort = 960; days = 960 / 80 = 12.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Overlooking the hours change and answering 10 days (10 x 8 / 8).

### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A walks at 4 km/h. Two hours later B starts from the same point on the same route at 6 km/h. How many hours after B starts does B catch up with A?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); chase problems are a 3-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Head-start gap divided by relative speed.
**Say this in the interview:** "A has an 8 km head start. B gains 6 minus 4 = 2 km each hour, so 8 / 2 = 4 hours after B starts."
**Answer:** **4 hours after B starts.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Head start = 4 km/h x 2 h = 8 km.
2. Same direction: closing speed = 6 - 4 = 2 km/h.
3. Time = 8 / 2 = 4 hours.
**Check:** B walks 6 x 4 = 24 km; A walks 4 x 6 = 24 km (2 + 4 hours). Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The faster person gains only the difference in speed each hour.
- Time to catch up = gap / relative speed.
**Likely follow-ups:** At what distance do they meet? 24 km from the start.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using A's total 6 hours as the answer; the question asks for time after B starts.

### Q43 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A courier covers three equal distances at 20 km/h, 30 km/h and 60 km/h respectively. What is the average speed for the whole journey?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); average-speed items appear in Wipro-style practice sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** The harmonic mean for equal distances, extended to three legs.
**Say this in the interview:** "Take each leg as 60 km: times are 3, 2 and 1 hours. Total 180 km in 6 hours is 30 km/h. The simple average, 36.7, would be wrong."
**Answer:** **30 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let each leg be 60 km (any convenient common multiple).
2. Times: 60/20 = 3 h; 60/30 = 2 h; 60/60 = 1 h. Total = 6 h.
3. Total distance = 3 x 60 = 180 km.
4. Average speed = 180 / 6 = 30 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 3 / (1/20 + 1/30 + 1/60) = 3 / (6/60) = 30.
**Check:** Simple mean (110/3 = 36.7) is higher, as expected, since slower legs take more time. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed is total distance / total time; equal distances mean time is weighted towards the slow legs.
- For n equal legs: harmonic mean = n / (sum of 1/speeds).
**Likely follow-ups:** Equal times at 20, 30, 60 would give a simple average of 36.7 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using (20 + 30 + 60) / 3 = 36.7.

### Q44 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A 200 m long train takes 25 seconds to cross a 300 m long bridge. What is the speed of the train in km/h?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); train and platform items are reported TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro topics, and the platform-length trap is noted on Careers360 and Naukri Code360 (unverified counts).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That crossing a bridge means covering train length plus bridge length.
**Say this in the interview:** "To cross the bridge the train covers its own length plus the bridge: 200 + 300 = 500 m in 25 seconds, which is 20 m/s, or 72 km/h."
**Answer:** **72 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Distance = 200 + 300 = 500 m (front of the train enters the bridge until the rear leaves it).
2. Speed = 500 / 25 = 20 m/s.
3. Convert: 20 x 3.6 = 72 km/h.
**Check:** 72 km/h = 20 m/s; 500 / 20 = 25 s. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Crossing a pole or a person: only the train's length. Crossing a bridge or platform: train + bridge.
- m/s to km/h: multiply by 3.6.
**Likely follow-ups:** At 54 km/h (15 m/s) the same crossing takes 500 / 15 = 33.3 seconds.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 300 m alone (43.2 km/h) or forgetting the unit conversion (20).
