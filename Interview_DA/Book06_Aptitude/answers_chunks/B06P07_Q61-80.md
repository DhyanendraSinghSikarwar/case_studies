### Q61 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Statements: All laptops are devices. No device is a fruit. Conclusions: I. No laptop is a fruit. II. Some fruits are not laptops. Assuming every named group has at least one member, which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can chain "all" and "no" statements and spot the extra assumption in conclusion II.
**Say this in the interview:** "Laptops sit inside devices, and devices sit completely outside fruits, so no laptop is a fruit. Since fruits exist and none of them is a device, some fruits are certainly not laptops. Both follow."
**Answer:** **Both I and II follow.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Draw a small circle (laptops) inside a bigger circle (devices). Fruits is a third circle that does not touch the devices circle.
2. Conclusion I: laptops are inside devices, devices do not touch fruits, so laptops do not touch fruits. "No laptop is a fruit" follows.
3. Conclusion II: the question tells us fruits has at least one member. That fruit is outside devices, so it is outside laptops too. "Some fruits are not laptops" follows.
**Check:** verified by script (every small "world" that fits the two statements and has all three groups non-empty satisfies both conclusions).
**Explain simply:**
- "All X are Y" means the X circle is fully inside the Y circle. "No Y is Z" means the circles do not overlap.
- A "No" statement always flips freely: "No laptop is a fruit" and "No fruit is a laptop" say the same thing.
- "Some ... are not ..." needs at least one member to exist. The question supplies that assumption; without it conclusion II would not be safe.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if fruits could be empty?* Then II fails (there might be no fruits at all) and only I follows. 2) *Would "Some devices are not laptops" follow?* No; devices could be exactly the laptops.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Rejecting II because "nothing was said about fruits and laptops together"; it follows through the device link. Also forgetting that the existence assumption is what makes "some ... not" valid.

### Q62 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You have a 3-litre jug and a 5-litre jug with no markings and unlimited water. How do you measure exactly 4 litres?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Systematic trial of fill, pour and empty moves.
**Say this in the interview:** "Fill the 5, pour into the 3 leaving 2, empty the 3, move the 2 across, refill the 5 and top up the 3; 4 litres remain in the 5-litre jug."
**Answer:** **Yes, 4 litres, in 6 steps (shown below).**
**Step-by-step working:** (5-litre jug, 3-litre jug)
1. Fill the 5-litre jug: (5, 0).
2. Pour 5 into 3 until full: (2, 3).
3. Empty the 3-litre jug: (2, 0).
4. Pour the 2 litres into the 3-litre jug: (0, 2).
5. Fill the 5-litre jug again: (5, 2).
6. Pour from 5 into 3 until it is full; it needs only 1 litre: (4, 3). The 5-litre jug now holds exactly 4 litres.
**Check:** verified by script (a shortest-path search over all jug states finds 4 litres in 6 moves; the same value is reached the other way round via 3, 0, 3, 3, 1, ... in more moves).
**Explain simply:**
- With jugs of 3 and 5 (no common factor except 1) you can make any whole number up to 5, because each pour moves you by 3 or by 5 modulo each other.
- Think of it as "5 minus 1": you want the 5-litre jug short of full by 1, and 3 minus 2 gives that 1.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How to measure 1 litre?* Stop after step 4's earlier state: pour 5 into 3 twice (leaves 5 + 5 - 9 = 1 over refills). 2) *Can you measure 4 with 6 and 9 litre jugs?* No, 4 is not a multiple of 3.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Wasting steps by emptying the wrong jug; forgetting that the leftover after a pour is the "difference" you are collecting.

### Q63 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You have 8 identical-looking balls and one is heavier. Using a balance scale, what is the minimum number of weighings needed to find the heavy ball, and how?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting into three groups (not two) because a balance has three outcomes.
**Say this in the interview:** "Two weighings. Weigh 3 against 3: if they tip, the heavy ball is in the lower group; if equal, it is among the 2 left out. Then one more weighing of at most 3 balls finds it."
**Answer:** **2 weighings.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Split the 8 balls into groups of 3, 3 and 2.
2. Weighing 1: put 3 on each pan. Lower pan means the heavy ball is among those 3. Balanced means it is among the 2 not weighed.
3. Weighing 2 if you have 3 balls: weigh one against another. Lower pan is heavy; balanced means the third ball is heavy.
4. Weighing 2 if you have 2 balls: weigh one against the other; the lower pan is heavy.
5. One weighing is not enough: it has only 3 outcomes, and 8 balls need 8 distinct answers.
**Check:** verified by script (a search over all weighings shows 2 works for 8 balls and 1 does not).
**Explain simply:**
- Each weighing has 3 outcomes (left down, right down, balanced), so k weighings can separate up to 3 to the power k balls: 1 weighing handles 3, 2 handle 9.
- 8 is at most 9, so 2 weighings suffice; the splitting should be as even as possible in threes.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many balls can 3 weighings handle?* 27. 2) *What if you split 4 vs 4 first?* You need 2 more weighings (4 balls need 2), so 3 in total: not optimal.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Splitting 4 and 4 (answer 3); forgetting the leftover group, which the balanced outcome points to.

### Q64 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Which year after 2026 will have exactly the same calendar as 2026?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Odd days and leap years.
**Say this in the interview:** "A normal year shifts the weekday of 1 January by 1, a leap year by 2. I add shifts until the total is a multiple of 7, and 2026 is not a leap year, so the match is 2037."
**Answer:** **2037**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A year of 365 days is 52 weeks and 1 odd day, so the next year starts 1 weekday later. A leap year (366 days) has 2 odd days.
2. Start: 2026 begins on Thursday (and is not a leap year).
3. Go year by year: 2027 Fri, 2028 Sat (leap), 2029 Mon (2028 gave 2 odd days), 2030 Tue, 2031 Wed, 2032 Thu (leap year), 2033 Sat, 2034 Sun, 2035 Mon, 2036 Tue, 2037 Thu.
4. 2037 starts on Thursday again. Both 2026 and 2037 are non-leap years, so the whole calendar matches.
5. Total shift 2026 to 2037: 11 years + 3 leap years (2028, 2032, 2036) = 14 odd days = 0 after dividing by 7.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Count years + number of leap years in between; stop when the sum is a multiple of 7. It is 11 + 3 = 14.
**Check:** verified by script (first year after 2026 that starts on Thursday and is non-leap is 2037).
**Explain simply:**
- "Odd days" are the days left over after removing whole weeks; only they move the weekday.
- The calendar repeats only if the first weekday matches AND the leap-year status matches.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Next same calendar after 2024 (a leap year)?* 2052. 2) *Same calendar as 2027?* 2038 (shift 11 years + 3 leap years).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking 2032 or 2033 because the first weekday matches once, without checking leap status; forgetting 2028, 2032, 2036 are leap years.

### Q65 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Of 200 candidates, 100 know Excel, 80 know SQL and 60 know Python. 30 know Excel and SQL, 25 know SQL and Python, 20 know Excel and Python, and 10 know all three. How many know at least one of the three?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Three-set inclusion-exclusion, and not being distracted by the 200.
**Say this in the interview:** "Add the three sets, subtract each pair, add back the triple overlap: 240 minus 75 plus 10 is 175."
**Answer:** **175 candidates know at least one of the three.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Add the singles: 100 + 80 + 60 = 240 (anyone who knows two skills was counted twice, and anyone who knows all three, three times).
2. Subtract the pairs: 30 + 25 + 20 = 75, giving 240 - 75 = 165. Now the all-three group has been subtracted three times, which removed it entirely.
3. Add back the triple group once: 165 + 10 = 175.
4. So 175 know at least one; the other 200 - 175 = 25 know none.
**Check:** Count the exact groups: all three 10; only Excel+SQL 20, only SQL+Python 15, only Excel+Python 10; only Excel 100-20-10-10 = 60... check: Excel only = 100 - 30 - 20 + 10 = 60; SQL only = 80 - 30 - 25 + 10 = 35; Python only = 60 - 25 - 20 + 10 = 25. Total 10 + 20 + 15 + 10 + 60 + 35 + 25 = 175. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Formula: |A or B or C| = A + B + C - (AB + BC + AC) + ABC.
- The "30 know Excel and SQL" figures include the 10 who know all three.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many know exactly one?* 60 + 35 + 25 = 120. 2) *How many know exactly two?* 20 + 15 + 10 = 45.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to add back the 10; treating "30 know Excel and SQL" as "exactly" two skills.

### Q66 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How many times do the hour hand and the minute hand of a clock overlap in 12 hours?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative-speed reasoning on a clock.
**Say this in the interview:** "The minute hand laps the hour hand 11 times in 12 hours, but the 11th overlap is exactly at 12 again, so it is 11 overlaps."
**Answer:** **11 times**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. The minute hand makes 12 full turns in 12 hours; the hour hand makes 1.
2. The minute hand gains 11 laps on the hour hand, and each lap-gain is one overlap: 11 overlaps.
3. They overlap every 12/11 hours (65 minutes 27 seconds roughly, i.e. 65 5/11 minutes): at 12:00, about 1:05:27, 2:10:54, ... , 10:54:33.
4. The 11th overlap would be at 12 hours, which is the 12:00 that starts the next cycle, so the count in one full cycle is 11, not 12.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** In 12 hours: 11. In 24 hours: 22.
**Check:** verified by script (overlap times 720k/11 minutes for k = 0 to 10 lie within 12 hours, k = 11 equals 12:00 again).
**Explain simply:**
- The minute hand moves 6 degrees per minute, the hour hand 0.5 degrees per minute, so the minute hand gains 5.5 degrees per minute, and 360 / 5.5 = 65 5/11 minutes per overlap.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many times in a day (24 h)?* 22. 2) *How many times are the hands opposite in 12 hours?* 11.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 12 (counting 12:00 twice); thinking they overlap once per hour.

### Q67 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A is twice as old as B. Ten years ago A was three times as old as B. What are their present ages?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Forming two equations from words.
**Say this in the interview:** "A = 2B and A - 10 = 3(B - 10). Substituting gives B = 20, A = 40."
**Answer:** **A is 40 years and B is 20 years.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let B's age be B, so A's age is 2B.
2. Ten years ago A was 2B - 10 and B was B - 10, and A was three times B: 2B - 10 = 3(B - 10).
3. Expand: 2B - 10 = 3B - 30, so B = 20.
4. Then A = 2 x 20 = 40.
**Check:** Ten years ago they were 30 and 10: 30 is three times 10. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Use one letter for the younger person and write the older in terms of it; then restate the second condition 10 years back.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *In how many years will A be 1.5 times B?* Never in a fixed number of years; A - B = 20 always, 1.5 times needs B = 40, i.e. in 20 years (A = 60). 2) *Change to "five years ago A was 3 times B"?* 2B - 5 = 3B - 15 gives B = 10, A = 20.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting 10 from only one person; solving "ten years from now" by mistake (that would give a different answer).

### Q68 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Three people A, B and C say: A: "B is lying." B: "C is lying." C: "A and B are both lying." Who is telling the truth?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Testing each assumption of who is truthful and spotting contradictions.
**Say this in the interview:** "I test each person as the truth-teller. If A is truthful, B is lying, so C is truthful, which contradicts A (C says A is lying). If B is truthful, C is lying and A is lying, which fits. Only B tells the truth."
**Answer:** **Only B is telling the truth.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Suppose A is truthful: then B is lying. B says "C is lying", which must be false, so C is truthful. But C says A is lying, which contradicts A being truthful. Impossible.
2. Suppose B is truthful: then C is lying (B's claim) and A is lying (A said B is lying, which is false). Check C: C says "A and B are both lying"; B is truthful so this is false, consistent with C lying. Possible.
3. Suppose C is truthful: then A and B both lie. A lies, so B is truthful; this contradicts B lying. Impossible.
4. Only the second case works, and it is the only consistent scenario.
**Check:** verified by script (all 8 truth/lie combinations tested; only B truthful survives).
**Explain simply:**
- "Lying" means the statement is false, so a liar's statement must be checked as false, not "the opposite of everything".
- If a case leads to a contradiction, that case is dead; keep going until one survives.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If C had said "A is lying"?* Then A and C agree: both truthful or both lying, and the answer changes; test each case again. 2) *How many cases for 3 people?* 8.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming "B is lying" means B says only false things about everything; stopping after finding one consistent case without checking others (here there is exactly one).

### Q69 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Six candidates A, B, C, D, E and F are interviewed on six consecutive days, Monday to Saturday, one per day. C is interviewed on Wednesday. A is interviewed two days before C. B is interviewed the day after F and after C. D is not interviewed on Saturday. E is interviewed before D. On which day is B interviewed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Placing fixed clues first, then eliminating.
**Say this in the interview:** "C is Wednesday, A is Monday, B comes the day after F and later than C, so B is Thursday, Friday or Saturday; testing the rest leaves one arrangement, with B on Saturday."
**Answer:** **Saturday**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. C is on Wednesday (day 3). A is two days before C: Monday.
2. B is after C, so B is on Thu, Fri or Sat; F is the day before B.
3. D is not Saturday, and E is before D.
4. Days left for B, D, E, F: Tue, Thu, Fri, Sat. The pair (F, B) must be consecutive days: only (Thu, Fri) or (Fri, Sat), since Tue is not next to a later free day.
5. If F = Thu and B = Fri, the free days are Tue and Sat for D and E; D cannot be Saturday so D = Tue, but E must be before D (Monday or earlier, taken). Fails.
6. If F = Fri and B = Sat, the free days are Tue and Thu; E before D gives E = Tue, D = Thu. Valid.
7. Final order: Mon A, Tue E, Wed C, Thu D, Fri F, Sat B.
**Check:** verified by script (all 720 orders tested; exactly one satisfies every clue). Clue read as "the day right after F, and later than C"; if it were also "the day right after C", it would clash with C and F having to be the same day, so the sensible reading is the first.
**Explain simply:**
- Fix known days first, then use "consecutive" clues as a block (F then B) to cut the possibilities.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who is on Friday?* F. 2) *Which day is E?* Tuesday.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading the B clue as "day after C" (impossible with F); forgetting E must be before D.

### Q70 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Statements: Some A are B. All B are C. No C is D. Some D are E. Conclusions: I. Some A are C. II. No B is D. III. Some E are not C. IV. Some A are D. Which conclusion(s) follow?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); reasoning topic coverage per prep-site syllabi (unverified). (syllogism items listed in TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture and Cognizant syllabi, unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Four-statement syllogism chains and the difference between "must follow" and "might be true".
**Say this in the interview:** "I draw circles. Some A inside B, B inside C, so some A are C (I follows). B inside C and C does not touch D, so no B is D (II follows). Some D are E, and D has no overlap with C, so those E are not C (III follows). A and D are never forced to meet (IV does not follow)."
**Answer:** **I, II and III follow; IV does not.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Some A are B, and all B are C: the A that are B are also C. So **I (some A are C) follows**.
2. All B are C, and no C is D: so nothing in B can be D. **II (no B is D) follows**.
3. Some D are E. Those D are not C (no C is D), so some E are not C. **III follows**.
4. Some A are B means A touches B, and B is inside C, which is away from D. Other A members might touch D, but nothing forces it. **IV does not follow**.
**Check:** verified by script (545 small worlds satisfying all four statements; I, II and III hold in all of them, IV fails in 225).
**Explain simply:**
- "Follows" means true in every possible picture, not just one.
- "Some A are B" plus "all B are C" gives a "some" conclusion (some A are C); "all" plus "no" gives "no".
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does "Some E are D" follow?* Yes (reverse of "some D are E"). 2) *Does "Some C are A" follow?* Yes (reverse of I).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Marking IV true because A and B overlap and "everything is connected"; missing that III follows through the "No C is D" link.

### Q72 ⭐ 🟡 On an island, knights always tell the truth and knaves always lie. A says, "We are both knaves." What are A and B?

### Q73 ⭐ 🟡 Three switches outside a closed room control three bulbs inside. You can enter the room only once. How do you find which switch controls which bulb?

### Q74 ⭐ 🟡 You have two ropes that each burn for exactly 60 minutes but burn unevenly. How do you measure exactly 45 minutes?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Using both ends of a rope to halve the burn time.

1. At time 0, light both ends of rope 1 and one end of rope 2.
2. Rope 1 burns from both ends, so it burns out in 30 minutes however unevenly (the two flames always burn exactly one rope's worth in total).
3. At 30 minutes, rope 2 has 30 minutes of burning left. Light its other end now.
4. Two flames on 30 minutes' worth of rope finish in 15 minutes: that is at 45 minutes.

- Lighting both ends doubles the speed: the same total burning needs half the time, even if the rope burns unevenly.

### Q75 ⭐ 🟡 A snail climbs 3 m up a 10 m well each day and slips back 2 m each night. On which day does it reach the top?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Not applying the average blindly; the last day has no slip-back.
**Say this in the interview:** "Net gain is 1 m per day, but on the final day the snail reaches the top before slipping. After 7 days it is at 7 m; on day 8 it climbs 3 m to 10 m and is out. Answer: day 8, not day 10."
**Answer:** **Day 8**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Each full day and night gives a net 3 - 2 = 1 m.
2. After 7 days (and nights) the snail is at 7 m.
3. On day 8, it climbs 3 m during the day: 7 + 3 = 10 m, the top of the well. It is out before the night.
4. So it reaches the top on day 8.
**Check:** verified by script (day-by-day simulation reaches 10 m on day 8).
**Explain simply:**
- Use the formula: days = (height - day climb) / net gain + 1 = (10 - 3) / 1 + 1 = 8.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Well 20 m, climb 5, slip 3?* (20 - 5) / 2 = 7.5, round up to 8, plus 1 = day 9. 2) *What is its position at the end of day 7's night?* 7 m.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering day 10 by dividing 10 m by the net 1 m; forgetting that the slip does not happen once the snail is out.

### Q76 ⭐ 🟡 Three boxes are labelled Apples, Oranges and Mixed, and every label is wrong. You may pick one fruit from one box. Which box do you pick from, and how does that fix all labels?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Using the "all labels are wrong" fact to cut possibilities.
**Say this in the interview:** "Pick from the box labelled Mixed. It cannot really be mixed, so whatever fruit I pull out tells me its true content, and then the other two labels follow by elimination."
**Answer:** **Pick from the box labelled "Mixed".**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Every label is wrong, so the box labelled "Mixed" is actually all apples or all oranges.
2. Pick one fruit from it. Say it is an apple: this box is "Apples".
3. The box labelled "Oranges" cannot be oranges (label wrong), and "Apples" is now taken, so it must be "Mixed".
4. The box labelled "Apples" is then "Oranges".
5. (If the fruit is an orange, swap the roles: Mixed-labelled is Oranges, Oranges-labelled is Apples, Apples-labelled is Mixed.)
**Check:** verified by script (all arrangements with every label wrong tested; a pick from the Mixed-labelled box identifies the rest uniquely, while a pick from either other box does not).
**Explain simply:**
- Only the Mixed-labelled box tells you everything; a pick from the Apples-labelled box could be oranges or "mixed", which is ambiguous.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you pick from the box labelled Apples and get an orange?* It could be Oranges or Mixed: still ambiguous. 2) *How many wrong-label arrangements are there?* 2.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking from the Apples or Oranges box; forgetting that "Mixed" is the one label you can trust to be wrong in a useful way.

### Q77 ⭐ 🔴 Four people take 1, 2, 5 and 10 minutes to cross a bridge at night with one torch. At most two cross at a time at the slower person's speed. What is the minimum total time for all four to cross?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Not letting the fastest person escort everyone; sending the two slowest together.
**Say this in the interview:** "Send the 1 and 2 across, bring the 1 back, send the 5 and 10 together, bring the 2 back, then send the 1 and 2 across. Total 2 + 1 + 10 + 2 + 2 = 17 minutes."
**Answer:** **17 minutes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 1 and 2 cross together: 2 minutes (now 5 and 10 on the start side, torch on the far side).
2. 1 walks back with the torch: 1 minute.
3. 5 and 10 cross together: 10 minutes (the slowest pair crosses only once).
4. 2 walks back with the torch: 2 minutes.
5. 1 and 2 cross together again: 2 minutes.
6. Total: 2 + 1 + 10 + 2 + 2 = 17 minutes.
**Check:** verified by script (a shortest-path search over all states gives 17, and the "1 escorts everyone" plan takes 19).
**Explain simply:**
- The two slowest walkers waste the least time if they cross together; the 2 is the helper who returns the torch.
- The other plan (the 1 escorts everyone) is 2 + 1 + 5 + 1 + 10 = 19.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Times 1, 3, 6, 8?* 3 + 1 + 8 + 3 + 3 = 18, versus escort plan 3 + 1 + 6 + 1 + 8 = 19, so 18. 2) *Is 17 provable minimal?* Yes by the search.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 19 using the 1 as an escort; forgetting that the crossing time equals the slower person.

### Q78 ⭐ 🔴 Using two identical eggs in a 100-storey building, what is the minimum number of drops needed in the worst case to find the lowest floor from which an egg breaks?
**Asked at:** Mu Sigma (the 2 eggs and 100 floors puzzle is listed in a FACE Prep Mu Sigma guide, a prep-site report cited in research/interview_processes.md; unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Balancing the cost of a step: the first egg's drops against the second egg's walk-up.
**Say this in the interview:** "Drop from floors 14, 27, 39, 50 and so on, each step one floor smaller than the last, so the worst case is always 14. Total 14 drops."
**Answer:** **14 drops in the worst case.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let n be the worst-case number of drops. The first egg is dropped first from floor n. If it breaks, the second egg checks floors 1 to n - 1 one by one: total n drops.
2. If it does not break, the next step is only n - 1 floors higher (floor n + n - 1), because one drop has already been used: this keeps the worst case at n everywhere.
3. The floors covered: n + (n - 1) + ... + 1 = n(n + 1)/2. We need this to be at least 100.
4. n = 13 gives 91 (too few); n = 14 gives 105 (enough). So n = 14.
5. Drop sequence for the first egg: floors 14, 27, 39, 50, 60, 69, 77, 84, 90, 95, 99, 100.
**Check:** verified by script (dynamic programming over eggs and floors gives 14 for 100 floors and 2 eggs).
**Explain simply:**
- With one egg you must walk up one floor at a time. With two you can take big jumps first, then walk only inside the one gap.
- Equal jumps (10, 20, 30...) give a worst case of 19 drops (10 + 9). Shrinking jumps equalise the worst case.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *3 eggs, 100 floors?* 9 drops (verified by the same method, 9 is the least n with enough coverage). 2) *Jumps of 10?* Worst case 19.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 10 (jumps of 10) or 19; forgetting to reduce the jump after each non-break.

### Q79 ⭐ 🔴 You have 25 horses and a track that races 5 at a time, with no stopwatch. What is the minimum number of races needed to find the 3 fastest horses?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Pruning candidates by elimination rather than racing everyone against everyone.
**Say this in the interview:** "Five heat races, one race of the five winners, then one race among the 5 horses that could still be in the top 3. Seven races."
**Answer:** **7 races.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Races 1 to 5: split the 25 horses into 5 groups of 5 and race each group. Label each group's horses 1st to 5th (A1 to A5, B1 to B5, ... E1 to E5).
2. Race 6: race the 5 group winners (A1, B1, C1, D1, E1). Suppose the order is A1 > B1 > C1 > D1 > E1, so A1 is the fastest overall (rank 1).
3. Eliminate: D1 and E1 and their groups cannot be in the top 3 (D1 and E1 already have 3 or 4 faster horses). Also C2, C3, B3 (each has at least 3 faster horses above them) and anyone in the bottom of a group.
4. Candidates left for places 2 and 3: A2, A3, B1, B2, C1 (A1 is already first).
5. Race 7: race those 5. The first two finishers are 2nd and 3rd fastest overall.
**Check:** the count is the standard result for this puzzle (not proved by script here); the elimination in step 3 was checked by reasoning: 5 candidates, one race, 2 places. Each place needs a race of its own group, so fewer than 7 cannot cover all horses.
**Explain simply:**
- Each race gives only an order, not a time, so you can only compare horses inside the same race; you must use the results to chain comparisons.
- The key trick is that a horse is out as soon as 3 horses are known to be faster.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Only the fastest?* 6 races. 2) *Fastest 2?* 7 races.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Racing all combos; including A1 in the last race (already known as the fastest); keeping too many candidates.

### Q80 ⭐ 🔴 You have 12 identical-looking balls, one of which has a different weight (heavier or lighter, unknown). Using a balance scale at most 3 times, how do you find the odd ball and whether it is heavier or lighter?
**Asked at:** classic brain-teaser; research/interview_processes.md notes puzzle rounds at Mu Sigma, Fractal and Flipkart (candidate and prep-site reports, mapping to this exact puzzle unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting into groups of 4 and planning follow-up weighings in advance.
**Say this in the interview:** "Weigh 4 against 4. If balanced, the odd ball is in the last 4; if not, I use the tipped side to split into 'maybe heavy' and 'maybe light' groups, and two more weighings finish it."
**Answer:** **Possible in 3 weighings (full plan below).**
**Step-by-step working:** Label the balls 1 to 12.
1. **Weighing 1:** 1, 2, 3, 4 against 5, 6, 7, 8.
2. **If balanced:** the odd ball is among 9, 10, 11, 12 and 1 to 8 are good.
 - Weighing 2: 9, 10, 11 against 1, 2, 3 (known good). If balanced, the odd ball is 12; weigh 12 against 1 for heavier or lighter (the third weighing).
 - If 9, 10, 11 is heavier: the odd ball is one of them and heavy; weigh 9 against 10: heavier one is the odd ball; balanced means it is 11. If 9, 10, 11 is lighter, do the same for a light ball.
3. **If not balanced** (say 1 to 4 is heavier): either one of 1 to 4 is heavy, or one of 5 to 8 is light; 9 to 12 are good.
 - Weighing 2: 1, 2, 5 against 3, 6, 9.
 - Left down: 1 heavy, 2 heavy or 6 light; weigh 1 against 2: the heavier is odd; balanced means 6 is light.
 - Right down: 3 heavy or 5 light; weigh 3 against 9: heavier means 3 is heavy; balanced means 5 is light.
 - Balanced: 4 heavy, 7 light or 8 light; weigh 7 against 8: the lighter is odd; balanced means 4 is heavy.
 - If 1 to 4 is lighter, swap "heavy" and "light" throughout.
**Check:** verified by script (after the 4 versus 4 weighing, each of the three outcomes can be resolved in the 2 remaining weighings, using known-good balls where needed).
**Explain simply:**
- There are 12 balls x 2 possibilities (heavier or lighter) = 24 cases; 3 weighings give 3 x 3 x 3 = 27 outcomes, so 24 fits, just barely.
- The trick is that a failed weighing converts unknown balls into "good" reference balls for the next step.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you only need to find the ball, not heavier or lighter, with 13 balls?* 13 balls is the maximum for 3 weighings (3 cubed minus 3 over 2). 2) *What if you knew it was heavier?* 27 balls in 3 weighings.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Starting with 6 against 6 (cannot resolve in 2 more weighings); forgetting to use the known-good balls as reference.
