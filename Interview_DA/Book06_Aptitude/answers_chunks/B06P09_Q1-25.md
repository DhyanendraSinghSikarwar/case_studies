### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is a data sufficiency question, and how is it different from a normal aptitude problem?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know that the task is to judge if the data is enough, not to compute the final number.
**Say this in the interview:** "A data sufficiency question gives a question and two statements. I do not have to find the answer; I only decide whether the statements give enough information to find one single answer."
**Answer:** **It tests whether the information is sufficient to answer, not what the answer is.**
**Explain simply:**
- Normal aptitude problem: all the data is given and you calculate the answer.
- Data sufficiency: the data comes in two pieces (Statement I and Statement II) and you decide which pieces, alone or together, pin down exactly ONE answer.
- "Sufficient" means one definite answer. For a yes/no question, a firm "always yes" or firm "always no" is sufficient; "sometimes yes, sometimes no" is not.
- HR link: it is like deciding whether you have enough facts in a file to take a decision, before spending time on analysis.
**Worked mini-example:** "What is Priya's age? I: She is 5 years older than Ravi. II: Ravi is 25." Neither alone fixes her age; together 25 + 5 = 30. You only needed to see that the two statements link up; the number 30 is a bonus.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you always compute the value?* No, often you only check that the equations can be solved. 2) *Why is it popular in tests?* It checks logic and speed together.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Spending time on full calculation; using outside knowledge (for example guessing a typical salary) that the statements do not give.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the five standard answer options in a data sufficiency question, and what does each one mean?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have the answer key memorised, because every question depends on it.
**Say this in the interview:** "A: only I works. B: only II works. C: need both together. D: each works alone. E: even both together are not enough."
**Answer:** **A, B, C, D, E as in the list below (this is the key used in this Part).**
**Explain simply:**
- A: Statement I alone is sufficient, II alone is not.
- B: Statement II alone is sufficient, I alone is not.
- C: Both together are sufficient, neither alone is.
- D: Each statement alone is sufficient.
- E: Both together are still not sufficient.
**Worked mini-example:** Q: "Is n even? I: n+1 is odd. II: n is divisible by 3." I alone makes n even (sufficient); II alone allows 3 (odd) and 6 (even) (not sufficient). Option A.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do all tests use this wording?* Some use only "Yes/No/Cannot say"; read the instructions first (unverified which tests do). 2) *If both alone work, which letter?* D, never C.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing C when each statement is already enough alone (should be D); mixing up A and B; choosing E without actually combining the statements.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is Priya's age? Statement I: Priya is 5 years older than Ravi. Statement II: Ravi is 25 years old.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Basic combining of two linked facts.
**Say this in the interview:** "Statement I gives the gap but not Ravi's age, and II gives Ravi's age but not the gap. Together Priya = 25 + 5 = 30, so C."
**Answer:** **30 years, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: Priya = Ravi + 5, but Ravi is unknown, so many ages fit (not sufficient).
2. II alone: Ravi is 25, nothing about Priya (not sufficient).
3. Together: Priya = 25 + 5 = 30, one answer (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Each statement holds a missing piece; the question needs both pieces.
- A chain of facts (Priya from Ravi, Ravi from a number) usually means C.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said Priya is 30?* Then II alone suffices, option B. 2) *If I said Priya is twice Ravi's age?* Still C, needing II.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing A because "5 years older" feels like an age clue.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is Anil's monthly salary? (Anil is paid the same amount every month, 12 months a year, with no bonus.) Statement I: His annual salary is Rs 6,00,000. Statement II: His salary is 20% more than Sunil's.

**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting that a statement relating two unknowns does not help.
**Say this in the interview:** "Annual salary divided by 12 gives Anil's monthly pay, so I is enough; II compares him to Sunil, whose pay is unknown, so II is not. Answer A."
**Answer:** **Rs 50,000 per month, so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: 6,00,000 / 12 = 50,000 (sufficient).
2. II alone: Anil = 1.2 x Sunil, Sunil is unknown, so Anil could be anything (not sufficient).
3. Verdict: I alone only, option A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A percentage comparison needs a base number; without Sunil's pay there is none.
- The bracket in the question (12 equal months, no bonus) is what makes I sufficient.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If there was a bonus?* I would no longer be enough. 2) *If II gave Sunil's pay as Rs 40,000?* II alone gives 48,000, so D.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing C: adding II does not help because it brings one new unknown (Sunil).

**Answer:** **Rs 1,000, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: CP = 800, but profit is unknown, so SP is unknown (not sufficient).

**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking A because the cost price looks like the main number.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Is the integer n even? Statement I: n + 1 is odd. Statement II: n is divisible by 3.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Parity (odd and even) reasoning and 'sufficient for yes/no'.
**Say this in the interview:** "If n + 1 is odd, n must be even, so I is a firm yes. II allows 3 (odd) and 6 (even). Answer A."
**Answer:** **Yes, n is even (from I alone), so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: n + 1 odd means n is even (always yes, sufficient).
2. II alone: n = 3 is odd, n = 6 is even (sometimes yes, sometimes no, not sufficient).
3. Verdict: A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Odd + 1 = even and even + 1 = odd, so n+1 odd forces n even.
- For yes/no questions, a certain 'yes' or certain 'no' is sufficient.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said n is divisible by 4?* Then II alone gives yes, option D. 2) *If I said n+1 is even?* Then n is odd, a firm 'no', still sufficient.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking a 'no' answer is 'not sufficient'; a firm no is sufficient.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the area of a rectangle? Statement I: Its perimeter is 40 cm. Statement II: Its length is twice its breadth.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Counting unknowns against equations.
**Say this in the interview:** "A rectangle has two unknowns, length and breadth, so I need two independent facts. Each statement gives one; together they fix both. Answer C."
**Answer:** **800/9 sq cm (about 88.9 sq cm), so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: L + B = 20; (10,10) gives 100, (15,5) gives 75 (not sufficient).
2. II alone: L = 2B; B = 1 gives 2, B = 2 gives 8 (not sufficient).
3. Together: 2B + B = 20, B = 20/3, L = 40/3, area = 40/3 x 20/3 = 800/9 = 88.89 sq cm (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Rule: two unknowns need two independent equations.
- Perimeter = 2(L + B), so half-perimeter is L + B = 20.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said L = B + 4?* Then B = 8, L = 12, area 96. 2) *If II said it is a square?* Then I alone gives 100.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming the sides are whole numbers; they need not be.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the step-by-step method (the AD / BCE elimination grid) for solving data sufficiency questions quickly?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A fixed, calm routine that avoids wasted calculation.
**Say this in the interview:** "I test Statement I alone: if it works the answer is A or D, if not it is B, C or E. Then I test II alone, and only if neither works alone do I combine them."
**Answer:** **Test I alone, test II alone, combine only if needed, using the AD / BCE grid.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Read the question, say it in your own words (value? yes/no?).
2. Test Statement I alone. If sufficient, the answer is A or D (cross out B, C, E). If not, it is B, C or E.
3. Test Statement II alone. Case I sufficient: II sufficient gives D, II not sufficient gives A. Case I not sufficient: II sufficient gives B; II not sufficient means go to step 4.
4. Combine both only when neither alone worked: sufficient gives C, not sufficient gives E.
**Explain simply:**
- AD group: Statement I is sufficient alone. BCE group: Statement I is not sufficient alone.
- Judge, do not solve: stop when you know whether one answer exists.
- Statement I alone means ignore Statement II completely.
**Worked mini-example:** Q: "What is Priya's age? I: Priya is 5 years older than Ravi. II: Ravi is 25." I alone fails, so the answer is B, C or E. II alone fails, so C or E. Together 30, so C.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long per question?* About 1 to 1.5 minutes. 2) *When to guess?* If stuck after combining, pick E if the unknowns clearly exceed the equations.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Skipping the "alone" tests and combining straight away; solving fully; forgetting D is possible.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why must you not carry information from Statement I into your test of Statement II when checking each statement alone?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand that each statement is a separate, independent test.
**Say this in the interview:** "If I borrow facts from Statement I, I might call II sufficient when it is not, and I could never tell D from C."
**Answer:** **Because "alone" means alone; mixing the statements destroys the difference between options A, B, C and D.**
**Explain simply:**
- Each statement is tested as if the other did not exist.
- Carrying information over turns every C answer into a false D, and every A or B into a false D.
- Combine only after both alone tests fail.
**Worked mini-example:** "What is Priya's age? I: Priya is 5 years older than Ravi. II: Ravi is 25." If you carry "5 years older" into II, then II looks like it gives 30. But II alone (Ravi is 25) says nothing about Priya, so the correct option is C, not B or D.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is it fine to reuse the question's own bracketed facts?* Yes, those belong to the question stem and apply to both. 2) *Can a statement repeat the other?* Yes, then it is D or E.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing D after silently using Statement I's number in Statement II.

**Answer:** **x = 5 (from I alone), so option A**

1. Stem: x^2 = 25 gives x = 5 or x = -5.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can the median equal the average?* Yes by chance: salaries 10k, 20k, 50k, 70k, 1,00k have median 50k and total 2,50,000. 2) *Same median, different average?* Change the last salary to 1,10k and the average becomes 52k while the median stays 50k.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming the median equals the average.

### Q22 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the average speed for a round trip between two towns? (Same road both ways, no stops.) Statement I: The speed going is 40 km/h and the speed returning is 60 km/h. Statement II: The one-way distance is 120 km.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Average speed for equal distances is the harmonic mean, not the arithmetic mean.
**Say this in the interview:** "For equal distances, average speed is total distance over total time, which works out to 2 x 40 x 60 / (40 + 60) = 48. The distance cancels, so II is not needed. Answer A."
**Answer:** **48 km/h, so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: with distance d each way, time = d/40 + d/60 = 5d/120 = d/24 for a total of 2d; average = 2d / (d/24) = 48 km/h (sufficient, d cancels).
2. II alone: distance only, no speeds (not sufficient).
3. Verdict: A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed = total distance / total time, not the average of the two speeds (50 is the trap).
- Shortcut: 2ab / (a + b).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *With II added?* Time = 3 + 2 = 5 h, 240 km, 48 km/h. 2) *If speeds were 30 and 60?* 40 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 50 km/h.

### Q23 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 At what rate percent per annum is simple interest charged on a loan? Statement I: The principal is Rs 5,000. Statement II: The simple interest earned in 2 years is Rs 1,000.

### Q24 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Is x positive? Statement I: x cubed is greater than 0. Statement II: x squared is greater than 0.

**What the interviewer is checking:** Sign tests: odd powers keep sign, even powers lose it.

### Q25 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Out of 100 employees, how many know both SQL and Excel? (Every employee knows at least one of the two.) Statement I: 70 employees know SQL. Statement II: 60 employees know Excel.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Overlap (inclusion-exclusion) needs both set sizes.
**Say this in the interview:** "Everyone knows at least one, so SQL + Excel - both = 100. I and II give the two set sizes, so both = 70 + 60 - 100 = 30. Answer C."
**Answer:** **30 employees, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: 70 know SQL, Excel unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: 60 know Excel, SQL unknown (not sufficient).
3. Together: both = 70 + 60 - 100 = 30 (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Union = A + B - overlap; with no one knowing neither, union = 100.
**Likely follow-ups:**  1) *Only SQL?* 40. 2) *If 10 knew neither?* Union 90, both = 40.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding 70 + 60 = 130 and stopping.
