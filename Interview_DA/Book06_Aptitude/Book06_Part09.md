# Book 6 — Aptitude & Business Maths | Part 9: Data Sufficiency 🟢🟡🔴

**Target:** 40 questions | **Actual:** 49 questions
**Scope:** data sufficiency method and fully specified problems with two statements each; answer key is the standard five options: A = Statement I alone is sufficient but II alone is not; B = II alone is sufficient but I alone is not; C = both together are sufficient but neither alone is; D = each alone is sufficient; E = both together are still not sufficient.

### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is a data sufficiency question, and how is it different from a normal aptitude problem?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know that the task is to judge if the data is enough, not to compute the final number.
**Say this in the interview:** "A data sufficiency question gives a question and two statements. I do not have to find the answer; I only decide whether the statements give enough information to find one single answer."
**Answer:** **It tests whether the information is sufficient to answer, not what the answer is.**
**Explain simply:**
- Normal aptitude problem: all the data is given and you calculate the answer.
- Data sufficiency: the data comes in two pieces (Statement I and Statement II) and you decide which pieces, alone or together, pin down exactly ONE answer.
- "Sufficient" means one definite answer. For a yes/no question, a firm "always yes" or firm "always no" is sufficient; "sometimes yes, sometimes no" is not.
- HR link: it is like deciding whether you have enough facts in a file to take a decision, before spending time on analysis.
**Worked mini-example:** "What is Priya's age? I: She is 5 years older than Ravi. II: Ravi is 25." Neither alone fixes her age; together 25 + 5 = 30. You only needed to see that the two statements link up; the number 30 is a bonus.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you always compute the value?* No, often you only check that the equations can be solved. 2) *Why is it popular in tests?* It checks logic and speed together.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Spending time on full calculation; using outside knowledge (for example guessing a typical salary) that the statements do not give.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the five standard answer options in a data sufficiency question, and what does each one mean?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have the answer key memorised, because every question depends on it.
**Say this in the interview:** "A: only I works. B: only II works. C: need both together. D: each works alone. E: even both together are not enough."
**Answer:** **A, B, C, D, E as in the list below (this is the key used in this Part).**
**Explain simply:**
- A: Statement I alone is sufficient, II alone is not.
- B: Statement II alone is sufficient, I alone is not.
- C: Both together are sufficient, neither alone is.
- D: Each statement alone is sufficient.
- E: Both together are still not sufficient.
**Worked mini-example:** Q: "Is n even? I: n+1 is odd. II: n is divisible by 3." I alone makes n even (sufficient); II alone allows 3 (odd) and 6 (even) (not sufficient). Option A.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do all tests use this wording?* Some use only "Yes/No/Cannot say"; read the instructions first (unverified which tests do). 2) *If both alone work, which letter?* D, never C.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing C when each statement is already enough alone (should be D); mixing up A and B; choosing E without actually combining the statements.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is Priya's age? Statement I: Priya is 5 years older than Ravi. Statement II: Ravi is 25 years old.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Basic combining of two linked facts.
**Say this in the interview:** "Statement I gives the gap but not Ravi's age, and II gives Ravi's age but not the gap. Together Priya = 25 + 5 = 30, so C."
**Answer:** **30 years, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: Priya = Ravi + 5, but Ravi is unknown, so many ages fit (not sufficient).
2. II alone: Ravi is 25, nothing about Priya (not sufficient).
3. Together: Priya = 25 + 5 = 30, one answer (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Each statement holds a missing piece; the question needs both pieces.
- A chain of facts (Priya from Ravi, Ravi from a number) usually means C.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said Priya is 30?* Then II alone suffices, option B. 2) *If I said Priya is twice Ravi's age?* Still C, needing II.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing A because "5 years older" feels like an age clue.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is Anil's monthly salary? (Anil is paid the same amount every month, 12 months a year, with no bonus.) Statement I: His annual salary is Rs 6,00,000. Statement II: His salary is 20% more than Sunil's.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Spotting that a statement relating two unknowns does not help.
**Say this in the interview:** "Annual salary divided by 12 gives Anil's monthly pay, so I is enough; II compares him to Sunil, whose pay is unknown, so II is not. Answer A."
**Answer:** **Rs 50,000 per month, so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: 6,00,000 / 12 = 50,000 (sufficient).
2. II alone: Anil = 1.2 x Sunil, Sunil is unknown, so Anil could be anything (not sufficient).
3. Verdict: I alone only, option A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A percentage comparison needs a base number; without Sunil's pay there is none.
- The bracket in the question (12 equal months, no bonus) is what makes I sufficient.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If there was a bonus?* I would no longer be enough. 2) *If II gave Sunil's pay as Rs 40,000?* II alone gives 48,000, so D.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing C: adding II does not help because it brings one new unknown (Sunil).

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the selling price of an item? Statement I: The cost price is Rs 800. Statement II: The profit is 25% of the cost price.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Seeing that a profit percentage needs a cost base.
**Say this in the interview:** "I gives the cost, II gives the profit as a fraction of the cost. Alone neither gives the selling price; together SP = 800 + 25% of 800 = 1,000. Answer C."
**Answer:** **Rs 1,000, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: CP = 800, but profit is unknown, so SP is unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: SP = 1.25 x CP, CP unknown (not sufficient).
3. Together: SP = 1.25 x 800 = 1,000 (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Profit % is always measured on cost price unless stated otherwise.
- SP = CP x (1 + profit%).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said the profit is Rs 200?* Then together SP = 1,000 as well. 2) *If the profit % were on SP?* Then SP = 800 / 0.75 = 1,066.67.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking A because the cost price looks like the main number.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Is the integer n even? Statement I: n + 1 is odd. Statement II: n is divisible by 3.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Parity (odd and even) reasoning and 'sufficient for yes/no'.
**Say this in the interview:** "If n + 1 is odd, n must be even, so I is a firm yes. II allows 3 (odd) and 6 (even). Answer A."
**Answer:** **Yes, n is even (from I alone), so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: n + 1 odd means n is even (always yes, sufficient).
2. II alone: n = 3 is odd, n = 6 is even (sometimes yes, sometimes no, not sufficient).
3. Verdict: A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Odd + 1 = even and even + 1 = odd, so n+1 odd forces n even.
- For yes/no questions, a certain 'yes' or certain 'no' is sufficient.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said n is divisible by 4?* Then II alone gives yes, option D. 2) *If I said n+1 is even?* Then n is odd, a firm 'no', still sufficient.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking a 'no' answer is 'not sufficient'; a firm no is sufficient.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the total marks of a student in 3 subjects? Statement I: The average marks in the 3 subjects is 70. Statement II: The highest mark in any one subject is 85.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Knowing that an average times the count gives the total.
**Say this in the interview:** "Average x number of subjects = total, so I gives 210. The highest mark does not give the total. Answer A."
**Answer:** **210 marks, so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: total = 70 x 3 = 210 (sufficient).
2. II alone: highest mark 85 leaves the other two free (not sufficient).
3. Verdict: A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Total = average x number of items.
- Max or median statements do not fix a total.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If the average were 70 over 4 subjects?* Total 280. 2) *Does II add anything after I?* No, it is extra.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing C because there are two numbers; extra data does not mean needed data.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the area of a rectangle? Statement I: Its perimeter is 40 cm. Statement II: Its length is twice its breadth.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Counting unknowns against equations.
**Say this in the interview:** "A rectangle has two unknowns, length and breadth, so I need two independent facts. Each statement gives one; together they fix both. Answer C."
**Answer:** **800/9 sq cm (about 88.9 sq cm), so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: L + B = 20; (10,10) gives 100, (15,5) gives 75 (not sufficient).
2. II alone: L = 2B; B = 1 gives 2, B = 2 gives 8 (not sufficient).
3. Together: 2B + B = 20, B = 20/3, L = 40/3, area = 40/3 x 20/3 = 800/9 = 88.89 sq cm (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Rule: two unknowns need two independent equations.
- Perimeter = 2(L + B), so half-perimeter is L + B = 20.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said L = B + 4?* Then B = 8, L = 12, area 96. 2) *If II said it is a square?* Then I alone gives 100.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Assuming the sides are whole numbers; they need not be.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A company has employees only in one pool; one employee is picked at random. What is the probability that the employee picked is a woman? Statement I: There are 40 women in the pool. Statement II: There are 100 employees in the pool.

**What the interviewer is checking:** Probability as favourable over total, and needing both numbers.
**Say this in the interview:** "Probability needs women and total. I has women only, II has total only. Together 40/100 = 0.4. Answer C."
**Answer:** **0.4 (40%), so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: 40 women, total unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: total 100, women unknown (not sufficient).
3. Together: 40/100 = 0.4 (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Probability = favourable / total outcomes.
- Both numerator and denominator must be known.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II gave the number of men (60)?* Total would be 100 from I and II, so C again. 2) *If I said women are 40% of the pool?* I alone suffices.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating 40 as the probability directly.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 By what percentage did sales rise this year compared with last year? Statement I: This year's sales are Rs 1.2 crore. Statement II: Last year's sales were Rs 1 crore.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Percentage change needs both old and new values.
**Say this in the interview:** "Percentage rise = (new - old)/old. I gives new, II gives old; one alone is not enough. Together 20%. Answer C."
**Answer:** **20% rise, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: this year 1.2 crore, last year unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: last year 1 crore, this year unknown (not sufficient).
3. Together: (1.2 - 1.0) / 1.0 = 0.2 = 20% (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Percentage change = (new - old) / old x 100.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If sales fell to 0.8 crore?* Change is -20% (a fall). 2) *If I said sales grew by Rs 20 lakh?* Then still need old value.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing by the new value (0.2/1.2 = 16.7%) instead of the old.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the discount percentage on an item? Statement I: The marked price is Rs 2,000. Statement II: The selling price is Rs 1,700.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Discount is measured on marked price, and needs two prices.
**Say this in the interview:** "Discount % = (MP - SP)/MP. I gives MP, II gives SP; together 300/2000 = 15%. Answer C."
**Answer:** **15%, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: MP = 2,000, SP unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: SP = 1,700, MP unknown (not sufficient).
3. Together: discount = 2,000 - 1,700 = 300; 300 / 2,000 = 0.15 = 15% (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Discount % = (marked price - selling price) / marked price x 100.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said the discount is Rs 300?* Then with I still 15%. 2) *If II gave 'SP is 85% of MP'?* II alone suffices (15%).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing by the selling price (300/1700 = 17.6%).

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the profit percentage on a sale? Statement I: The selling price is Rs 1,500. Statement II: The selling price is 20% above the cost price.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the step-by-step method (the AD / BCE elimination grid) for solving data sufficiency questions quickly?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A fixed, calm routine that avoids wasted calculation.
**Say this in the interview:** "I test Statement I alone: if it works the answer is A or D, if not it is B, C or E. Then I test II alone, and only if neither works alone do I combine them."
**Answer:** **Test I alone, test II alone, combine only if needed, using the AD / BCE grid.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Read the question, say it in your own words (value? yes/no?).
2. Test Statement I alone. If sufficient, the answer is A or D (cross out B, C, E). If not, it is B, C or E.
3. Test Statement II alone. Case I sufficient: II sufficient gives D, II not sufficient gives A. Case I not sufficient: II sufficient gives B; II not sufficient means go to step 4.
4. Combine both only when neither alone worked: sufficient gives C, not sufficient gives E.
**Explain simply:**
- AD group: Statement I is sufficient alone. BCE group: Statement I is not sufficient alone.
- Judge, do not solve: stop when you know whether one answer exists.
- Statement I alone means ignore Statement II completely.
**Worked mini-example:** Q: "What is Priya's age? I: Priya is 5 years older than Ravi. II: Ravi is 25." I alone fails, so the answer is B, C or E. II alone fails, so C or E. Together 30, so C.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long per question?* About 1 to 1.5 minutes. 2) *When to guess?* If stuck after combining, pick E if the unknowns clearly exceed the equations.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Skipping the "alone" tests and combining straight away; solving fully; forgetting D is possible.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why must you not carry information from Statement I into your test of Statement II when checking each statement alone?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand that each statement is a separate, independent test.
**Say this in the interview:** "If I borrow facts from Statement I, I might call II sufficient when it is not, and I could never tell D from C."
**Answer:** **Because "alone" means alone; mixing the statements destroys the difference between options A, B, C and D.**
**Explain simply:**
- Each statement is tested as if the other did not exist.
- Carrying information over turns every C answer into a false D, and every A or B into a false D.
- Combine only after both alone tests fail.
**Worked mini-example:** "What is Priya's age? I: Priya is 5 years older than Ravi. II: Ravi is 25." If you carry "5 years older" into II, then II looks like it gives 30. But II alone (Ravi is 25) says nothing about Priya, so the correct option is C, not B or D.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is it fine to reuse the question's own bracketed facts?* Yes, those belong to the question stem and apply to both. 2) *Can a statement repeat the other?* Yes, then it is D or E.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing D after silently using Statement I's number in Statement II.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the value of x, if x squared equals 25? Statement I: x is greater than 0. Statement II: x is an integer.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Squares have two roots; integer tests are not enough.
**Say this in the interview:** "x squared = 25 gives x = 5 or -5. I removes -5 and leaves 5. II says integer but both are integers. Answer A."
**Answer:** **x = 5 (from I alone), so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Stem: x^2 = 25 gives x = 5 or x = -5.
2. I alone: x > 0 picks 5 (sufficient).
3. II alone: 5 and -5 are both integers, so still two values (not sufficient).
4. Verdict: A.
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Every positive square has two square roots, one positive and one negative.
- A property that both candidates share does not separate them.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If II said x is less than 0?* Then x = -5 and II alone suffices. 2) *If the stem said x^3 = 125?* x = 5 only.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Taking only x = 5 and calling II sufficient, or ignoring that I is needed.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How many employees are in a department? (Every employee is either a man or a woman.) Statement I: The men are 3/5 of the department. Statement II: There are 24 women in the department.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** A fraction alone gives a ratio, not a count.
**Say this in the interview:** "Men are 3/5, so women are 2/5. I gives only the proportion. II gives 24 women. Together 24 is 2/5 of the total, so total 60. Answer C."
**Answer:** **60 employees, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: men = 3/5 of total, total unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: 24 women, men unknown (not sufficient).
3. Together: women = 1 - 3/5 = 2/5; 2/5 x total = 24, total = 24 x 5/2 = 60 (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Proportions need one actual count to become numbers.
- The bracket (every employee is a man or woman) is what makes women = 2/5.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Number of men?* 36. 2) *If II said 36 men?* Then together 36 is 3/5, total 60 again.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing A because the fraction feels like a full description.

### Q17 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the speed of a train in m/s? Statement I: It crosses a 200 m long platform in 30 seconds. Statement II: The length of the train is 100 m.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).

**Answer:** **10 m/s, so option C**

- 10 m/s = 36 km/h (multiply by 18/5).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If it crossed a pole in 10 s?* Speed 10 m/s from II alone. 2) *In km/h?* 36 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using only 200 m (20 m/s).

### Q18 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 In how many days can A alone finish a job? Statement I: A and B together finish the job in 12 days. Statement II: B alone finishes the job in 20 days.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Work rates: A's rate = combined rate minus B's rate.
**Say this in the interview:** "Rates add. Together 1/12 per day, B 1/20, so A is 1/12 - 1/20 = 1/30. Each statement alone is not enough. Answer C."
**Answer:** **30 days, so option C**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. I alone: A + B = 1/12 per day, B's share unknown (not sufficient).
2. II alone: B = 1/20, says nothing about A (not sufficient).
3. Together: A = 1/12 - 1/20 = 5/60 - 3/60 = 2/60 = 1/30 per day, so 30 days (sufficient).
**Check:** verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Work per day (rate) = 1 / days to finish alone; rates of people working together add up.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If I said 15 days together and II 20 days?* A = 1/15 - 1/20 = 1/60, so 60 days. 2) *Days for B?* Given: 20.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting days (12 - 20) instead of rates.

### Q19 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A company had 200 employees at the start of the year. (Attrition rate = employees who left during the year divided by the opening headcount.) Was the annual attrition rate above 10%? Statement I: 25 employees left during the year. Statement II: 15 new employees joined during the year.

**Answer:** **Yes, attrition was above 10%, so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Threshold: 10% of 200 = 20 leavers.
2. I alone: 25 / 200 = 12.5% > 10%, firm yes (sufficient).

### Q20 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is the average salary of 5 employees? Statement I: The total salary of the 5 employees is Rs 2,50,000. Statement II: The median salary of the 5 employees is Rs 50,000.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (FACE Prep, PlacementPreparation.io) say data sufficiency style items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and some company aptitude rounds (unverified prep-site claim).

**Answer:** **Rs 50,000, so option A**
**Step-by-step working:**

2. II alone: median is the middle salary, others can be anything (not sufficient).

**Answer:** **C (both together are sufficient but neither alone is): Rs 2,50,000**
**Verdict:** Statement I alone: not sufficient: B and C share 60%, but the split is unknown. Statement II alone: not sufficient: A and C share 65%, split unknown. Together: sufficient: C = 100% - 40% - 35% = 25% of Rs 10,00,000 = Rs 2,50,000.

1. Total = 100%.
2. I alone: C = 60% - B, so many values.
3. II alone: C = 65% - A, so many values.
4. Together: C = 100 - 40 - 35 = 25%.
5. 25% of 10,00,000 = Rs 2,50,000.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** The remaining share equals total minus the known shares, but only when you know all the other shares.

**Explain simply:**

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Is the positive integer n divisible by 12? Statement I: n is divisible by 4. Statement II: n is divisible by 6.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites say data sufficiency items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and company aptitude rounds (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Using the lowest common multiple (LCM): divisible by 4 and by 6 means divisible by 12.
**Say this in the interview:** "The LCM of 4 and 6 is 12, so together n is always divisible by 12: yes. Alone, 4 and 6 fail: C."
**Answer:** **C (both together are sufficient but neither alone is): yes, always**
**Verdict:** Statement I alone: not sufficient: n = 4 (no) and n = 12 (yes). Statement II alone: not sufficient: n = 6 (no) and n = 12 (yes). Together: sufficient: n is a multiple of the LCM of 4 and 6, which is 12, so the answer is always yes.
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Statement I: n = 4 is divisible by 4 but not 12. n = 12 is. So answers vary.
2. Statement II: n = 6 is not; n = 12 is. Vary again.
3. Together: n is a common multiple of 4 and 6. LCM(4,6) = 12. Every common multiple is a multiple of 12.
4. So the answer is always yes. Note that 4 x 6 = 24 is not the test: LCM is.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** The correct test is "divisible by each of two numbers means divisible by their LCM", not their product.
**Check:** verified by script (brute force over all cases).
**Explain simply:**
- For coprime numbers (like 3 and 4) the LCM equals the product, so "divisible by 3 and 4" means divisible by 12.
- For 4 and 6, which share a factor 2, the LCM is 12 not 24.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is n divisible by 24 if divisible by 4 and 6?* Not necessarily (n = 12). 2) *Divisible by 8 and 12 means divisible by?* LCM = 24.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Believing 4 and 6 can't combine because they share a factor. Common multiples still need to be multiples of 12.

**Verdict:** Statement I alone: sufficient: x > 1 gives x squared = x times x > x times 1 = x. Always yes (x = 2: 4 > 2). Statement II alone: sufficient: x < 0 makes x squared positive and x negative, so always yes (x = -3: 9 > -3). Together: not needed.
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Statement I: test x = 1.5 (2.25 > 1.5), 2 (4 > 2), 10 (100 > 10): always yes.
2. Statement II: test -0.5 (0.25 > -0.5), -1 (1 > -1), -5 (25 > -5): always yes.
3. Both give a definite yes, so each is sufficient.

- For a yes/no question, one definite answer is enough, even if two statements are about different ranges of x.

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What are the common traps in data sufficiency questions (squares and roots, assuming integers, assuming a figure is to scale, and similar)?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites say data sufficiency items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and company aptitude rounds (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Awareness of the classic DS traps that tempt you to pick the wrong option.
**Say this in the interview:** "I watch for squares giving two roots, hidden integer assumptions, drawings that are not to scale, and carrying Statement I into Statement II when judging II alone."
**Answer:** **The main traps: squares with two roots, assuming integers or positives, trusting a diagram, mixing the statements, and assuming the answer must exist.**
**Explain simply:**
- Squares and roots: x squared = 9 gives x = 3 or -3, so x is not found unless sign is given.
- Assuming integers: if "integer" is not stated, x can be a fraction. If it is stated, use it to prune candidates.
- Not-to-scale figures: a triangle that looks right-angled may not be unless stated.
- Carrying information across: judge Statement I alone first, without II, and then II alone; only combine at the end.
- Zero, negatives and fractions: test them in yes/no questions (e.g. x squared > x).
- Two equations that are the same: 2x + 3y = 12 and 4x + 6y = 24 give no unique pair.
- Solving too far: you only need to decide whether the answer is unique, not compute it.
- Option order: remember A, B, C, D, E are fixed by the key (D is "each alone", E is "not even together").
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Give a square-root trap.* "What is x? Statement: x squared = 25": x = 5 or -5, insufficient. 2) *Give an integer trap.* "x + y = 3" is insufficient unless positive integers are stated.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Falling for the one trap per question that the setter plants. Test the awkward cases deliberately.

### Q48 ⭐ 🟡 A manager says: 'Sales in Hyderabad fell 10% after our campaign, so the campaign failed.' How would you judge whether the data given is sufficient to conclude that?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites say data sufficiency items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and company aptitude rounds (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Applying sufficiency thinking to a business claim: correlation versus causation.
**Say this in the interview:** "A 10% fall after the campaign is not enough to say it failed. I would ask what would have happened without it: trend, season, other changes, and a comparison group."
**Answer:** **Not sufficient. A fall after the campaign does not show the campaign caused it or failed; you need a baseline, a comparison and the campaign's goal.**
**Explain simply:**
- Compare with what would have happened anyway: was the previous trend already falling by more than 10%?
- Seasonality: compare the same month last year.
- Other causes: price changes, competitor offers, stock-outs, a festival, a hiring freeze.
- A control group: other cities (say Bengaluru) without the campaign; if they fell 20%, the campaign may have helped.
- Campaign goal: the aim may have been brand awareness, not immediate sales; define success first.

**Common mistakes / traps:** Concluding "failed" from before-and-after alone, the post hoc fallacy: after this, therefore because of this.

### Q49 ⭐ 🔴 Is n squared minus 1 divisible by 8? (n is a positive integer.) Statement I: n is odd. Statement II: n is prime.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites say data sufficiency items appear in AMCAT, TCS NQT and company aptitude rounds (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Factorising n squared minus 1 and testing special cases such as n = 2.
**Say this in the interview:** "n squared - 1 is (n-1)(n+1). For odd n these are consecutive even numbers, so a multiple of 8. Primes include 2, where it is 3, so II fails: A."
**Answer:** **A (Statement I alone is sufficient but II alone is not)**
**Verdict:** Statement I alone: sufficient: for odd n, (n-1)(n+1) is a product of two consecutive even numbers, which is always divisible by 8 (n = 3: 8; n = 5: 24; n = 7: 48; n = 9: 80). Statement II alone: not sufficient: n = 3 gives 8 (yes), but n = 2 (which is prime) gives 3 (no). Together: not needed.
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Factorise: n squared - 1 = (n - 1)(n + 1).
2. If n is odd, n - 1 and n + 1 are consecutive even numbers, e.g. 2 and 4, 4 and 6. One of any two consecutive evens is a multiple of 4, and the other is at least a multiple of 2, so the product has 4 x 2 = 8 as a factor.
3. Statement II: primes are 2, 3, 5, 7... n = 3, 5, 7, 11 give 8, 24, 48, 120, all yes. But n = 2 gives 4 - 1 = 3, not divisible by 8.
4. So II gives both yes and no.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Odd numbers have a simple structure around them; the only even prime, 2, is the exception.
**Check:** verified by script (brute force over all cases).
**Explain simply:**
- 2 is the only even prime and is a classic trap.
- Factorising a difference of squares: a squared - b squared = (a - b)(a + b).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is n squared - 1 divisible by 8 for every odd n?* Yes (verified for odd n below 50 by script). 2) *Is it divisible by 16?* No: n = 3 gives 8.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Believing that every prime greater than 2 is odd means primes are fine: but the statement includes 2.
