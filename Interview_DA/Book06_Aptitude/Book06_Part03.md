# Book 6 — Aptitude & Business Maths | Part 3: Time & Work, Time-Speed-Distance 🟢🟡🔴

**Target:** 60 questions | **Actual:** 65 questions
**Scope:** time and work (rates, efficiency, wages, pipes and cisterns), speed-distance-time, average speed, trains, boats and streams, relative speed, in the form asked in online aptitude gates.

### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A can complete a job in 12 days and B in 15 days. Working together, how many days do they take?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "A and B together" is the standard opener in time-and-work lists on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you add daily rates, not days.
**Say this in the interview:** "A does 1/12 of the job a day and B does 1/15, so together they do 9/60 = 3/20 a day. One job takes 20/3 days."
**Answer:** **6 2/3 days (20/3 days, about 6.67 days)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A's one-day work = 1/12; B's one-day work = 1/15.
2. Together in one day = 1/12 + 1/15 = 5/60 + 4/60 = 9/60 = 3/20.
3. Days for the whole job = 1 divided by 3/20 = 20/3 days = 6 2/3 days.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Product over sum: (12 x 15)/(12 + 15) = 180/27 = 20/3. It works because adding the fractions 1/12 + 1/15 always gives (12+15)/(12x15).
**Check:** In 20/3 days A does (20/3)/12 = 5/9 and B does (20/3)/15 = 4/9; 5/9 + 4/9 = 1. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A "rate" is the share of the job done in one day. Rates add when people work at the same time.
- Time for the whole job = 1 divided by the combined rate.
**Likely follow-ups:** If A takes 10 days and B 15 days? (10 x 15)/25 = 6 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Averaging the days ((12+15)/2 = 13.5) or adding them (27). Both are wrong; together must be faster than the faster person alone (under 12 days).

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 If A alone takes a days and B alone takes b days to finish a job, what is the formula for the time they take together, and why do you add their rates rather than their times?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a core concept item in time-and-work syllabi (Byju's concept notes, prep sites; unverified for any company).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand the idea behind the formula, not only the memorised shortcut.
**Say this in the interview:** "Together time = ab/(a+b). I add rates because people working at the same time each contribute part of the job every day, and contributions per day add up; days do not add because the two people are not working one after the other."
**Answer:** **Time together = ab/(a + b) days**
**Explain simply:**
- Rate = job done per day. A's rate = 1/a, B's rate = 1/b.
- Combined rate = 1/a + 1/b = (a + b)/(ab). Time = 1 divided by the combined rate = ab/(a + b).
- Rates add because they are "amount per day" and both are measured in the same unit (jobs per day). Times (days per job) cannot be added: two people sharing a job do not take longer than one.
- Sanity test: the answer is always smaller than the smaller of a and b.
**Worked mini-example:** Two HR coordinators take 6 and 3 hours to screen a batch of CVs. Rates 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2 per hour, so together 2 hours; formula: (6 x 3)/(6 + 3) = 18/9 = 2.
**Likely follow-ups:** What if B undoes the work (an outlet pipe)? Subtract the rate: ab/(a - b). For 6 and 8 hours: 48/2 = 24 hours.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing (a + b)/2 or a + b. Also forgetting that the formula is only for two workers; for three, add three rates.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Convert a delivery van's speed of 54 km/h into m/s.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); km/h to m/s is the first step of train problems listed on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Unit conversion with the 5/18 factor.
**Say this in the interview:** "Multiply km/h by 5/18 to get m/s: 54 x 5/18 = 15 m/s."
**Answer:** **15 m/s**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. 1 km = 1,000 m, 1 hour = 3,600 seconds.
2. 54 km/h = 54 x 1,000 / 3,600 m/s.
3. = 54,000 / 3,600 = 15 m/s.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 1,000/3,600 simplifies to 5/18. Divide 54 by 18 = 3, then multiply by 5 = 15.
**Check:** Reverse: 15 x 18/5 = 54 km/h. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- km/h to m/s: multiply by 5/18. m/s to km/h: multiply by 18/5.
- Speed numbers divisible by 18 (36, 54, 72, 90) give clean answers, which is why exams love them.
**Likely follow-ups:** 72 km/h in m/s? 72 x 5/18 = 20 m/s.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 18/5 instead of 5/18 (multiplying by 3.6 when you should divide by it). A m/s figure bigger than the km/h figure is a red flag.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 An employee commutes 30 km at an average speed of 40 km/h. How many minutes does the trip take?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Time = distance/speed, and converting hours to minutes.
**Say this in the interview:** "Time = 30/40 = 0.75 hours, which is 45 minutes."
**Answer:** **45 minutes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Time = distance / speed = 30 / 40 hours.
2. 30/40 = 3/4 hour.
3. 3/4 x 60 = 45 minutes.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** At 40 km/h you cover 2 km every 3 minutes (40/60 km per minute = 2/3 km). 30 km / (2/3) = 45 minutes.
**Check:** 45 minutes = 0.75 h; 0.75 x 40 = 30 km. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The triangle: distance = speed x time; time = distance / speed; speed = distance / time.
- The answer comes out in the unit of the speed (hours here); convert at the end.
**Likely follow-ups:** 50 km at 60 km/h? 5/6 h = 50 minutes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Giving 0.75 as "75 minutes", or answering in hours when minutes are asked.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Pipe A fills a tank in 20 minutes and pipe B fills it in 30 minutes. If both are opened together, how long does the tank take to fill?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); pipes are treated as a time-and-work variant in Infosys-branded practice sets (prep sites, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Pipes are just workers: add the fill rates.
**Say this in the interview:** "Rates are 1/20 and 1/30 per minute, together 5/60 = 1/12, so 12 minutes."
**Answer:** **12 minutes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A fills 1/20 of the tank per minute; B fills 1/30.
2. Together: 1/20 + 1/30 = 3/60 + 2/60 = 5/60 = 1/12.
3. Time = 12 minutes.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** (20 x 30)/(20 + 30) = 600/50 = 12. Or LCM method: take the tank as 60 units; A gives 3 units/min, B gives 2; together 5; 60/5 = 12.
**Check:** In 12 minutes A fills 12/20 = 3/5 and B fills 12/30 = 2/5; total 1. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A pipe filling a tank is the same maths as a worker doing a job.
- Fill rates add; the tank is "1 whole job".
**Likely follow-ups:** Pipes of 15 and 10 minutes? 150/25 = 6 minutes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding times (50) or averaging them (25). Together must be faster than 20.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A 150 m long train runs at 54 km/h. How many seconds does it take to cross a telegraph pole?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); train problems are reported for TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro tests on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That for a pole (a point), the distance to cover is just the train's own length.
**Say this in the interview:** "Speed is 54 x 5/18 = 15 m/s. The train must cover its own length, 150 m, so 150/15 = 10 seconds."
**Answer:** **10 seconds**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Convert: 54 km/h = 54 x 5/18 = 15 m/s.
2. Distance to cover = length of train = 150 m (the pole has no length).
3. Time = 150 / 15 = 10 seconds.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 54 km/h = 15 m/s at once; 150/15 = 10.
**Check:** 15 m/s x 10 s = 150 m. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- "Crossing" means the whole train passes the object: from the engine reaching it to the last coach leaving it.
- For a point object (pole, signal, man standing), distance = train length only.
**Likely follow-ups:** Same train, 100 m platform? (150 + 100)/15 = 16 2/3 s.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Leaving the speed in km/h while the length is in metres.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A can finish a job in 10 days, and B is twice as efficient as A. How many days does B alone take?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); efficiency-ratio items appear on practice sites (PlacementPreparation; unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Efficiency and time are inversely related.
**Say this in the interview:** "Twice as efficient means half the time: 5 days."
**Answer:** **5 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A's rate = 1/10 job per day.
2. B is twice as efficient, so B's rate = 2 x 1/10 = 1/5 per day.
3. Days = 1 / (1/5) = 5.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Efficiency ratio A:B = 1:2, so time ratio A:B = 2:1. A takes 10, so B takes 10/2 = 5.
**Check:** B does 1/5 x 5 = 1 job. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- More efficient = more work per day = fewer days for the same job.
- For the same job: time is inversely proportional to efficiency.
**Likely follow-ups:** B three times as efficient? 10/3 = 3 1/3 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 20 (multiplying instead of dividing).

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Data-entry operator A enters 30 records per minute and operator B enters 20 records per minute. Working together, how many minutes do they need to enter 1,500 records?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the data-entry framing is a gap-fill added for this book, not seen in a source.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Rates already given as quantities per minute; add and divide.
**Say this in the interview:** "Together they enter 50 records a minute, so 1,500/50 = 30 minutes."
**Answer:** **30 minutes**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Combined rate = 30 + 20 = 50 records per minute.
2. Time = total work / rate = 1,500 / 50.
3. = 30 minutes.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 1,500/50 = 150/5 = 30.
**Check:** 30 x 30 = 900 and 20 x 30 = 600; 900 + 600 = 1,500. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- When the work is a count (records) and rates are counts per minute, you do not need fractions: time = work / combined rate.
**Likely follow-ups:** If B takes a 10-minute break? A alone does 300 in those 10 minutes; remaining 1,200 / 50 = 24 minutes, so 34 minutes in all.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Computing only one operator's rate, or dividing 1,500 by 30 and 20 and adding.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 12 clerks process 600 forms in 10 days. How many days would 20 clerks take to process the same 600 forms, assuming equal efficiency?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the clerks and forms wording is a gap-fill added for this book.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Man-days: more workers, fewer days (inverse proportion).
**Say this in the interview:** "Total work is 12 x 10 = 120 clerk-days. With 20 clerks that is 120/20 = 6 days."
**Answer:** **6 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Work in "clerk-days" = 12 x 10 = 120.
2. The same work with 20 clerks: days = 120 / 20.
3. = 6 days.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Workers x days stays constant: 12 x 10 = 20 x d, d = 6.
**Check:** 20 x 6 = 120 = 12 x 10. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A man-day (here clerk-day) is one person working one day. A job has a fixed number of them.
- More people share the same fixed total, so each needs fewer days. This is "inverse proportion": when one quantity goes up, the other comes down by the same factor.
- Assumption: every clerk works at the same pace.
**Likely follow-ups:** 15 clerks? 120/15 = 8 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** The 600 forms is a distraction; since the forms are the same, you do not need it. Do not treat this as direct proportion (which would give 16.7).

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 8 men can finish a job in 15 days. How many men are needed to finish the same job in 10 days?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the classic man-days item in prep lists (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Inverse proportion between men and days.
**Say this in the interview:** "The job is 8 x 15 = 120 man-days. In 10 days I need 120/10 = 12 men."
**Answer:** **12 men**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Total work = 8 x 15 = 120 man-days.
2. Men needed = 120 / 10.
3. = 12 men.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Days dropped from 15 to 10 (ratio 2/3), so men must rise by 3/2: 8 x 3/2 = 12.
**Check:** 12 x 10 = 120. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Finishing in fewer days needs more workers, in exact inverse ratio.
- Extra men needed here: 12 - 8 = 4, in case the question asks "how many more".
**Likely follow-ups:** In 20 days? 120/20 = 6 men.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 4 (the additional men) when the total is asked, or 5.33 (direct proportion).

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A car travels from home to office at 60 km/h and returns along the same road at 40 km/h. What is the average speed for the whole round trip?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a return-trip average-speed item style appears in a GfG Wipro mock question (practice site, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That average speed is total distance over total time, not the simple average.
**Say this in the interview:** "Equal distances means the harmonic mean: 2 x 60 x 40/(60 + 40) = 48 km/h. The simple average 50 is the trap."
**Answer:** **48 km/h**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Pick a distance that suits both speeds: say 120 km each way (120 is the LCM, the smallest number both 60 and 40 divide into).
2. Time going = 120/60 = 2 h; time returning = 120/40 = 3 h; total 5 h.
3. Total distance = 240 km.
4. Average speed = 240/5 = 48 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** For equal distances: 2xy/(x + y) = 2 x 60 x 40/100 = 48. It works because average = total distance/total time, and the distance cancels out.
**Check:** The car spends more time at the slow speed, so the answer must sit below the simple average of 50, pulled towards 40: 48 does. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed = total distance / total time, always.
- You spend longer at the slower speed, so it counts for more in the average.
- The "harmonic mean" is just the name for the average that weights slow speeds more; Q24 explains it in full.
**Likely follow-ups:** 30 km/h up and 60 km/h back? 2 x 30 x 60/90 = 40 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking 50 km/h (arithmetic mean). That is always listed as an option.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A and B together finish a job in 8 days. A alone can finish it in 12 days. How many days does B alone take?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Subtracting a known rate from the combined rate.
**Say this in the interview:** "B's rate = 1/8 - 1/12 = 1/24, so B takes 24 days."
**Answer:** **24 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Together: 1/8 of the job a day. A alone: 1/12 a day.
2. B's rate = 1/8 - 1/12 = 3/24 - 2/24 = 1/24.
3. B takes 24 days.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** LCM method: job = 24 units. Together 3 units/day, A 2 units/day, so B 1 unit/day; 24/1 = 24 days.
**Check:** 1/12 + 1/24 = 3/24 = 1/8 which is 8 days together. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Combined rate = A's rate + B's rate, so B's rate = combined minus A's.
- Convert back to days by taking 1 over the rate.
**Likely follow-ups:** Together 6 days, A alone 10? 1/6 - 1/10 = 1/15, so 15 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting days (8 - 12 is negative; 12 - 8 = 4 is wrong).

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A, B and C can finish a job alone in 10, 15 and 30 days respectively. How many days do they take working together?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); three-worker combinations are a standard textbook extension.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Adding three rates, preferably with the LCM method.
**Say this in the interview:** "Take the job as 30 units: A does 3, B does 2, C does 1 a day, 6 together, so 5 days."
**Answer:** **5 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM of 10, 15, 30 = 30, so let the job be 30 units.
2. A: 30/10 = 3 units/day; B: 30/15 = 2; C: 30/30 = 1.
3. Together: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 units/day.
4. Days = 30/6 = 5.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** The LCM trick above. Fraction form: 1/10 + 1/15 + 1/30 = (3 + 2 + 1)/30 = 6/30 = 1/5.
**Check:** In 5 days: A 1/2, B 1/3, C 1/6; 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Choosing the job size as the LCM makes every daily rate a whole number.
- The units are imaginary; only the ratio matters.
**Likely follow-ups:** If C leaves? A and B: 5 units/day, so 6 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 1/10 + 1/15 + 1/30 and then forgetting to invert.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A can do a job in 20 days and B in 30 days. They start together, but A leaves after 4 days and B finishes the rest alone. In how many days from the start is the job completed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the "one worker leaves" pattern is a standard variant (prep-site pattern, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting the work into phases and tracking the remaining fraction.
**Say this in the interview:** "Job = 60 units. Together they do 5 a day for 4 days = 20 units. 40 units left at 2 a day for B = 20 days. Total 24 days from the start."
**Answer:** **24 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Job = LCM(20, 30) = 60 units. A does 3 units/day, B does 2.
2. Together 5 units/day for 4 days = 20 units done.
3. Remaining = 60 - 20 = 40 units.
4. B alone at 2 units/day: 40/2 = 20 days.
5. Total = 4 + 20 = 24 days.
**Check:** A works 4 days: 12 units; B works 24 days: 48 units; 12 + 48 = 60. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Phase 1 (both working) and phase 2 (B alone) each use their own rate.
- Ask "from the start", so add the 4 days back; "after A leaves" would give 20.
**Likely follow-ups:** A leaves after 6 days? Done 30; left 30; B takes 15; total 21 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 20 (the days B works alone), forgetting the first 4 days.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A 120 m long train travelling at 72 km/h crosses a 280 m long platform. How many seconds does it take?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the platform length trap is described in Careers360 and Naukri Code360 trains material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Distance = train + platform length.
**Say this in the interview:** "72 km/h is 20 m/s. Distance is 120 + 280 = 400 m, so 400/20 = 20 seconds."
**Answer:** **20 seconds**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Speed = 72 x 5/18 = 20 m/s.
2. Distance to clear the platform = 120 + 280 = 400 m.
3. Time = 400/20 = 20 seconds.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 72 km/h = 20 m/s at once (72/18 x 5). 400/20 = 20.
**Check:** 20 m/s x 20 s = 400 m. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The train starts crossing when its front reaches the platform edge and finishes when its tail leaves the other edge. The front has travelled the platform plus the train's own length.
**Likely follow-ups:** Speed if it took 25 s? 400/25 = 16 m/s = 57.6 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using only 280 m (forgetting the train's length) giving 14 s.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Two trains of lengths 100 m and 140 m run on parallel tracks in opposite directions at 60 km/h and 48 km/h. How many seconds do they take to cross each other completely?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); opposite-direction crossing is a standard train pattern on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative speed = sum of speeds; distance = sum of lengths.
**Say this in the interview:** "Closing speed is 60 + 48 = 108 km/h = 30 m/s. Distance is 240 m, so 8 seconds."
**Answer:** **8 seconds**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Opposite directions: relative speed = 60 + 48 = 108 km/h.
2. In m/s: 108 x 5/18 = 30 m/s.
3. Distance = 100 + 140 = 240 m.
4. Time = 240/30 = 8 seconds.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 108 km/h is a neat 30 m/s; 240/30 = 8.
**Check:** In 8 s the trains close 30 x 8 = 240 m, exactly the two lengths. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Relative speed (how fast the gap between two moving things changes) is the sum of the speeds when they move in opposite directions, so the gap closes at that rate.
- "Cross completely" means front meets front until tail clears tail, i.e. both lengths.
**Likely follow-ups:** Same direction? Relative 12 km/h = 10/3 m/s, 240/(10/3) = 72 s.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting the speeds, or using only one train's length.

### Q17 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A boat's speed in still water is 12 km/h and the stream flows at 3 km/h. How long does it take to go 45 km downstream and come back the same 45 km upstream?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); boats and streams are a listed pattern in Naukri Code360 and Careers360 material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Downstream = boat + stream; upstream = boat - stream.
**Say this in the interview:** "Downstream 15 km/h gives 3 hours; upstream 9 km/h gives 5 hours; total 8 hours."
**Answer:** **8 hours**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Downstream speed = 12 + 3 = 15 km/h; time = 45/15 = 3 h.
2. Upstream speed = 12 - 3 = 9 km/h; time = 45/9 = 5 h.
3. Total = 3 + 5 = 8 hours.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Do each leg separately; no shortcut is needed. Equal distances, so the slower upstream leg takes the bigger share of time (5 h of 8).
**Check:** Round-trip distance 90 km over 8 h means average speed 11.25 km/h, below 12 as expected (the slower upstream leg takes longer). Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Downstream means moving with the current; upstream means against it. The stream helps going downstream and hinders going upstream.
- Still-water speed = (down + up)/2; stream speed = (down - up)/2.
**Likely follow-ups:** Stream 4 km/h? Down 16 (2.8125 h), up 8 (5.625 h); total 8.4375 h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 12 km/h for both legs (7.5 h), or averaging speeds.

### Q18 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A tank is filled by pipe A in 6 hours and emptied by outlet pipe B in 8 hours. If both are opened together on an empty tank, how many hours does it take to fill?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); outlet as "negative work" is described in GfG and Careers360 pipes material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Subtract the outlet's rate from the inlet's rate.
**Say this in the interview:** "Net rate = 1/6 - 1/8 = 1/24, so 24 hours."
**Answer:** **24 hours**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Inlet A: +1/6 of the tank per hour. Outlet B: -1/8 per hour.
2. Net = 1/6 - 1/8 = 4/24 - 3/24 = 1/24 per hour.
3. Time = 24 hours.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Take the tank as 24 litres: A adds 4 per hour, B removes 3, net +1 per hour, so 24 hours. Or ab/(b - a) = 48/2 = 24 (a = inlet hours, b = outlet hours, valid only when b > a).
**Check:** In 24 h A fills 4 tanks, B empties 3, net 1 tank. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- An outlet is a worker who undoes work, so its rate is negative.
- The tank only fills if the inlet is faster than the outlet; here 6 h is faster than 8 h.
**Likely follow-ups:** Outlet of 4 hours (faster than the inlet)? It never fills; it empties.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding the rates: 1/6 + 1/8 = 7/24 gives 24/7 = 3.43 h, which is wrong because the outlet drains the tank.

### Q19 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Two people start 90 km apart and travel towards each other at 25 km/h and 20 km/h. After how many hours do they meet?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); meeting-point items are a standard relative-speed pattern.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Closing speed is the sum of speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "They close the gap at 45 km/h, so 90/45 = 2 hours."
**Answer:** **2 hours**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Towards each other: relative speed = 25 + 20 = 45 km/h.
2. Time = gap / relative speed = 90/45 = 2 hours.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Time to meet = gap / (sum of speeds) = 90/45.
**Check:** In 2 h they cover 50 km and 40 km; 50 + 40 = 90. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Facing each other, the gap shrinks by both speeds each hour.
- Distances covered at the meeting point are in the ratio of their speeds (25:20 = 5:4).
**Likely follow-ups:** How far from the 25 km/h person's start? 50 km.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing by one speed, or subtracting the speeds.

### Q20 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A thief runs at 30 km/h. A police officer starts chasing from a point 20 km behind at 40 km/h in the same direction. After how many hours does the officer catch the thief?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); chase problems are standard relative-speed items.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Same direction means subtract speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "The officer gains 10 km every hour, and the gap is 20 km, so 2 hours."
**Answer:** **2 hours**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Same direction: relative speed = 40 - 30 = 10 km/h.
2. Gap to close = 20 km.
3. Time = 20/10 = 2 hours.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Time to catch = gap / (difference of speeds) = 20/10.
**Check:** In 2 h the officer runs 80 km and the thief 60 km; 60 + 20 head start = 80. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Chasing: the faster one eats the gap at the difference of speeds.
**Likely follow-ups:** Distance the officer covers? 40 x 2 = 80 km.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding the speeds (20/70).

### Q21 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A man walking at 5/6 of his usual speed reaches the office 12 minutes late. What is his usual time to reach the office?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the "usual speed" late-arrival item is standard on prep sites.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Speed and time are inversely proportional for a fixed distance.
**Say this in the interview:** "Speed 5/6 means time 6/5 of usual. The extra 1/5 of his usual time is 12 minutes, so usual time = 60 minutes."
**Answer:** **60 minutes (1 hour)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Fixed distance: new time = usual time x 6/5.
2. Extra time = 6/5 T - T = T/5.
3. T/5 = 12 minutes, so T = 60 minutes.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Speed ratio 5:6 means time ratio 6:5. The one-unit difference equals 12 minutes, so the usual time (5 units) = 5 x 12 = 60.
**Check:** 60 x 6/5 = 72 minutes, which is 12 more. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Slower speed = more time, in the exact flipped ratio.
- The "difference of ratio parts = the delay" trick finds the unit.
**Likely follow-ups:** At 4/5 speed, 15 minutes late? Time ratio 5:4, 1 unit = 15, usual = 4 x 15 = 60 minutes.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 72 (the new time) or treating 5/6 as a time ratio.

### Q22 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A is 3 times as fast as B. Together they finish a job in 12 days. How many days does A alone take?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); efficiency-ratio problems are a listed pattern (PlacementPreparation; unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Turning an efficiency ratio into a rate split.
**Say this in the interview:** "A:B efficiency is 3:1, so A does 3/4 of the work. A alone takes 12 x 4/3 = 16 days."
**Answer:** **16 days** (B alone takes 48 days)
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Efficiency A:B = 3:1, so together they have 4 parts.
2. Together they take 12 days, so job = 4 x 12 = 48 part-days.
3. A alone does 3 parts a day: 48/3 = 16 days.
4. B alone does 1 part a day: 48 days.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Together time x (total parts / A's parts) = 12 x 4/3 = 16.
**Check:** 1/16 + 1/48 = 3/48 + 1/48 = 4/48 = 1/12, so 12 days together. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- "3 times as fast" means 3 times the rate, so a third of the time.
- A does 3/4 of the combined work each day, so A alone needs the combined time divided by 3/4, that is 12 x 4/3 = 16 days.
**Likely follow-ups:** A twice as fast, together 12 days? 12 x 3/2 = 18 days for A.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 36 (12 x 3) or 4 (12/3).

### Q23 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A and B can finish a job in 6 and 12 days respectively. They work together and are paid Rs 9,000 in total, shared in proportion to the work each does. What is A's share?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); wage-split items are a gap-fill pattern in the Part 3 sources file.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know that pay follows work done, and that work done follows daily rate (efficiency).
**Say this in the interview:** "They work for the same number of days, so work done is in the ratio of their daily rates. A does 1/6 a day, B does 1/12, which is 2 : 1, so A gets 2/3 of Rs 9,000."
**Answer:** **A's share = Rs 6,000** (B gets Rs 3,000). Assumption: both work every day until the job is done.
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A's daily work = 1/6; B's daily work = 1/12 = 0.5/6.
2. Ratio of A : B = 1/6 : 1/12 = 2 : 1.
3. Total parts = 2 + 1 = 3.
4. A's share = 2/3 x 9,000 = Rs 6,000.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Pay is shared in the inverse ratio of the days taken (6 : 12 flips to 12 : 6 = 2 : 1). The person who finishes faster does more of the work in any shared time, so earns more.
**Check:** B = 1/3 x 9,000 = 3,000; 6,000 + 3,000 = 9,000. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Efficiency (work per day) = 1 / days taken.
- Both work the same number of days together, so the work each does is in the ratio of efficiencies.
- Money is split in that same ratio, like splitting a team bonus by contribution.
**Likely follow-ups:** If B had 18 days instead of 12, A : B = 18 : 6 = 3 : 1, so A gets Rs 6,750.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Splitting in the ratio of days (6 : 12 = 1 : 2) gives A Rs 3,000, which is the trap option. Faster worker must get more, not less.

### Q24 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Why is the average speed over two equal distances not the arithmetic mean of the two speeds? Which mean is correct, and when is the simple average correct?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a return-trip average-speed item style appears in a GfG Wipro mock item (practice site, unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand that average speed is total distance divided by total time, not the mean of the speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "Average speed is total distance over total time. Over equal distances you spend more time at the slower speed, so the slower speed counts more, and the correct mean is the harmonic mean, 2ab/(a+b). The simple average is right only when the times are equal."
**Answer:** **The harmonic mean, 2ab/(a+b), is correct for equal distances; the simple average (a+b)/2 is correct only for equal times.**
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed = total distance / total time. Always start from this.
- With equal distances, the slow leg takes longer, so you are "stuck" at the slow speed for more of the trip. That drags the average below the simple mean.
- Harmonic mean (a way of averaging that weights slow speeds more) for two speeds: 2ab / (a + b).
- With equal times (say 1 hour at a, then 1 hour at b), distances are a and b over 2 hours, so the average is (a + b)/2, the simple mean.
**Worked mini-example:** Go 60 km at 30 km/h and back 60 km at 60 km/h. Times: 2 h and 1 h, total 3 h for 120 km, so 40 km/h. The simple mean (45) is wrong. Formula: 2 x 30 x 60 / 90 = 40. Same answer.
**Likely follow-ups:** Drive 1 hour at 30 and 1 hour at 60: average = 45 km/h (equal times). For three equal distances: 3 / (1/a + 1/b + 1/c).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Picking the arithmetic mean (45) because it "looks right". For equal distances it is always larger than the true average when the speeds differ.

### Q25 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you solve a time-and-work problem quickly using the LCM of the days as the total units of work? Show the method on a job that A does in 12 days and B in 18 days.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the LCM/units method is standard prep content on time-and-work practice sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can avoid messy fractions by choosing a convenient size for the whole job.
**Say this in the interview:** "I take the LCM of the days as the total units of work. Then each person's daily output is a whole number, I add them, and divide the total by that. For 12 and 18, LCM is 36 units, A does 3 a day, B does 2, together 5, so 36/5 = 7.2 days."
**Answer:** **A and B together take 7.2 days (7 1/5 days).**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM (smallest number both 12 and 18 divide into) of 12 and 18 = 36. Call the whole job 36 units.
2. A's daily output = 36 / 12 = 3 units.
3. B's daily output = 36 / 18 = 2 units.
4. Together = 3 + 2 = 5 units per day.
5. Days = 36 / 5 = 7.2.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Same as above; it replaces 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36 with whole numbers. Any total works (36, 72, 360) because the answer is a ratio; the LCM just keeps numbers small.
**Check:** Fraction method: 1/12 + 1/18 = 3/36 + 2/36 = 5/36 per day, so 36/5 = 7.2 days. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The "units" are invented. We only need the total work to be a number both days divide into cleanly.
- Units per day = efficiency; the faster worker has the larger number.
- Works for part-time stints too: days worked x units per day = units done.
**Likely follow-ups:** A works 4 days alone first: 4 x 3 = 12 units done, 24 left, together 24/5 = 4.8 more days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 12 x 18 = 216 as the total also works but is slower; picking a total that one of the days does not divide into gives fractions and errors.

### Q26 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A can do a job in 24 days and B in 36 days. They work on alternate days, starting with A. In how many days is the job completed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); alternate-day work is listed as a variant in the sources file (2-star pattern, judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you handle a repeating cycle and the leftover work at the end, instead of dividing by an average.
**Say this in the interview:** "Take 72 units of work: A does 3 a day, B does 2. A two-day cycle gives 5 units. After 14 cycles (28 days) 70 units are done, and A finishes the last 2 units on day 29."
**Answer:** **29 days** (precisely 28 2/3 days, since A needs only 2/3 of the last day).
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM of 24 and 36 = 72 units.
2. A does 72 / 24 = 3 units a day; B does 72 / 36 = 2 units a day.
3. One cycle (A day + B day) = 5 units in 2 days.
4. 14 cycles = 28 days, 14 x 5 = 70 units done.
5. 2 units remain. Day 29 is A's turn and A does 3 units, so A needs 2/3 of a day.
6. Total = 28 + 2/3 days, counted as 29 days.
**Check:** After day 28: 14 x 3 + 14 x 2 = 70; 70 + 2 = 72. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Alternate-day problems repeat in a pattern; find the work done in one full cycle first.
- Divide total by cycle work to see how many full cycles fit (here 72 / 5 = 14.4, so 14 full cycles).
- Always check who works on the leftover day. Here it is A, who is faster.
**Likely follow-ups:** If B started first: after 28 days 70 units, B (2 per day) does the last 2 units on day 29, a full day, so 29 days exactly.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 72 / 5 = 14.4 cycles = 28.8 days and answering 29 by luck, or answering 28.8; or using the average of 24 and 36. The leftover day depends on who starts.

### Q27 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A 200 m long train passes a man running in the opposite direction at 6 km/h in 12 seconds. What is the speed of the train in km/h?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); trains with relative motion are reported as TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro topics on prep sites (counts unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative speed in opposite directions and the km/h to m/s conversion.
**Say this in the interview:** "The train covers its own length, 200 m, in 12 seconds relative to the man. That relative speed is 200/12 m/s, which is 60 km/h. Since he runs towards the train, the speeds add, so the train is 60 minus 6 = 54 km/h."
**Answer:** **54 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Distance covered = length of train = 200 m (the man is a point).
2. Relative speed = 200 / 12 = 16.67 m/s.
3. Convert to km/h: multiply by 18/5 (= 3.6): 16.67 x 3.6 = 60 km/h.
4. Opposite directions: train + man = 60, so train = 60 - 6 = 54 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** m/s to km/h is x 3.6. 200/12 x 3.6 = 60. Subtract the man's 6.
**Check:** Train 54 km/h = 15 m/s; man 6 km/h = 1.67 m/s; together 16.67 m/s; 200 / 16.67 = 12 s. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Opposite directions: speeds add. Same direction: speeds subtract.
- Passing a person or pole means covering only the train's own length.
- 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h because 3,600 seconds in an hour and 1,000 m in a km.
**Likely follow-ups:** If the man ran in the same direction at 6 km/h, relative speed 60 means train = 66 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding 6 instead of subtracting (66 km/h); forgetting to convert m/s to km/h (16.67); wrong direction logic.

### Q28 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A boat goes 30 km upstream and 44 km downstream in 10 hours. It goes 40 km upstream and 55 km downstream in 13 hours. Find the speed of the boat in still water and the speed of the stream.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); boats and streams is listed among TSD topics on prep sites.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Setting up two equations in the upstream and downstream speeds and solving cleanly.
**Say this in the interview:** "Let upstream speed be u and downstream speed be d. Two equations: 30/u + 44/d = 10 and 40/u + 55/d = 13. Solving gives u = 5 and d = 11, so still water is 8 km/h and stream is 3 km/h."
**Answer:** **Boat in still water = 8 km/h; stream = 3 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Upstream speed u = boat - stream; downstream speed d = boat + stream.
2. Equation 1: 30/u + 44/d = 10. Equation 2: 40/u + 55/d = 13.
3. Make the 1/u parts equal: multiply Eq 1 by 4 and Eq 2 by 3: 120/u + 176/d = 40 and 120/u + 165/d = 39.
4. Subtract: 11/d = 1, so d = 11 km/h.
5. Put back in Eq 1: 30/u + 44/11 = 10, so 30/u = 6, u = 5 km/h.
6. Still water = (11 + 5)/2 = 8 km/h; stream = (11 - 5)/2 = 3 km/h.
**Check:** 30/5 + 44/11 = 6 + 4 = 10; 40/5 + 55/11 = 8 + 5 = 13. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Stream helps you downstream (add) and slows you upstream (subtract).
- Still water speed = average of the two; stream = half the difference.
- Solving for time-like quantities (1/u, 1/d) is like solving any two simple equations.
**Likely follow-ups:** With stream 3 and boat 8, time for 55 km downstream = 55 / 11 = 5 hours.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 8 + 3 for upstream (swapping the signs); arithmetic slips when eliminating; forgetting that u and d are the unknowns, not the boat and stream.

### Q29 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 A and B together can do a job in 10 days, B and C in 15 days, and A and C in 12 days. How many days do all three take working together?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); no source reports this exact pattern.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Adding pair rates and halving, because each person is counted twice.
**Say this in the interview:** "Add the three pair rates: 1/10 + 1/15 + 1/12 = 1/4. That counts everyone twice, so A + B + C together is 1/8 per day, which means 8 days."
**Answer:** **8 days.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM of 10, 15, 12 = 60 units of work.
2. A + B = 60 / 10 = 6 units a day; B + C = 60 / 15 = 4; A + C = 60 / 12 = 5.
3. Add: 2(A + B + C) = 6 + 4 + 5 = 15, so A + B + C = 7.5 units a day.
4. Days = 60 / 7.5 = 8.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Add the three pair rates and halve: (1/10 + 1/15 + 1/12)/2 = (1/4)/2 = 1/8 per day, so 8 days.
**Check:** A = 7.5 - 4 = 3.5, B = 7.5 - 5 = 2.5, C = 7.5 - 6 = 1.5; A + B = 6, B + C = 4, A + C = 5, all match. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Each person appears in two of the three pairs, so adding pairs doubles the total; halve it.
- Once you have the three-person rate, subtract a pair to get the missing person alone.
**Likely follow-ups:** How long does C alone take? 60 / 1.5 = 40 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting to halve (giving 60/15 = 4 days); adding days 10 + 15 + 12.

### Q30 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Convert 25 m/s into km/h.

### Q31 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A tap fills a tank in 6 hours. Because of a leak at the bottom it takes 7 hours. How long would the leak alone take to empty the full tank?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a leak is the "negative work" variant of pipes and cisterns (Careers360, snippet).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Treating the leak as a negative rate.
**Say this in the interview:** "Tap fills 1/6 per hour, net rate with the leak is 1/7. The leak removes the difference: 1/6 minus 1/7 is 1/42 per hour, so 42 hours."
**Answer:** **42 hours.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Tap alone: 1/6 of the tank per hour.
2. With leak: 1/7 per hour.
3. Leak rate = 1/6 - 1/7 = 7/42 - 6/42 = 1/42 per hour.
4. Time to empty a full tank = 42 hours.

**Likely follow-ups:** Tap 4 h, with leak 5 h: leak alone = 20 h.

### Q33 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What are the formulas for relative speed when two objects move in the same direction and in opposite directions? Give one business-day example of each.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); relative motion is a reported TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro theme on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know when to subtract and when to add speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "Same direction: relative speed is the difference of the speeds. Opposite directions: it is the sum. Relative speed is how fast the gap between them changes."
**Answer:** **Same direction: S1 - S2 (larger minus smaller). Opposite directions: S1 + S2.**
**Explain simply:**
- Relative speed means the speed at which the distance between two moving things shrinks or grows.
- Same direction: the faster one gains only the extra speed, so subtract.
- Opposite direction: they close in from both sides, so add.
**Worked mini-example:**
- Same direction: a delivery bike at 30 km/h is 10 km behind a courier van doing 20 km/h on the same road. Closing speed is 30 - 20 = 10 km/h, so it catches up in 1 hour.
- Opposite direction: an HR manager leaves the office at 40 km/h and a candidate leaves the venue 120 km away at 20 km/h, heading towards each other. Closing speed 60 km/h, so they meet in 120 / 60 = 2 hours.
**Likely follow-ups:** For trains, add the lengths when crossing each other; for a pole or a person, use only the train's length.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting for opposite directions or adding for the same direction; using the wrong distance (gap vs. lengths).

### Q35 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A boat goes 20 km downstream in 2 hours and returns the same 20 km upstream in 4 hours. Find the speed of the boat in still water and of the stream.

### Q37 ⭐⭐ 🟢 In Excel you have a delivery log with distance and time for each trip. How would you compute the correct average speed for all trips, and why is AVERAGE of the speed column wrong?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a gap-fill question for the analyst audience, not from a source.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you apply "total distance / total time" in a spreadsheet and know why a plain average misleads.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would use =SUM(distance)/SUM(time), which is total distance over total time. AVERAGE of the speed column treats a 5-minute trip and a 5-hour trip as equal, so it is wrong."
**Answer:** **=SUM(Distance_range) / SUM(Time_range)** (with time in hours).
**Explain simply:**
- Each trip's speed is distance / time. A plain average gives every trip the same weight, whatever its length or duration.
- The correct overall speed is a weighted average: the weights are the times.
- If distances are equal, it matches the harmonic mean; if times are equal, it matches the simple average.
**Worked mini-example:** Trip 1: 60 km in 2 h (30 km/h). Trip 2: 60 km in 1 h (60 km/h). AVERAGE of speeds = 45. Correct: 120 / 3 = 40 km/h.
Excel: if distance is in B2:B3 and time (hours) in C2:C3, use `=SUM(B2:B3)/SUM(C2:C3)` for 40. Alternative: `=SUMPRODUCT(speed_range, time_range)/SUM(time_range)` gives the same. These Excel formulas were not run in Excel here; the arithmetic (40 km/h) was checked in code.
**Likely follow-ups:** If time is in minutes? Divide by 60 first, or multiply the result by 60 for km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using AVERAGE on the speed column; mixing minutes with hours.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A can do a job in 20 days and B in 30 days. A works alone for 5 days and then B joins. How many days from the start does the job take to finish?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); partial-work variants are a 2-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting the work into a solo stage and a joint stage.
**Say this in the interview:** "A does a quarter in 5 days. Three quarters remain, and together they do 1/12 per day, so 9 more days. Total 14 days."
**Answer:** **14 days from the start.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. LCM of 20 and 30 = 60 units. A = 3 units a day, B = 2 units a day.
2. A alone for 5 days = 15 units. Remaining = 60 - 15 = 45 units.
3. Together = 5 units a day. 45 / 5 = 9 days.
4. Total = 5 + 9 = 14 days.
**Check:** 5 x 3 + 9 x 5 = 15 + 45 = 60. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Do the solo days first, subtract the work, then use the combined rate for the rest.
- Remember the answer is counted from the start, so add the solo days back.
**Likely follow-ups:** If A works only 3 days before B joins: 60 - 9 = 51, 51 / 5 = 10.2 days, total 13.2 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 9 (forgetting the 5 solo days).

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟡 12 workers can complete a project in 15 days. After 5 days, 6 workers leave the project. How many more days are needed to finish the remaining work?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "workers leaving" is a 2-star variant in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Man-days (workers x days) as the measure of work.
**Say this in the interview:** "The project is 12 x 15 = 180 worker-days. 5 days done is 60, so 120 remain. With 6 workers that is 20 more days."
**Answer:** **20 more days** (25 days in total). Assumption: all workers are equally productive.
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Total work = 12 x 15 = 180 worker-days.
2. Done in 5 days = 12 x 5 = 60 worker-days.
3. Remaining = 180 - 60 = 120 worker-days.
4. Workers left = 12 - 6 = 6. Days = 120 / 6 = 20.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Remaining 10 days at full strength; half the team means double the time: 10 x 2 = 20.
**Check:** 60 + 6 x 20 = 180. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A worker-day (man-day) is one worker working one day. It is the total effort of the project.
- More workers, fewer days: they are inversely linked.
**Likely follow-ups:** If 6 more workers joined after 5 days (18 workers): 120 / 18 = 6.67 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 25 (total, not "more days"); computing with 15 days left instead of 10.

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A is 25% more efficient than B. If B alone takes 20 days to do a job, how many days does A alone take?

3. A's time = 80 / 5 = 16 days.

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A team of 10 analysts clears 500 tickets in 8 days working 8 hours a day. How many days would 8 analysts need to clear 500 tickets working 10 hours a day?

### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A walks at 4 km/h. Two hours later B starts from the same point on the same route at 6 km/h. How many hours after B starts does B catch up with A?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); chase problems are a 3-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Head-start gap divided by relative speed.
**Say this in the interview:** "A has an 8 km head start. B gains 6 minus 4 = 2 km each hour, so 8 / 2 = 4 hours after B starts."
**Answer:** **4 hours after B starts.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Head start = 4 km/h x 2 h = 8 km.
2. Same direction: closing speed = 6 - 4 = 2 km/h.
3. Time = 8 / 2 = 4 hours.
**Check:** B walks 6 x 4 = 24 km; A walks 4 x 6 = 24 km (2 + 4 hours). Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The faster person gains only the difference in speed each hour.
- Time to catch up = gap / relative speed.
**Likely follow-ups:** At what distance do they meet? 24 km from the start.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using A's total 6 hours as the answer; the question asks for time after B starts.

### Q43 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A courier covers three equal distances at 20 km/h, 30 km/h and 60 km/h respectively. What is the average speed for the whole journey?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); average-speed items appear in Wipro-style practice sets (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** The harmonic mean for equal distances, extended to three legs.
**Say this in the interview:** "Take each leg as 60 km: times are 3, 2 and 1 hours. Total 180 km in 6 hours is 30 km/h. The simple average, 36.7, would be wrong."
**Answer:** **30 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let each leg be 60 km (any convenient common multiple).
2. Times: 60/20 = 3 h; 60/30 = 2 h; 60/60 = 1 h. Total = 6 h.
3. Total distance = 3 x 60 = 180 km.
4. Average speed = 180 / 6 = 30 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 3 / (1/20 + 1/30 + 1/60) = 3 / (6/60) = 30.
**Check:** Simple mean (110/3 = 36.7) is higher, as expected, since slower legs take more time. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed is total distance / total time; equal distances mean time is weighted towards the slow legs.
- For n equal legs: harmonic mean = n / (sum of 1/speeds).
**Likely follow-ups:** Equal times at 20, 30, 60 would give a simple average of 36.7 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using (20 + 30 + 60) / 3 = 36.7.

### Q44 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A 200 m long train takes 25 seconds to cross a 300 m long bridge. What is the speed of the train in km/h?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); train and platform items are reported TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro topics, and the platform-length trap is noted on Careers360 and Naukri Code360 (unverified counts).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That crossing a bridge means covering train length plus bridge length.

**Answer:** **72 km/h.**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Distance = 200 + 300 = 500 m (front of the train enters the bridge until the rear leaves it).
2. Speed = 500 / 25 = 20 m/s.
3. Convert: 20 x 3.6 = 72 km/h.
**Check:** 72 km/h = 20 m/s; 500 / 20 = 25 s. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Crossing a pole or a person: only the train's length. Crossing a bridge or platform: train + bridge.

**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 300 m alone (43.2 km/h) or forgetting the unit conversion (20).

### Q45 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Two trains, each 150 m long, run in the same direction at 54 km/h and 36 km/h. How many seconds does the faster train take to overtake the slower one completely?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites (unverified) list train-overtaking items in TCS NQT, Cognizant and Wipro style tests.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative speed in the same direction, adding both train lengths, and km/h to m/s conversion.
**Say this in the interview:** "Same direction, so I subtract the speeds: 18 km/h, which is 5 m/s. The faster train must cover both lengths, 300 m, so 300 divided by 5 is 60 seconds."
**Answer:** **60 seconds**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Relative speed (same direction) = 54 - 36 = 18 km/h.
2. Convert to m/s: 18 x 5/18 = 5 m/s.
3. To overtake "completely", the front of the fast train must go from the tail of the slow train to the front of it, then the tail must clear it too. Distance = 150 + 150 = 300 m.
4. Time = 300 / 5 = 60 seconds.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** km/h to m/s means multiply by 5/18. So 18 km/h is exactly 5 m/s, and 300 / 5 = 60.
**Check:** In 60 s the fast train covers 15 x 60 = 900 m and the slow one 10 x 60 = 600 m; the gap gained is 300 m. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Relative speed is how fast one thing closes the gap on another. Same direction: subtract. Opposite directions: add.
- A train has length, so "crossing" a train means covering both lengths, not just one.
- To turn km/h into m/s, multiply by 5/18 (1 km = 1000 m, 1 hour = 3600 s, and 1000/3600 = 5/18).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Same trains, opposite directions?* Relative speed 90 km/h = 25 m/s, so 300 / 25 = 12 s. 2) *Only one train's length counted?* Wrong; both lengths must be covered.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding the speeds (that gives the opposite-direction case, 12 s); using only 150 m (gives 30 s); forgetting to convert km/h to m/s.

### Q46 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A boat's speed in still water is 10 km/h and the stream speed is 2 km/h. For the same distance, the boat takes 3 hours more going upstream than downstream. What is the distance?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); boats and streams is a standard topic in prep-site syllabi (unverified for specific companies).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Downstream and upstream speeds, and setting up a time-difference equation.
**Say this in the interview:** "Downstream speed is 12, upstream is 8. Distance over 8 minus distance over 12 equals 3, so distance times 1/24 equals 3, giving 72 km."
**Answer:** **72 km**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Downstream speed = 10 + 2 = 12 km/h (the stream helps).
2. Upstream speed = 10 - 2 = 8 km/h (the stream hurts).
3. Let the distance be d. Time upstream - time downstream = 3, so d/8 - d/12 = 3.
4. d/8 - d/12 = (3d - 2d)/24 = d/24.
5. d/24 = 3, so d = 72 km.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** The difference between 1/8 and 1/12 is 1/24, so the distance is 3 x 24 = 72.
**Check:** Upstream 72/8 = 9 h; downstream 72/12 = 6 h; difference 3 h. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Still-water speed is the boat's own speed with no current. The stream adds to it going with the flow and subtracts going against it.
- Time = distance / speed, so a time gap turns into a simple equation in d.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Same boat, gap of 5 hours?* d = 5 x 24 = 120 km. 2) *What is the average speed for a round trip?* 2 x 12 x 8 / 20 = 9.6 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Swapping upstream and downstream; using 10 km/h for both legs; subtracting speeds instead of times.

### Q47 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Pipe A fills a tank in 12 hours and pipe B in 16 hours. Outlet pipe C empties the full tank in 24 hours. If all three are opened together on an empty tank, how many hours does it take to fill?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); Infosys-branded practice pages (prep sites, unverified) treat pipes as a time-and-work variant.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Adding rates, and treating the outlet as negative work.
**Say this in the interview:** "Per hour A fills 1/12 and B 1/16, and C removes 1/24. Net is 5/48 per hour, so the tank fills in 48/5, which is 9.6 hours."
**Answer:** **9.6 hours (9 hours 36 minutes)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Take the whole tank as 1 job. A's rate = 1/12, B's rate = 1/16, C's rate = -1/24 (it removes water).
2. Use the common denominator 48: A = 4/48, B = 3/48, C = -2/48.
3. Net rate = (4 + 3 - 2)/48 = 5/48 of the tank per hour.
4. Time = 1 / (5/48) = 48/5 = 9.6 hours. 0.6 hour = 36 minutes.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Pick a tank size that all times divide into: 48 litres. A fills 4 L/h, B 3 L/h, C removes 2 L/h. Net 5 L/h, so 48 / 5 = 9.6 h.
**Check:** In 9.6 h: A 9.6/12 = 0.8, B 9.6/16 = 0.6, C 9.6/24 = 0.4; 0.8 + 0.6 - 0.4 = 1.0. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A rate is "part of the job done in one hour". Rates of things working together add up.
- An outlet works against you, so its rate is subtracted.
- Time = 1 divided by the combined rate.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If C alone were opened on a full tank?* 24 hours. 2) *Make C 48 hours?* Net = (4+3-1)/48 = 6/48, so 8 hours.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding C's rate instead of subtracting; adding the times (12 + 16 - 24); stopping at "9.6" and writing 9 h 60 min.

### Q48 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Pipe A fills a tank in 10 hours and pipe B in 15 hours. They are opened alternately for one hour each, starting with A. In how many hours is the tank full?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); alternating-pipe cases are a 2-star variant in the sources file (judgement, not measured frequency).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Working in cycles and handling the last partial cycle carefully.
**Say this in the interview:** "A and B together in a two-hour cycle fill 1/10 + 1/15 = 1/6. After five cycles (10 hours) it is 5/6 full. Hour 11 is A's, adding 1/10; the last 1/15 is exactly B's one hour. So 12 hours."
**Answer:** **12 hours**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. A fills 1/10 per hour, B fills 1/15 per hour.
2. One two-hour cycle (A then B) fills 1/10 + 1/15 = 3/30 + 2/30 = 5/30 = 1/6.
3. After 5 cycles (10 hours): 5/6 is full; 1/6 is left.
4. Hour 11 is A's turn: adds 1/10 = 3/30. Filled = 25/30 + 3/30 = 28/30; left = 2/30 = 1/15.
5. Hour 12 is B's turn: B adds exactly 1/15 in one hour. The tank is full at the end of hour 12.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Use 30 litres as the tank: A = 3 L/h, B = 2 L/h. A cycle gives 5 L. After 10 h it holds 25 L; hour 11 adds 3 (28 L); hour 12 adds 2 (30 L).
**Check:** 6 A-hours x 3 + 6 B-hours x 2 = 18 + 12 = 30 L in 12 h. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- When pipes take turns you cannot just add rates; you add per cycle, then look at the leftover.
- Do not simply divide 1 by the cycle rate: here it happens to give 12 h, but in general the tank can fill part-way through a cycle, so always check the leftover.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Start with B instead?* Hours 1-10 same (5/6); hour 11 B adds 1/15, leaving 1/10; hour 12 A adds 1/10. Still 12 hours. 2) *Pipes of 10 h and 20 h, alternating from A?* Cycle = 1/10 + 1/20 = 3/20; 6 cycles (12 h) = 18/20; then A adds 2/20 in hour 13: full in 13 hours.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 6 hours (treating both as open together: 1/(1/6)); or answering 12 by shortcut without checking the partial cycle.

### Q49 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A car going at 40 km/h reaches its destination 15 minutes late. Going at 50 km/h it reaches 6 minutes early. What is the distance?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); late/early distance is a 2-star pattern in the sources file (judgement, not measured frequency).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Turning two scenarios into one time-difference equation and handling minutes versus hours.
**Say this in the interview:** "The two trips differ by 21 minutes, which is 0.35 hours. Distance over 40 minus distance over 50 equals 0.35, so distance times 1/200 equals 0.35, which gives 70 km."
**Answer:** **70 km**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Slow trip arrives 15 min late, fast trip 6 min early, so the trips differ by 15 + 6 = 21 minutes = 21/60 = 0.35 hours.
2. Let the distance be d. d/40 - d/50 = 0.35.
3. d/40 - d/50 = (5d - 4d)/200 = d/200.
4. d/200 = 0.35, so d = 70 km.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Distance = (speed1 x speed2 / speed difference) x time gap = (40 x 50 / 10) x 0.35 = 200 x 0.35 = 70. It works because d x (1/s1 - 1/s2) equals the gap.
**Check:** 70/40 = 1.75 h; 70/50 = 1.4 h; difference 0.35 h = 21 min. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Both trips cover the same distance against the same scheduled time, so the difference in actual times equals the total of the "late" and "early" gaps.
- Always convert minutes to hours before dividing by km/h.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What is the scheduled time?* 70/40 = 1.75 h minus 0.25 h = 1.5 h (check: 70/50 = 1.4 h plus 0.1 h = 1.5 h). 2) *Both trips late, by 15 and 6 minutes?* The gap is then 9 minutes = 0.15 h, so d = 200 x 0.15 = 30 km.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 15 - 6 = 9 minutes (wrong for late versus early); leaving minutes unconverted.

### Q50 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A support team clears 90 tickets per day with team A and 60 tickets per day with team B. The backlog is 1,200 tickets and 30 new tickets arrive each day. If both teams work, in how many days is the backlog cleared?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); this is a gap-fill item (backlog flavour) added from standard practice, not from a source.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Net rate thinking: work capacity minus inflow, the same logic as an outlet pipe.
**Say this in the interview:** "Both teams clear 150 a day, but 30 new ones arrive, so the backlog shrinks by 120 a day. 1,200 divided by 120 is 10 days."
**Answer:** **10 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Clearing rate = 90 + 60 = 150 tickets per day.
2. New arrivals = 30 per day (they add to the pile).
3. Net reduction = 150 - 30 = 120 tickets per day.
4. Days = 1,200 / 120 = 10.
**Check:** After 10 days: 1,500 cleared, 300 new arrived; 1,200 + 300 - 1,500 = 0. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- The incoming tickets act like a leak working against you, so subtract them from your clearing rate.
- Assumption: the 1,200 is the backlog at the start and arrivals are steady.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Only team A works?* Net 90 - 30 = 60/day, so 20 days. 2) *Arrivals rise to 150/day?* Net 0: the backlog never clears.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing 1,200 by 150 (8 days), forgetting the inflow.

### Q51 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A train takes 9 seconds to cross a pole and 24 seconds to cross a 300 m long platform at the same constant speed. What is the length of the train?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the pole versus platform pattern is a staple of train problems in prep sources (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Knowing that crossing a pole covers only the train's length, while a platform adds its length.
**Say this in the interview:** "The extra 15 seconds is the time to run the 300 m platform, so speed is 20 m/s. In 9 seconds that is 180 m, the train's length."

1. Crossing the pole: distance = train length L, time 9 s, so speed = L/9.
2. Crossing the platform: distance = L + 300, time 24 s.
3. The extra time is 24 - 9 = 15 s, which covers exactly the extra 300 m. Speed = 300 / 15 = 20 m/s.
4. L = 20 x 9 = 180 m.

### Q52 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A man alone can do a job in 24 days and a woman alone in 36 days. In how many days can 6 men and 6 women together do it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); man-days and group-work items are listed in time-and-work prep material (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Rates for individuals, then scaling to a group.
**Say this in the interview:** "A man does 1/24 a day and a woman 1/36. Together one pair does 5/72. Six pairs do 30/72, which is 5/12 a day, so 12/5, or 2.4 days."
**Answer:** **2.4 days (2 and 2/5 days)**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. One man's rate = 1/24 per day; one woman's rate = 1/36 per day.
2. One man plus one woman = 1/24 + 1/36 = 3/72 + 2/72 = 5/72.
3. Six men and six women = 6 x 5/72 = 30/72 = 5/12 of the job per day.
4. Days = 1 / (5/12) = 12/5 = 2.4.

**Check:** 2.4 days x 30 units = 72 units. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Rates (job per day) add for people working together, and a group of identical workers multiplies the rate.
- Picking a total of 72 units (a common multiple of 24 and 36) removes fractions.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many days for 3 men and 4 women?* 3x3 + 4x2 = 17 units/day; 72/17 ≈ 4.24 days. 2) *Women only (6)?* 12 units/day, so 6 days.

### Q53 ⭐⭐ 🟡 If a person increases speed by 25%, by what percentage does the travel time over the same distance fall?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); speed-up versus time-saved is a classic inverse-proportion item.
**What the interviewer is checking:** That time and speed are inversely proportional, and that percentage drops are not symmetric.
**Say this in the interview:** "Speed goes from 4 to 5, so time goes from 1/4 to 1/5 of the distance. The drop is 1/20 over 1/4, which is 20%, not 25%."
**Answer:** **20%**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let the original speed be 100 and the distance be 100. Time = 100/100 = 1.
2. New speed = 125. New time = 100/125 = 0.8.
3. Fall in time = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2, which is 20% of the original 1.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** If speed rises by x/y, time falls by x/(x + y). Here 25% = 1/4, so the fall is 1/(1+4) = 1/5 = 20%.
**Check:** 20% fall means 0.8 of the time; 1 / 0.8 = 1.25, which is 25% faster. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Speed x time = distance. With the distance fixed, if speed goes up, time must come down in the same ratio (inverse proportion).
- The base differs: the 25% rise is on the old speed, the fall is on the old time, so the two percentages are not equal.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Speed cut by 20%, time rises by?* 25%. 2) *Speed up 50%?* Time falls 1/3 = 33.3%.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 25% (assuming symmetry).

### Q54 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A vehicle travels from P to Q at 50 km/h. At what speed must it return so that the average speed for the round trip is 60 km/h?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a GfG Wipro mock item in this style is noted in the sources (snippet only).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Average speed is total distance over total time (harmonic), not the simple mean of speeds.
**Say this in the interview:** "Average speed of 60 for equal distances means 2 x 50 x x / (50 + x) = 60. That gives x = 75 km/h."
**Answer:** **75 km/h**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. For equal distances d each way, average speed = 2 x s1 x s2 / (s1 + s2).
2. 2 x 50 x x / (50 + x) = 60, so 100x = 60(50 + x) = 3000 + 60x.
3. 40x = 3000, so x = 75 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Take d = 150 km one way. Total distance 300 km at 60 km/h needs 5 h. Going took 150/50 = 3 h, so the return must take 2 h, i.e. 150/2 = 75 km/h.
**Check:** 2 x 50 x 75 / 125 = 7500 / 125 = 60. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Average speed = total distance / total time. Since you spend more time at the slow speed, the average is pulled below the simple mean (62.5 here would be wrong).
- Choosing a convenient distance makes the time budget obvious.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Target average 100 km/h?* Impossible: 2d/100 = d/50 already uses all the time, leaving none for the return. 2) *Average for 50 and 75?* 60 km/h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using the simple mean (70 km/h) as the answer: (50 + x)/2 = 60 gives x = 70, which is wrong.

### Q55 ⭐⭐ 🔴 A and B together can do a job in 8 days. They work together for 3 days, after which B leaves and A finishes the remaining work in 15 more days. How many days would each take alone?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); "worker leaves" variants are 1-2 star patterns in the sources file (judgement, not measured frequency).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Splitting work into phases and solving for an unknown rate.
**Say this in the interview:** "In 3 days together they finish 3/8, leaving 5/8. A does that in 15 days, so A does 1/24 a day, 24 days alone. B is 1/8 minus 1/24, which is 1/12, 12 days alone."
**Answer:** **A alone: 24 days; B alone: 12 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Together rate = 1/8 per day. Work in 3 days = 3 x 1/8 = 3/8.
2. Remaining = 1 - 3/8 = 5/8.
3. A does 5/8 in 15 days, so A's rate = (5/8) / 15 = 5/120 = 1/24 per day. A alone takes 24 days.
4. B's rate = 1/8 - 1/24 = 3/24 - 1/24 = 2/24 = 1/12. B alone takes 12 days.
**Check:** A + B = 1/24 + 1/12 = 3/24 = 1/8. And 3/8 + 15/24 = 3/8 + 5/8 = 1. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Split the job into "together" and "A alone" phases; the parts must add up to 1 whole job.
- Once you know the combined rate and one person's rate, the other is the difference.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *If A leaves instead?* B needs 5/8 / (1/12) = 7.5 days. 2) *Check with A alone?* A at 1/24 per day covers 15/24 = 5/8 in 15 days, as required.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking A took 15 + 3 = 18 days in total as a "solo" figure; dividing 1 by 15 instead of 5/8 by 15.

### Q56 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Two trains start at the same time from stations A and B towards each other. After meeting, the train from A reaches B in 4 hours and the train from B reaches A in 9 hours. What is the ratio of the speed of the train from A to the speed of the train from B?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); speed-ratio-after-meeting is a 2-star pattern in the sources file (judgement, not measured frequency).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know the square-root rule for speeds after a meeting, and why it works.
**Say this in the interview:** "The ratio of speeds is the square root of the ratio of the times taken after meeting, flipped: speed of A to speed of B equals root of 9 over 4, which is 3 to 2."
**Answer:** **3 : 2**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Let the trains meet after t hours. Speeds are a (train A) and b (train B).
2. After meeting, B covers the stretch A covered before meeting, so b x 9 = a x t.
3. Likewise A covers the stretch B covered before meeting, so a x 4 = b x t.
4. Divide the first equation by the second: (b x 9)/(a x 4) = (a x t)/(b x t), so 9b / 4a = a / b.
5. So 9b^2 = 4a^2, so (a/b)^2 = 9/4, so a/b = 3/2.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Speed of A : speed of B = square root of (time B takes after meeting) : square root of (time A takes after meeting) = root 9 : root 4 = 3 : 2. The train that takes longer after meeting (B, 9 h) is the slower one, which fits 3 : 2.
**Check:** With a = 3, b = 2: meeting time t from 4a = bt gives t = 6. Distances: A covers 18 before meeting, B covers 12. After meeting A covers 12 at 3 km/h = 4 h; B covers 18 at 2 km/h = 9 h. Matches. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- After the meeting each train covers the stretch the other one covered before the meeting, so the times and speeds are linked in a loop.
- Both trains started together and met together, so each train's pre-meeting time is the same t.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How long until they meet?* 6 hours, in the check above (t = root of 4 x 9). 2) *Post-meeting times 16 h and 25 h?* Ratio = 5 : 4 (speed of A over B = root 25 : root 16).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Giving 4 : 9 or 9 : 4 (forgetting the square root); flipping the order so the answer is 2 : 3.

### Q57 ⭐⭐ 🔴 A man rows 30 km upstream and 30 km downstream in a total of 8 hours. If the stream speed is 2 km/h, what is his speed in still water?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a standard boats and streams equation item (unverified for specific companies).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Setting up a total-time equation and solving a quadratic, or testing answer options smartly.
**Say this in the interview:** "Let still-water speed be x. 30/(x+2) + 30/(x-2) = 8. That reduces to 2x squared minus 15x minus 8 equals 0, so x = 8 km/h."
**Answer:** **8 km/h**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Downstream speed = x + 2, upstream speed = x - 2.
2. Time equation: 30/(x+2) + 30/(x-2) = 8.
3. Combine: 30 x (x-2 + x+2) / (x^2 - 4) = 60x / (x^2 - 4) = 8.
4. 60x = 8x^2 - 32, so 8x^2 - 60x - 32 = 0, which is 2x^2 - 15x - 8 = 0.
5. Factor: (2x + 1)(x - 8) = 0. So x = 8 (the other root, -0.5, is impossible).
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Guess and test. Try 8: downstream 10 km/h gives 3 h, upstream 6 km/h gives 5 h, total 8 h. Done.
**Check:** 30/10 + 30/6 = 3 + 5 = 8 hours. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- A quadratic equation can have two roots; reject the one that makes no physical sense (a negative speed).
- In an exam with options, plugging in a number is often faster than solving.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Still-water speed 10 km/h, stream 2, same 30 km each way?* 30/12 + 30/8 = 2.5 + 3.75 = 6.25 hours. 2) *Time each way here?* 3 h downstream, 5 h upstream.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Using 8/2 = 4 h each way; forgetting the upstream speed has to stay positive.

### Q58 ⭐ 🟢 Why do pipes-and-cisterns problems reduce to time-and-work problems, and how do you treat an outlet or leak?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); GfG and Careers360 pages (snippets) describe an outlet as negative work.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you see the common structure behind topics that look different.
**Say this in the interview:** "A pipe filling a tank is a worker doing a job: a tank is one job, the time is how long that pipe alone takes. Fill pipes add their rates; an outlet or leak is negative work, so I subtract its rate."
**Answer:** **Filling a tank is the same as finishing a job: add the per-hour rates of inlet pipes, subtract the per-hour rates of outlets or leaks, then take 1 divided by the net rate.**
**Explain simply:**
- Work = tank filled (1 whole). A person's "days to finish the job" becomes a pipe's "hours to fill the tank".
- Rate = 1 / time. A pipe that fills in 12 h has a rate of 1/12 tank per hour.
- Inlets add; outlets and leaks subtract. If the net rate is negative, the tank is emptying, not filling.
- A leak is simply an outlet nobody opened on purpose.
**Worked mini-example:** Pipe A fills a tank in 6 h; a leak empties it in 12 h. Net rate = 1/6 - 1/12 = 1/12. The tank takes 12 hours to fill with the leak, instead of 6 h. (HR flavour: new hires per month versus attrition per month; net growth = hires minus leavers.)
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Pipe takes 5 h to fill, but 6 h with a leak; how long does the leak take to empty a full tank?* 1/5 - 1/6 = 1/30, so 30 h. 2) *Two pipes alternate?* Add rates per cycle, then handle the leftover (see Q48).
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding the outlet's rate; adding times instead of rates; forgetting that the leak keeps working even when the pipe is shut.

### Q59 ⭐ 🟡 A, B and C can finish a job in 6, 8 and 12 days respectively. They work together and are paid Rs 5,400 in total in proportion to the work done. What is C's share?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); wage-split items are a gap-fill added from standard practice (payroll flavour), not from a source.
**What the interviewer is checking:** That pay follows the work done, which follows rate, not time taken.
**Say this in the interview:** "Their rates are in the ratio 1/6 : 1/8 : 1/12, which is 4 : 3 : 2. C gets 2 out of 9 parts of 5,400, which is Rs 1,200."
**Answer:** **Rs 1,200**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Rates per day: A = 1/6, B = 1/8, C = 1/12.
2. Convert to units of 1/24: A = 4/24, B = 3/24, C = 2/24. Ratio of work = 4 : 3 : 2.
3. Total parts = 4 + 3 + 2 = 9. One part = 5,400 / 9 = Rs 600.
4. C's share = 2 x 600 = Rs 1,200. (A gets 2,400, B gets 1,800.)
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Share is inversely proportional to days taken. Use the LCM of 6, 8, 12, which is 24: 24/6 = 4, 24/8 = 3, 24/12 = 2.
**Check:** 2,400 + 1,800 + 1,200 = 5,400. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- All three work for the same number of days together, so work done is proportional to speed.
- The faster worker (fewer days) earns more, so the share is the inverse of days.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *A works 2 days only, B and C all days?* Needs the total job time; share by work done = rate x days worked. 2) *Total pay Rs 9,000?* One part = 1,000, so C gets Rs 2,000.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Splitting in the ratio 6 : 8 : 12 (the wrong way round), which gives C the largest share.

### Q60 ⭐ 🟡 A boat has speed 8 km/h in still water and the stream flows at 2 km/h. What is its average speed for a round trip over the same distance?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a classic average-speed twist on boats and streams.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Average speed is total distance over total time, not the average of 10 and 6.
**Say this in the interview:** "Downstream is 10 and upstream is 6. For equal distances the average is 2 x 10 x 6 / 16, which is 7.5 km/h."
**Answer:** **7.5 km/h**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Downstream speed = 8 + 2 = 10 km/h. Upstream speed = 8 - 2 = 6 km/h.
2. Pick a convenient distance each way, say 30 km: downstream time = 3 h, upstream time = 5 h.
3. Total distance = 60 km; total time = 8 h. Average = 60 / 8 = 7.5 km/h.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** 2 x 10 x 6 / (10 + 6) = 120 / 16 = 7.5.
**Check:** The simple mean 8 is wrong because more time is spent going slowly upstream; 7.5 is below 8, as expected. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Spending more time at the slow speed drags the average below the still-water speed. The stream always costs you on a round trip.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Stream 4 km/h?* Speeds 12 and 4; 2 x 12 x 4 / 16 = 6 km/h. 2) *Why is it less than 8?* Time lost upstream exceeds time saved downstream.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 8 (the simple mean of 10 and 6).

### Q61 ⭐ 🟡 A man can walk both ways in 9 hours. He can ride one way and walk back in 6 hours. How long does he take to ride both ways?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the walk/ride puzzle is a 1-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Breaking the trip into one-way times and subtracting.
**Say this in the interview:** "Walking both ways is 9 hours, so one way is 4.5. Riding one way and walking back is 6, so riding one way is 1.5. Riding both ways is 3 hours."
**Answer:** **3 hours**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Walk one way = 9 / 2 = 4.5 hours.
2. Ride one way + walk one way = 6 hours, so ride one way = 6 - 4.5 = 1.5 hours.
3. Ride both ways = 2 x 1.5 = 3 hours.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Ride both ways = 2 x (ride + walk) - (walk both ways) = 12 - 9 = 3 hours.
**Check:** Walk twice 9 h; ride once + walk once = 1.5 + 4.5 = 6 h; ride twice 3 h. Consistent. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- Each trip is two one-way legs. Treat "walk leg" and "ride leg" as two unknowns and use the two facts.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How many times faster is riding?* 4.5 / 1.5 = 3 times. 2) *Walk both ways 12 h, ride + walk 8 h?* Walk leg 6, ride leg 2, ride both 4 h.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Answering 6 hours (that is the mixed trip, not ride both ways) or 1.5 hours (one way only).

### Q62 ⭐ 🟡 Two runners start together on a circular track of 400 m, running in the same direction at 5 m/s and 3 m/s. After how many seconds does the faster runner first lap (catch up with) the slower one?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); circular track is a 1-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Relative speed with a lap to gain.
**Say this in the interview:** "The faster runner gains 2 metres every second. To gain one full lap of 400 m takes 400 / 2 = 200 seconds."
**Answer:** **200 seconds**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Same direction, so relative speed = 5 - 3 = 2 m/s.
2. The faster runner must gain one full lap = 400 m to catch the slower one from behind.
3. Time = 400 / 2 = 200 seconds.
**Check:** In 200 s the faster runner runs 1,000 m (2.5 laps) and the slower runner 600 m (1.5 laps); the difference is 400 m, one lap. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- On a circle, "catching up" means gaining a whole lap, because they started together.
- Opposite directions: add the speeds; they would meet every 400 / 8 = 50 seconds.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Opposite directions?* 50 seconds. 2) *Where do they meet first?* The faster runner has done 1,000 m, i.e. 200 m past the start line.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing 400 by 5 or by 3; adding the speeds for the same-direction case.

### Q63 ⭐ 🟡 In a 100 m race A beats B by 20 m. In another 100 m race B beats C by 10 m. Assuming constant speeds, by how many metres does A beat C in a 100 m race?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); races are a 1-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Chaining ratios of distances in the same time, not just adding the margins.
**Say this in the interview:** "When A runs 100, B runs 80. When B runs 100, C runs 90, so when B runs 80, C runs 72. So A beats C by 28 m."
**Answer:** **28 m**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. When A finishes 100 m, B has run 80 m. So A : B distances = 100 : 80.
2. When B runs 100 m, C runs 90 m. So B : C = 100 : 90, which means that while B runs 80 m, C runs 80 x 90/100 = 72 m.
3. So when A finishes 100 m, C has run 72 m.
4. A beats C by 100 - 72 = 28 m.
**Fast method (30 seconds):** Multiply the fractions: C's distance = 100 x 0.8 x 0.9 = 72, so the margin is 28 (not 20 + 10 = 30).
**Check:** Speed ratios A : B : C = 100 : 80 : 72 (in the same time). Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- "Beats by 20 m" means B is 20 m short when A finishes. B's speed is 80% of A's, C's speed is 90% of B's, so C's is 72% of A's.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *In a 200 m race?* C is 56 m behind. 2) *How many metres does B beat C by in the A-race?* B has run 80, C 72; 8 m.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Adding the margins to get 30 m.

### Q64 ⭐ 🟡 In what real situations does the simple rate-addition model of time and work fail, such as adding people late to a project or handoffs between team members?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a gap-fill item (model-limits discussion) added from standard practice, not from a source.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Judgement: can you tell where a textbook formula stops matching real work.
**Say this in the interview:** "The formula assumes every person works at a constant rate, independently, on work that can be split freely. Real projects break those assumptions: people need onboarding, tasks depend on each other, and coordination costs grow with team size."
**Answer:** **Rate addition (1/A + 1/B) holds only when workers are independent, constant-speed and the work is perfectly divisible; it fails when tasks are sequential, when adding people adds overhead, or when speed changes over time.**
**Explain simply:**
- Late additions: a new joiner needs onboarding time and takes a senior person's time away, so the team can get slower at first (the idea behind "adding people to a late project makes it later", known as Brooks's law in software management).
- Handoffs and dependencies: if step 2 cannot start until step 1 ends (data cleaning before analysis), two people cannot halve the time.
- Communication overhead: more people means more meetings and coordination, so the combined rate is less than the sum.
- Learning curves, fatigue and skill differences: rates change over time and differ by task.
- Fixed parts: some tasks (an approval, a data refresh that runs overnight) take the same time whatever the headcount.
**Worked mini-example (illustrative numbers I made up):** A report takes 1 analyst 12 days. The textbook says 4 analysts take 3 days. In reality, 2 days are sequential data cleaning that cannot be shared, then 10 days of work split across 4 gives 2.5 days, so about 4.5 days, plus a day of coordination: roughly 5.5 days. The model is a good first estimate, not a promise.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When is the formula still safe?* Repetitive, independent items such as processing identical tickets or screening resumes. 2) *How would you plan then?* Split into sequential and parallel parts, add a buffer for onboarding.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Quoting only the formula; or listing problems without an example. In an aptitude test, always use the formula; in an interview, show you know its limits.

### Q65 ⭐ 🔴 A, B and C can finish a job alone in 10, 15 and 12 days. They start together, but C leaves 3 days before the job is completed. In how many days is the job finished?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a "worker leaves early" variant is a 1-star pattern in the sources file (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Counting phases backwards from the finish, which is where the "3 days before" trap lives.
**Say this in the interview:** "All three together do 1/4 a day. Let the total time be T. C works T minus 3 days, A and B (1/6 a day) work all T. Solving gives T = 5 days."
**Answer:** **5 days**
**Step-by-step working:**
1. Rates: A = 1/10, B = 1/15, C = 1/12. In units of 1/60: A = 6, B = 4, C = 5 (job = 60 units).
2. All three together: 6 + 4 + 5 = 15 units per day. A and B only: 10 units per day.
3. Let the total time be T days. All three work for T - 3 days; A and B alone work for the last 3 days.
4. Last 3 days: 3 x 10 = 30 units. The first part must supply the other 30 units: (T - 3) x 15 = 30, so T - 3 = 2.
5. T = 5 days.
**Check:** Days 1-2: 2 x 15 = 30 units. Days 3-5: 3 x 10 = 30 units. Total 60 = the full job. Verified by script.
**Explain simply:**
- "C leaves 3 days before completion" means the last 3 days are done by A and B only; do not start counting from the beginning.
- Using units (60) turns the fractions into whole numbers.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *C leaves 2 days before the end?* Last 2 days 20 units; first part 40 units at 15/day gives 8/3 days, so T = 4 2/3 days. 2) *If C had left on day 1?* A and B alone take 6 days.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating the 3 days as the time C worked; dividing 60 by 15 and answering 4 days.
