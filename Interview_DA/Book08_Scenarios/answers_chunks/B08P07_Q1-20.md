### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You have five open requests from different teams and time to finish only two this week. What framework do you use to decide which two to do, and what do you tell the other three requesters?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list this scenario shape (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you prioritise by business value and effort, and communicate the trade-off instead of silently dropping work.
**Say this in the interview:** "I score each request on impact, urgency (is there a real deadline) and effort, pick the two with the best value for the time, and tell the other three requesters their new dates the same day."

**First, clarify / contain:** I confirm what decision each request supports, who uses it, and the real deadline. I do not start any of the five until I know this.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. For each request: what decision depends on it, and by when?
2. Impact: how many people or how much money or risk does it touch?
3. Effort in hours, including data cleaning.
4. Dependencies: does one request unblock another?
5. Can any be answered with a quick partial version (for example a rough number now)?
**If I find...:** High impact with a hard date: do now. High impact, no date: schedule. Low effort, moderate impact: squeeze in as a quick win. Low impact and no date: decline or push back.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I can deliver your report on Thursday of next week, not this week, because two requests with fixed deadlines came first. If your date is firm, please speak to my manager so we can agree what moves. Meanwhile I can send a rough figure by tomorrow."
**Prevent it next time:** A shared request queue with owner, deadline and effort, so priorities are visible to everyone.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who decides if two teams disagree?* My manager, with the facts I bring. 2) *What if a requester insists?* I offer the trade-off ("this moves, which one?") rather than refusing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Doing the loudest first; saying yes to all five; going silent on those left out.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Your manager and a senior director each ask you for an urgent report due tomorrow morning, and you can finish only one. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the conflicting-stakeholder prompt appears on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you escalate a conflict transparently instead of choosing by rank alone or hiding it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not decide alone. I would tell my manager and the director the clash at once, show the effort for each, and ask them to agree which goes first."

**First, clarify / contain:** Within minutes, I confirm what each report is for, what exactly is needed by tomorrow, and how long each takes. I do not quietly start one and ignore the other.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Is there a real deadline attached to each (a meeting, a board pack), or only a wish?
2. Can either be delivered in a smaller form (headline numbers only)?
3. Can part of one be reused for the other?
4. Does my manager already know about the director's ask?
**If I find...:** One has a fixed meeting tomorrow: that goes first. Both fixed: ask my manager to align with the director; offer a draft of the second. A cheap partial version exists: deliver both at different depth.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** To both, in one message: "Two reports are due tomorrow morning and I can complete one in full. Report A has a 9 am meeting, so I propose A first and B by noon. Please confirm or tell me if B must come first."
**Prevent it next time:** Agree a rule with my manager for ranking requests, and ask senior stakeholders to route requests through the shared queue.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the director insists?* Their call after hearing the trade-off, and my manager is informed. 2) *Rank always wins?* No, the decision it supports does.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing by seniority silently; promising both and delivering both badly.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Every stakeholder marks their request "urgent". How do you separate genuinely urgent work from work that only sounds loud?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you test urgency with questions and evidence, not tone or seniority.
**Say this in the interview:** "I ask what happens, and when, if this is not delivered. A real date and a real consequence make it urgent; a loud tone does not."

**First, clarify / contain:** For each request I ask: what decision is this for, by when, and what is the cost of a day's delay?
**What I would check, in order:**
1. A dated event behind it (audit, board meeting, payroll cut-off, legal deadline).
2. The consequence of delay (money, compliance, a person affected).
3. Whether a rough answer now would be enough.
4. Whether the requester has submitted it through the queue like others.
5. How often this person marks "urgent" (pattern).
**If I find...:** Dated and costly delay: genuinely urgent. No date: important maybe, not urgent. "ASAP" without a reason: ask one more question, then schedule normally.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I want to give this the right slot. What is the date or meeting it is needed for? If it is Friday's review, I will plan it for Thursday."
**Prevent it next time:** A simple priority form with a mandatory "needed by" and "decision it supports" field, plus a defined urgent definition.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if everything truly has a date?* Then escalate to my manager to rank. 2) *Do you ever refuse?* I re-schedule with a reason; I do not refuse.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating seniority as urgency; being rude about "urgent" labels; never asking why.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between urgent and important work, and how would you use an urgent-important grid on an analyst's request queue?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know the concept and can apply it to a real request queue.
**Say this in the interview:** "Urgent means time-pressured; important means it moves a goal that matters. I sort each request into four boxes and protect time for important work that is not yet urgent."

**Explain simply:**
- **Urgent:** needs action soon, with a deadline or consequence (payroll cut-off report).
- **Important:** contributes to a lasting goal (an attrition model, a cleaned master employee table), even without a deadline.
- The grid has four boxes:
  1. Urgent and important: do now (board-pack numbers due tomorrow).
  2. Important, not urgent: schedule (automating a monthly report).
  3. Urgent, not important: shorten, delegate or standardise (a one-off "quick number" someone could self-serve).
  4. Neither: decline or park (a report nobody reads).
- On my queue, I tag each request on both counts, place it in a box, and review weekly.
**Mini-example:** Five requests: board pack (box 1), data-quality clean-up (box 2), a manager wanting a headcount lookup (box 3), a legacy chart nobody uses (box 4).
**Practice step:** Put this week's tasks on a four-box sketch.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why does box 2 matter most?* Skipping it means box 1 grows later. 2) *Who decides "important"?* The business goal, agreed with my manager, not my taste.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Living in box 1 and 3 only; judging importance by the requester's seniority.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is personally identifiable information (PII) in HR or customer data, and how do you handle it in everyday work such as exports, emails and screenshots?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); data-privacy handling is a standard ethics prompt on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you recognise PII and have practical habits, not just a definition.
**Say this in the interview:** "PII is any data that identifies a person alone or when combined. I share the minimum, only with people who need it, and through approved channels."

**Explain simply:**
- **PII** (personally identifiable information): name, employee ID, phone, email, address, government ID numbers, bank details, salary, health or performance data, and combinations such as date of birth plus pincode plus department that point to one person.
- Everyday habits:
  1. Exports: only needed columns, only needed rows; remove or mask names and IDs where possible.
  2. Emails: do not attach raw files; use a controlled shared folder with access limited to named people.
  3. Screenshots: crop or blur names and IDs; check browser tabs and chat windows in the frame.
  4. Storage: no personal laptops, personal cloud or WhatsApp; delete temporary files.
  5. Ask the owner (HR head or data protection team) when unsure.
- Legal obligations vary; this is from memory, unverified, confirm with legal.
**Practice step:** Look at your last export and mark which columns were truly needed.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is an employee ID PII?* Yes in practice, since it links to a person. 2) *What if a screenshot leaks?* Report it quickly to the owner or IT/security.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking only name and phone count; emailing full sheets "for convenience".

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Can you use your company's data, or a copy of it, for a personal portfolio project or to practise on while switching careers?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you respect confidentiality and know how to build a portfolio legitimately.
**Say this in the interview:** "No, not without written permission. Company data belongs to the company, so I practise on public or synthetic data and describe my work only in general terms."

**Explain simply:**
- Company data, even with names removed, is usually covered by your employment contract and confidentiality terms. Copying it home or into a personal account is typically a breach; check your contract and policy (from memory, unverified, confirm with HR or legal).
- Employee-level HR data is especially sensitive.
- Safe options: public datasets (government open data, Kaggle), synthetic data you generate (for example 1,000 fake employees with invented attrition patterns), or an HR-style dataset you build yourself.
- In an interview you can describe the problem, method and impact at a general level, with no names, no real figures that are confidential, and no screenshots of internal dashboards, unless approved.

**Practice step:** Generate a synthetic attendance dataset and build one dashboard on it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if I remove names?* Still the company's data; ask first. 2) *Can I mention the project on my CV?* Yes, in general terms.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Three requests are in your queue, scored 1 to 10 on Impact, Confidence and Ease. Compute each ICE score (Impact x Confidence x Ease) and say which you would start with and what the score does not capture.
| Request | Impact | Confidence | Ease |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: monthly attrition dashboard refresh | 8 | 6 | 4 |
| B: fix duplicate employee IDs in the HRIS export | 5 | 9 | 8 |
| C: build a pay-band analysis for the CHRO | 9 | 3 | 5 |
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ICE scoring appears in prioritisation guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you can apply a scoring method and also see its limits.
**Say this in the interview:** "B scores highest at 360, so I would start there, but I would check whether a deadline or the CHRO's seniority changes the order."

**What the data shows:** The cheap, reliable fix (B) scores highest; the high-impact but uncertain and harder request (C) scores lowest.
**Working:**
1. A: 8 x 6 x 4 = 192.
2. B: 5 x 9 x 8 = 360.
3. C: 9 x 3 x 5 = 135.
4. Ranking: B (360), A (192), C (135).
**Result:** Start with B. All scores are unitless points; out of a maximum of 1,000.
**Caveats / what else I would check:** The score ignores deadlines (a CHRO meeting could make C urgent), dependencies (the duplicate-ID fix may be needed before A and C are trustworthy, which strengthens B), reach (how many people benefit), strategic fit, and the fact that scores are subjective guesses. Low Confidence on C also suggests a quick scoping step could raise it. Multiplying also lets one low number sink an item.
**Next step / recommendation:** Do B first, schedule A, and do a 1-hour scoping call on C to raise confidence and clarify the date. Review scores with stakeholders for agreement.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why multiply, not add?* Multiplying punishes any single weak factor. 2) *When would you override the score?* A hard deadline, a compliance issue or a dependency.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating scores as facts; ignoring deadlines; not saying what you tell the loser.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You discover an error in a report that went to the leadership team last week, and a decision may already have used it. What do you do, and in what order?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); wrong-data scenarios lead many question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, speed, impact assessment and a calm correction process.
**Say this in the interview:** "I confirm the error, size its impact, tell my manager straight away, then send a clear correction to the people who used it. I would rather correct early than be found out."

**First, clarify / contain:** Re-run to confirm it is really an error and not a misreading. Stop further use: pause any scheduled sends of the same report. Do not quietly fix and re-send.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. What exactly is wrong (formula, filter, source, date range)?
2. How big is the error, and which numbers change?
3. Who received it and which decision used it?
4. Does the corrected figure change the conclusion?
5. Root cause, so I can say it will not repeat.
**If I find...:** Conclusion unchanged: send a short correction note. Conclusion changes: tell my manager first, then the decision owner personally, with the corrected figures. Other reports share the cause: check and fix them too.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I found an error in last week's attrition report: leavers from the notice-period group were counted twice. The corrected rate is X, not Y. This changes/does not change the recommendation. I am checking the other reports from the same file and will confirm by 5 pm."
**Prevent it next time:** Peer review for leadership reports, reconciliation checks against a known total, and version notes.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you wait for the root cause?* No, flag it now; root cause follows. 2) *What if it is embarrassing?* Still disclose; owning it builds trust.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Silent fix; blaming the data team; over-apologising with no corrected numbers.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 HR and Finance define "headcount" differently and both want their dashboard first. How do you prioritise and resolve the conflict?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you resolve the definition conflict before building, and not by picking a side.
**Say this in the interview:** "The real problem is two definitions, not two dashboards. I would document both, get them agreed or labelled, then build once from a shared source."

**First, clarify / contain:** I write down each definition side by side. For example, HR may count active employees including contractors and those on leave at month-end, Finance may count paid full-time equivalents (FTE). I do not build either dashboard yet.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Exact definition, source table and cut-off date for each side.
2. How much the two numbers differ and why (contractors, notice period, part-time, leave).
3. Which decisions each team makes with its number.
4. Deadlines for each dashboard.
5. Who can arbitrate (CHRO and CFO, or a data governance forum).
**If I find...:** Differences are explainable: build one dashboard showing both, named clearly ("HR headcount", "Finance FTE") with a reconciliation bridge. Teams want one number: escalate for a single agreed definition. Deadlines differ: the earlier hard date goes first.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "Your numbers differ by N because of contractors and part-timers. I propose one dashboard with both labelled, and a one-page definition note. I need a 30-minute meeting with both of you this week."
**Prevent it next time:** A shared metric dictionary with an owner for each definition.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who wins?* Neither; the definition owner agrees what is used where. 2) *Which dashboard first?* The one with the hard deadline, built on the agreed definition.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Building two conflicting dashboards; taking sides.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You have 40 working hours this week, 12 of them already committed to recurring reports and data fixes. The open requests are listed below. Which combination uses the free hours exactly, and what do you say to the requester whose work is left out?
| Request | Estimated hours |
|---|---|
| Attrition deep-dive for a plant head | 14 |
| Hiring-funnel report for Talent Acquisition | 10 |
| Overtime audit for Payroll | 8 |
| Survey summary for the CHRO | 6 |
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Capacity arithmetic plus honest communication about what is dropped.
**Say this in the interview:** "I have 28 free hours. Attrition, overtime and survey add up to exactly 28, so the hiring funnel waits, and I would tell Talent Acquisition the new date straight away."

**What the data shows:** Requests total 38 hours against 28 free, so not all can fit.
**Working:**
1. Free hours: 40 - 12 = 28.
2. Total requested: 14 + 10 + 8 + 6 = 38, which is 10 over.
3. Checked every combination: only 14 + 8 + 6 = 28 matches exactly. Others: 14 + 10 = 24, 14 + 10 + 6 = 30 (over), 14 + 10 + 8 = 32 (over), 10 + 8 + 6 = 24 (4 spare).
**Result:** Do attrition (14), overtime audit (8) and survey (6) = 28 hours; leave the hiring funnel (10 hours).
**Caveats / what else I would check:** Estimates are guesses and need a buffer; an exact fit leaves no slack, so in practice I would keep a few hours spare and accept slippage. Deadlines and impact matter more than a perfect fit: if the hiring funnel has a hard date or a bigger impact, swap it for the attrition work. Pure hour-matching is a puzzle, not a real policy.
**Next step / recommendation:** Confirm priorities with my manager, then message Talent Acquisition.
**What I would tell the requester:** "I can start your hiring-funnel report next week, about 10 hours. This week's capacity went to work with earlier dates. If you need a quick headline by Friday, I can send one number, or we can ask my manager to reprioritise."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the plan overruns?* I flag early and cut scope. 2) *Would you work extra hours?* Not as a default; I would raise it.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting the 12 committed hours; planning at 100% utilisation.

### Q18 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 Your manager asks you to remove some outliers so that the attrition rate presented to the board falls below the target, although the records are genuine. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); pressure-to-alter-numbers prompts are common in ethics questions on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you refuse to manipulate genuine data, calmly, and offer legitimate alternatives.
**Say this in the interview:** "Genuine records are not outliers to delete. I would respectfully decline to remove them, show the honest number with context, and escalate if pressed."

**First, clarify / contain:** Ask what is behind the request: is there a real data problem, or only the target? Do not change numbers meanwhile.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Are the records really genuine (not duplicates, test records or errors)?
2. Is there a legitimate definitional reason (for example, a one-time restructuring exit that is reported separately, defined beforehand)?
3. Which segment explains the high rate?
4. What the honest number is, and how far from target.
5. What company policy or code of conduct says on reporting.
**If I find...:** Records are errors: fix them and document. A defined, consistent exclusion exists: apply it openly, showing both with and without. Records are genuine and request is just to hit target: decline to delete.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I cannot remove genuine records; the board would be misled and we would be exposed. I suggest we present the rate as it is, split out the restructuring exits and show the underlying trend, with our action plan."
**Prevent it next time:** Define metric rules and exclusions in a written metric dictionary before results are known.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the manager insists?* Escalate to the next level, or compliance or ethics channel per company policy. 2) *Is showing both numbers OK?* Yes, if both are labelled.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Complying quietly; accusing your manager; confusing real outliers with errors.

### Q19 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 Two senior leaders give you conflicting priorities, your manager is on leave for a week and the deadlines clash. How do you decide and who do you inform?

**What the interviewer is checking:** Judgement and communication when your usual decision-maker is absent.

**First, clarify / contain:** Confirm each ask and date. Check who is covering for my manager. Do not start work that hides the conflict.

3. Who is the delegate or my manager's manager?

### Q20 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is data minimisation, and how does it change your response when someone asks for the full employee export "just in case"?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you apply a privacy principle in a real request.
**Say this in the interview:** "Data minimisation means using only the data needed for a stated purpose. So I ask what they need and send only those columns and rows, not the full export."

**Explain simply:**
- **Data minimisation:** collect, use and share only the personal data necessary for a specific purpose, and keep it no longer than needed. Many privacy laws, such as GDPR and India's DPDP Act, reflect this idea; stated as principle, from memory, unverified, confirm with legal.
- For a "just in case" export: I ask the purpose, then offer the smallest dataset that meets it (e.g., department and tenure, no names, no salary).
- "Just in case" is not a purpose; if a new need arises, we can request again.
**Mini-example:** Request: all 60 columns for 5,000 employees. Purpose: attrition by department. Needed: employee ID (or none), department, join date, exit date, 4 or 5 columns, aggregated where possible.
**Practice step:** Next time you export, write the purpose in the file name or ticket and delete unused columns.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they say it's faster?* Faster now, risk later. 2) *Can I keep an extra copy?* No, avoid duplicates and delete after use.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Sending everything to avoid conflict; confusing minimisation with refusing all requests.
