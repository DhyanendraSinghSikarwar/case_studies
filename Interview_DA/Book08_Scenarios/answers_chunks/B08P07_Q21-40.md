### Q21 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between anonymised and pseudonymised data, and which one is a name-masked employee list with employee IDs kept?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); privacy-basics questions appear in prep-site ethics banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know masking a name is not the same as making data safe, and that you will not call it "anonymous" by mistake.
**Say this in the interview:** "A name-masked list with employee IDs kept is pseudonymised, not anonymised, because the ID can be linked back to a person. I would treat it as personal data."
**Explain simply:**
- **Anonymised:** no one can reasonably work out who a record belongs to, even by combining it with other data. The link back is gone for good. Example: a count of leavers by department with no individual rows.
- **Pseudonymised:** direct identifiers (name) are replaced by a code, but a key or lookup table (ID to person) still exists somewhere, or the record can be matched with other data. Example: the HRIS export with names blanked but employee ID, joining date and grade kept.
- The masked list is therefore **pseudonymised**. Anyone with access to the HRIS can look up the ID in seconds. Even without the ID, joining date plus grade plus location can single out one person in a small team.
- Why it matters: laws such as GDPR and India's DPDP Act generally still treat pseudonymised data as personal data, so the same care applies. From memory, unverified, confirm with legal.
**Practice step:** Take a 5-row sample and ask "could a colleague guess who this row is?" If yes, it is not anonymous.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you make it anonymous?* Drop the ID, group ages and dates into bands, report only aggregates, and hide small groups. 2) *Is pseudonymisation useless then?* No, it lowers risk and is good practice; it just does not remove the duty of care.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying "I removed the names so it is anonymous"; forgetting that rare combinations (one female in a senior Legal grade) identify people.

### Q22 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the RICE scoring method, and when would you use it instead of ICE for an analytics backlog?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); RICE and ICE are standard in product-management guides and vendor blogs (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know a simple, defensible scoring method and when the extra factor is worth it.
**Say this in the interview:** "RICE scores each request as Reach times Impact times Confidence, divided by Effort. I use it instead of ICE when the number of people affected differs a lot between requests."
**Explain simply:**
- **Reach:** how many people or decisions are touched in a period (employees, managers, orders).
- **Impact:** how much it moves things for each of them, on a small scale such as 1 to 3.
- **Confidence:** how sure you are of your Reach and Impact guesses (as a percentage). It stops a hopeful idea from beating a proven one.
- **Effort:** person-weeks to deliver. Bigger effort lowers the score.
- **ICE** is Impact, Confidence, Ease, with no Reach. It is quicker and fine for a small backlog where everything touches about the same audience.
- Use RICE when requests differ in audience: a payroll fix for 300 people versus a roster study for 1,200. Use ICE when you are short of time or have no reach data.
- Scores are a conversation starter, not a verdict (see Q29 for overrides).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who sets the numbers?* I propose them, then check Reach and Impact with the requester and Effort with whoever builds it. 2) *Is a high score always first?* No; legal deadlines and blockers can override.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating the score as exact; scoring Impact on different scales per request; ignoring Confidence.

### Q23 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A weekly report takes you 3 hours to prepare by hand, and automating it would take about 20 hours. After how many weeks does the automation pay back, and would you automate it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); gap-fill exercise (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Simple payback arithmetic plus judgement about whether the report will live long enough and be correct enough to automate.
**Say this in the interview:** "20 hours divided by 3 hours a week is 6.7 weeks, so it pays back in 7 weeks. If the report will run for months, I automate it, after checking it is still wanted."
**Explain simply:**
- Payback = build time / time saved per run = 20 / 3 = 6.67 weeks, so about **7 weeks** (21 hours saved by then, rounded up to a whole run).
- Over a year (52 weeks): 52 x 3 = 156 hours by hand, versus 20 to build; about 136 hours saved before maintenance.
- Real payback is a little longer because automated reports need upkeep (a changed column, a new filter) and a check that the numbers still match; I would budget a few hours a quarter and re-check.
- Before building, confirm: will the report run at least a few months, are the source and logic stable, and does anyone actually read it (see Q17)? If the report may be dropped in a month, do not automate.
- Decision: automate, because the payback is short, and tell the requester the manual hours return to the team for analysis.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the build is 80 hours?* 80 / 3 = 26.7 weeks (about 27); still worth it for a long-lived report, not for a short one. 2) *What is the risk?* Silent errors; add row-count and total checks.

### Q28 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Three analytics projects are scored with RICE (Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort). Compute the scores, pick the first project, and say how you would break the tie between the other two.
| Project | Reach (employees) | Impact (1-3) | Confidence | Effort (person-weeks) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X: exit-interview theme summary | 500 | 2 | 80% | 4 |
| Y: shift-roster optimisation study | 1,200 | 1 | 50% | 3 |
| Z: payroll-error root-cause check | 300 | 3 | 100% | 2 |
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); RICE exercise is a gap-fill (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Correct arithmetic, a clear pick, and sensible tie-breaking beyond the score.
**Say this in the interview:** "Z scores 450, X and Y tie at 200. Z goes first; between X and Y I would look at confidence and risk, not flip a coin."
**What the data shows:** Z is small in reach but high impact, certain, and cheap, so it wins by a wide margin. X and Y land on the same score from very different shapes.
**Working:**
1. X: 500 x 2 x 0.80 / 4 = 800 / 4 = 200.
2. Y: 1,200 x 1 x 0.50 / 3 = 600 / 3 = 200.

For the X versus Y tie: X has the higher confidence (80% versus 50%), so the benefit is more likely real; Y has lower effort (3 versus 4 weeks) but its low confidence suggests a 1-week feasibility check first. I would take X next, and run the small Y check if capacity allows. Revisit the order after Z is done.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not Y, with the biggest reach?* Its impact per person is small and I am only half sure of it. 2) *Could you change the Impact scale?* Yes, but fix it for all requests before scoring.

### Q29 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Requests are scored on weights Impact 50%, Urgency 30% and Ease 20% (each scored 1 to 10). Compute the weighted scores and choose; then say when you would override the score.
| Request | Impact | Urgency | Ease |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 8 | 5 | 6 |
| B | 6 | 9 | 4 |
| C | 7 | 6 | 9 |

**What the interviewer is checking:** Weighted-average arithmetic, and the judgement to treat the score as a guide.
**Say this in the interview:** "C scores 7.1, A 6.7, B 6.5, so C first by the model; but the scores are close, and I would override for a hard deadline or a risk to people."
**What the data shows:** The three are within 0.6 points of each other, so the ranking is fragile.

1. A: 0.5 x 8 + 0.3 x 5 + 0.2 x 6 = 4.0 + 1.5 + 1.2 = 6.7.

### Q36 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A survey of 1,000 employees got 150 responses, mostly from head office. The CHRO wants one company-wide engagement score on a slide. What is the response rate, and what do you do?

**What the interviewer is checking:** That you spot non-response and sampling bias and still help the CHRO.
**Say this in the interview:** "The response rate is 15 percent, and mostly head office, so one company-wide score would not represent everyone. I would show it as 'head-office-weighted' with caveats, or by group."

**Caveats / what else I would check:** Who did not respond and why (no access, no time, fear of being identified). Small groups (a handful of responses) give unstable scores and may identify people, so group scores need a minimum count. Weighting cannot fix a group with almost no responses.
**Next step / recommendation:** Present the score by group with the number of responses beside each, label it clearly ("based on 15% of employees, mostly head office"), avoid one headline claim, and propose a follow-up push to under-represented sites. Tell the CHRO: "I can give you a number for the slide, with the label; a single company-wide claim is not safe yet."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What response rate is good?* There is no universal bar; the mix of responders matters more than the percentage. 2) *Can you just weight it?* Partly, with caution.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reporting the average as "the company's engagement"; hiding the response rate; reporting results for groups with only a few responses.

### Q37 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A report breaks attrition down by department, gender and grade, and one cell contains only 2 employees. What is the risk, and what do you do?
| Department | Grade | Gender | Headcount | Leavers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal | Senior | Female | 2 | 1 |
| Legal | Senior | Male | 9 | 1 |
| Ops | Senior | Female | 40 | 5 |
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); small-cell disclosure is a gap-fill (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know small groups can expose individuals and give unreliable percentages.
**Say this in the interview:** "A cell of 2 people lets readers work out who left, and its 50 percent rate is meaningless. I would combine or suppress the cell, following the company's minimum group size."
**What the data shows:** The Legal senior female row is 1 leaver out of 2, which looks like a 50 percent rate; the other rows are 1 of 9 (11.1%) and 5 of 40 (12.5%).
**Working:**
1. Legal, Senior, Female: 1 / 2 = 50.0%.
2. Legal, Senior, Male: 1 / 9 = 11.1%.
3. Ops, Senior, Female: 5 / 40 = 12.5%.
4. Combined Legal Senior: 2 / 11 = 18.2%.
**Result:** Two risks: (1) **Identification:** with only 2 women, colleagues can guess who left, and the report reveals a personal outcome. (2) **Unreliable rate:** one person leaving swings the rate by 50 points, so 50% says nothing about real attrition. Percentages are rounded to one decimal.
**Caveats / what else I would check:** Even if I hide the small cell, the total (Legal Senior 11, 2 leavers) with the male cell makes the female cell calculable by subtraction (complementary disclosure), so I must hide or combine more than one cell. The minimum size (often 5 or 10) is a company policy; confirm it and the legal position with the privacy team (from memory, unverified).
**Next step / recommendation:** Merge cells (Legal Senior, both genders, or all of Legal across grades) or suppress with "fewer than 5", show headcount and leavers only for adequate groups, and add a footnote on the rule. Offer HR leaders the detailed view only through an approved, access-controlled channel if there is a legitimate need.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Does anonymising names fix it?* No; the combination of department, grade and gender identifies. 2) *Why 5?* A convention to lower the chance of re-identification, not a magic number.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Hiding just one cell while totals reveal it; reporting 50% as a finding; dropping the group completely and losing the signal instead of merging.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A manager asks you for a named list of "the weakest performers" built from attendance and ratings data. What do you ask, and what do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); HR-data ethics prompts appear in prep-site banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you check purpose, authorisation and fairness before releasing a named, consequential list.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would ask what decision this list is for and who authorised it. I would not hand over a named list from two thin signals; I would route it through HR with context."
**First, clarify / contain:** Do not send anything yet. Ask: What decision is it for (coaching, a performance plan, redundancy)? Is this manager entitled to see this data for those people (their own team only)? Has HR or the business partner been involved?
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Authorisation and purpose: is this use consistent with why the data was collected (from memory, a purpose-limitation principle in privacy laws such as GDPR and India's DPDP Act, unverified, confirm with legal)?
2. Data quality: attendance can reflect approved leave, illness or system errors; ratings can be biased or from different managers, and are not comparable across teams.
3. Fairness: would the list disproportionately hit protected groups (women on maternity-related leave, people with disabilities)?
4. Is a named list needed at all, or would an anonymised distribution or team pattern answer the need?
**If I find...:** Legitimate need and HR sign-off: provide a limited list with context columns (leave type, rating history) and an agreed disclaimer. No clear purpose, or the request is for punitive use without HR: decline politely and escalate to HR. Data issues: fix before any use.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I can help, but a named list built from attendance and one rating can be unfair and risky. Let us go through HR on the purpose; meanwhile I can give you a team-level view showing where support is most needed."
**Prevent it next time:** Written rules on who can see named performance data and a request form that records purpose and approver.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the manager is your boss?* Same approach, and I escalate to HR or my manager's manager if pressed. 2) *What alternative can you give?* Aggregated patterns or a list for coaching rather than punishment.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Just sending the list; treating attendance as performance; refusing without offering an alternative.

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟡 You accidentally emailed a spreadsheet with employee salaries to a wrong external address. What do you do in the next hour?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); data-breach scenario in prep-site ethics banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Speed, honesty, and following the incident process, not hiding it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would report it immediately to my manager and the data protection or IT security team, try to recall the email, and write down exactly what happened."
**First, clarify / contain:** Do not delete the evidence or hope it goes unnoticed. Try the email recall (it may not work for external addresses). Note the exact time, recipient address, subject and file name.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. What was in the file: how many people, which fields (names, IDs, salaries, bank details)?
2. Who was the recipient: a clearly wrong typo address, or an external company? Is the attachment password-protected?
3. Was it opened or forwarded (a read receipt or mail-server logs, via IT)?
4. Is there any other copy, such as a shared link I can revoke?
**If I find...:** Sensitive fields or many people: the incident team decides on contacting the recipient to ask for deletion in writing and on whether affected employees or regulators must be informed. Laws such as GDPR and India's DPDP Act have breach-notification principles with time limits; from memory, unverified, confirm with legal and the privacy officer, who decide, not me. Low-risk (a protected file, a mistyped internal-looking address) still gets reported.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "At 2:10 pm I sent the salary sheet (about 300 employees: name, ID, salary) to an incorrect external address. I tried to recall it. I am reporting it now so you can decide next steps. I will send you the full details in 15 minutes."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if no one noticed?* I still report it; hiding it is worse. 2) *Will you be punished?* Prompt reporting is what most policies look for (confirm local policy).

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 A resume-screening tool shortlists 40 of 100 male applicants and 24 of 100 female applicants. Compute the selection-rate ratio, say what it suggests, and what you do as the analyst.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the selection-rate ratio is a gap-fill (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Correct arithmetic, the right caution about what the ratio proves, and an action plan that does not overclaim.
**Say this in the interview:** "Male selection is 40 percent and female is 24 percent, a ratio of 0.60. That is a warning sign of possible adverse impact, not proof of discrimination, so I would investigate and tell HR."
**What the data shows:** Women were shortlisted at 60 percent of the men's rate, a 16 percentage point gap.
**Working:**
1. Male selection rate = 40 / 100 = 40%.
2. Female selection rate = 24 / 100 = 24%.
3. Ratio = 24 / 40 = 0.60 (the gap is 40 - 24 = 16 percentage points).
4. A simple two-group test (chi-square, checked by script) gives p about 0.015 (uncorrected) to 0.023 (with correction or Fisher), so a gap this size is unlikely to be chance alone with 100 per group.
**Result:** Selection-rate ratio 0.60; difference 16 percentage points.
**Caveats / what else I would check:** A common rule of thumb in US employment practice is the "four-fifths rule" (a ratio below 0.80 flags possible adverse impact); from memory, unverified, other countries differ, so confirm with legal. The ratio alone does not prove bias: check whether the applicant groups differ in qualifications or roles applied for, whether the tool uses proxies (gaps in work history, college names, keywords), and the data for other attributes. Small samples (100 each) are limited.
**Next step / recommendation:** Report it to HR and the tool owner now with the figures; compare shortlisting by job-relevant criteria; ask the vendor how the tool was trained and tested; suggest a human review of rejected female applicants; and pause or monitor the tool until understood. Monitor the ratio every hiring cycle. Handle the applicants' data carefully (privacy), and keep the analysis within a small, authorised group.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is 0.60 proof of discrimination?* No; it is a flag that needs investigation. 2) *What if the vendor says it is fair?* Ask for evidence, run our own check on our data.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Subtracting instead of dividing and calling it "16 percent" (it is 16 percentage points); claiming intent; ignoring the legal and privacy side of sensitive attributes.
