### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 An attrition-risk model is 90% accurate on a company where 10% of employees leave. Show why that number can be misleading and how you would present it to HR.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you know accuracy hides failure on a rare group, and can report recall and precision in plain words.
**Say this in the interview:** "A model that predicts 'nobody leaves' is also 90% accurate and finds zero leavers. I would report how many real leavers it catches and how many of its flags are right, not accuracy alone."

**What the data shows:** Only 1 in 10 employees leaves, so "everyone stays" is right 90% of the time without any intelligence.
**Working (illustrative, 1,000 employees):**
1. 100 leave, 900 stay. The do-nothing rule "nobody leaves" scores 900/1,000 = 90% accuracy and catches 0 of 100 leavers.
2. A real-looking model: flags 80 people; 40 of them truly leave (true positives), 40 stay (false alarms). It misses 60 leavers. Correct stayers = 900 - 40 = 860.
3. Accuracy = (40 + 860) / 1,000 = 90%, the same as the do-nothing rule.
4. Recall (share of leavers caught) = 40/100 = 40%. Precision (share of flags that are right) = 40/80 = 50%.
**Result:** Both models show 90% accuracy; the useful one catches 40% of leavers with 50% of flags correct. (Rounded to whole percentages; numbers are exercise inputs.)
**Caveats / what else I would check:** Compare against the do-nothing baseline; check results on a later time period, not only the training period; check by department; check how many flags HR can realistically act on.
**Next step / recommendation:** Present to HR as: "Out of 100 people who will leave, we flag 40 early. Of every 10 people we flag, 5 would have left anyway." Ask HR what a missed leaver and a false alarm each cost, then choose the cut-off together.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) Which metric would you use? Recall and precision, plus a lift view (how much better than random). 2) What is a false positive here? An employee flagged who would have stayed; treat flags as conversation starters, not verdicts.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Quoting accuracy alone; skipping the baseline; presenting flags as certainties about named individuals.

I recommend human review and a quarterly check."
**Prevent it next time:** Fairness testing before launch and on a schedule; documented limits.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) Remove gender column only? Not enough, proxies remain. 2) Does fairness cost accuracy? Sometimes slightly; report both.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Hiding the bug; only dropping the gender column; claiming it is fixed without retesting.

### Q59 ⭐ 🔴 A retail model infers customers' health conditions from their purchases to target ads. How do you evaluate whether to build or ship it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you see privacy and harm in sensitive inference and apply an ethics check.
**Say this in the interview:** "Inferring health from shopping is a sensitive use customers would not expect. I would raise it with legal and privacy before building, and I lean towards not shipping."

**First, clarify / contain:** Ask what the ads are for and who sees them. Do not train or ship until reviewed.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Expectation: would a customer be surprised and uncomfortable (a pregnancy or illness guessed from purchases)?
2. Legal and policy: sensitive inferences may be treated strictly (principle; from memory, unverified, confirm with legal).
3. Harm: wrong guesses, exposure to family members, discrimination.
4. Benefit versus alternative: can we target by product interest instead of health?
5. Controls: opt-in, opt-out, no sharing with third parties.
**If I find...:** Surprising or high-harm: do not build. Clear opt-in and low harm: possibly proceed under review. Always use product categories, not health labels.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "The model could earn clicks but risks customer trust and legal trouble. I suggest targeting based on product interest and offering customers control."
**Prevent it next time:** An ethics and privacy review step before any new model that infers personal traits.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) What if it is accurate? Accuracy does not make it acceptable. 2) Who decides? Legal, privacy and leadership, with my input.
**Common mistakes / traps:** "If it is legal it is fine"; ignoring wrong inferences.

### Q60 ⭐ 🔴 A teammate asks you to approve a report you suspect contains copied data they did not have permission to use. How do you proceed?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you act with integrity: verify, do not sign off, and escalate properly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not approve it until I know where the data came from. I would ask my teammate first, and if I cannot get a clear answer, tell my manager or the data owner."

**First, clarify / contain:** Do not approve, publish or forward. Do not accuse; I only suspect.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Ask my teammate for the source, licence or permission for each dataset.
2. Look for signs: different formatting, external names, no matching internal tables.
3. Ask the data owner or legal if the source is allowed.
**If I find...:** Permission exists: approve, and add the source to the report. No permission: remove the data or get permission before release. Teammate evasive: escalate to my manager.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I cannot sign off yet because the source of the data in section 3 is not documented. Please send the permission or licence, or I will need to remove it. I will review again as soon as you send it."
**Prevent it next time:** Source and permission lines on every report; a standard review checklist.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) What if it is already published? Tell my manager so it can be pulled. 2) What if the teammate is a friend? Same process.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Approving to avoid conflict; accusing without checking; saying nothing.
