# Book 8 — Scenario-Based & Data Interpretation | Part 7: Prioritisation & Ethics Scenarios 🟡

**Target:** 60 questions | **Actual:** 60 questions
**Scope:** On-the-job prioritisation (competing requests, deadlines, scoring methods) and data ethics (privacy, integrity, fairness, pressure from stakeholders), with some inline-number exercises.

### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You have five open requests from different teams and time to finish only two this week. What framework do you use to decide which two to do, and what do you tell the other three requesters?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep sites list this scenario shape (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you prioritise by business value and effort, and communicate the trade-off instead of silently dropping work.
**Say this in the interview:** "I score each request on impact, urgency (is there a real deadline) and effort, pick the two with the best value for the time, and tell the other three requesters their new dates the same day."

**First, clarify / contain:** I confirm what decision each request supports, who uses it, and the real deadline. I do not start any of the five until I know this.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. For each request: what decision depends on it, and by when?
2. Impact: how many people or how much money or risk does it touch?
3. Effort in hours, including data cleaning.
4. Dependencies: does one request unblock another?
5. Can any be answered with a quick partial version (for example a rough number now)?
**If I find...:** High impact with a hard date: do now. High impact, no date: schedule. Low effort, moderate impact: squeeze in as a quick win. Low impact and no date: decline or push back.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I can deliver your report on Thursday of next week, not this week, because two requests with fixed deadlines came first. If your date is firm, please speak to my manager so we can agree what moves. Meanwhile I can send a rough figure by tomorrow."
**Prevent it next time:** A shared request queue with owner, deadline and effort, so priorities are visible to everyone.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who decides if two teams disagree?* My manager, with the facts I bring. 2) *What if a requester insists?* I offer the trade-off ("this moves, which one?") rather than refusing.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Doing the loudest first; saying yes to all five; going silent on those left out.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Your manager and a senior director each ask you for an urgent report due tomorrow morning, and you can finish only one. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the conflicting-stakeholder prompt appears on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you escalate a conflict transparently instead of choosing by rank alone or hiding it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not decide alone. I would tell my manager and the director the clash at once, show the effort for each, and ask them to agree which goes first."

**First, clarify / contain:** Within minutes, I confirm what each report is for, what exactly is needed by tomorrow, and how long each takes. I do not quietly start one and ignore the other.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Is there a real deadline attached to each (a meeting, a board pack), or only a wish?
2. Can either be delivered in a smaller form (headline numbers only)?
3. Can part of one be reused for the other?
4. Does my manager already know about the director's ask?
**If I find...:** One has a fixed meeting tomorrow: that goes first. Both fixed: ask my manager to align with the director; offer a draft of the second. A cheap partial version exists: deliver both at different depth.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** To both, in one message: "Two reports are due tomorrow morning and I can complete one in full. Report A has a 9 am meeting, so I propose A first and B by noon. Please confirm or tell me if B must come first."
**Prevent it next time:** Agree a rule with my manager for ranking requests, and ask senior stakeholders to route requests through the shared queue.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the director insists?* Their call after hearing the trade-off, and my manager is informed. 2) *Rank always wins?* No, the decision it supports does.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Choosing by seniority silently; promising both and delivering both badly.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Every stakeholder marks their request "urgent". How do you separate genuinely urgent work from work that only sounds loud?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you test urgency with questions and evidence, not tone or seniority.
**Say this in the interview:** "I ask what happens, and when, if this is not delivered. A real date and a real consequence make it urgent; a loud tone does not."

**First, clarify / contain:** For each request I ask: what decision is this for, by when, and what is the cost of a day's delay?
**What I would check, in order:**
1. A dated event behind it (audit, board meeting, payroll cut-off, legal deadline).
2. The consequence of delay (money, compliance, a person affected).
3. Whether a rough answer now would be enough.
4. Whether the requester has submitted it through the queue like others.
5. How often this person marks "urgent" (pattern).
**If I find...:** Dated and costly delay: genuinely urgent. No date: important maybe, not urgent. "ASAP" without a reason: ask one more question, then schedule normally.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I want to give this the right slot. What is the date or meeting it is needed for? If it is Friday's review, I will plan it for Thursday."
**Prevent it next time:** A simple priority form with a mandatory "needed by" and "decision it supports" field, plus a defined urgent definition.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if everything truly has a date?* Then escalate to my manager to rank. 2) *Do you ever refuse?* I re-schedule with a reason; I do not refuse.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating seniority as urgency; being rude about "urgent" labels; never asking why.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between urgent and important work, and how would you use an urgent-important grid on an analyst's request queue?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know the concept and can apply it to a real request queue.
**Say this in the interview:** "Urgent means time-pressured; important means it moves a goal that matters. I sort each request into four boxes and protect time for important work that is not yet urgent."

**Explain simply:**
- **Urgent:** needs action soon, with a deadline or consequence (payroll cut-off report).
- **Important:** contributes to a lasting goal (an attrition model, a cleaned master employee table), even without a deadline.
- The grid has four boxes:
  1. Urgent and important: do now (board-pack numbers due tomorrow).
  2. Important, not urgent: schedule (automating a monthly report).
  3. Urgent, not important: shorten, delegate or standardise (a one-off "quick number" someone could self-serve).
  4. Neither: decline or park (a report nobody reads).
- On my queue, I tag each request on both counts, place it in a box, and review weekly.
**Mini-example:** Five requests: board pack (box 1), data-quality clean-up (box 2), a manager wanting a headcount lookup (box 3), a legacy chart nobody uses (box 4).
**Practice step:** Put this week's tasks on a four-box sketch.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why does box 2 matter most?* Skipping it means box 1 grows later. 2) *Who decides "important"?* The business goal, agreed with my manager, not my taste.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Living in box 1 and 3 only; judging importance by the requester's seniority.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What is personally identifiable information (PII) in HR or customer data, and how do you handle it in everyday work such as exports, emails and screenshots?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); data-privacy handling is a standard ethics prompt on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you recognise PII and have practical habits, not just a definition.
**Say this in the interview:** "PII is any data that identifies a person alone or when combined. I share the minimum, only with people who need it, and through approved channels."

**Explain simply:**
- **PII** (personally identifiable information): name, employee ID, phone, email, address, government ID numbers, bank details, salary, health or performance data, and combinations such as date of birth plus pincode plus department that point to one person.
- Everyday habits:
  1. Exports: only needed columns, only needed rows; remove or mask names and IDs where possible.
  2. Emails: do not attach raw files; use a controlled shared folder with access limited to named people.
  3. Screenshots: crop or blur names and IDs; check browser tabs and chat windows in the frame.
  4. Storage: no personal laptops, personal cloud or WhatsApp; delete temporary files.
  5. Ask the owner (HR head or data protection team) when unsure.
- Legal obligations vary; this is from memory, unverified, confirm with legal.
**Practice step:** Look at your last export and mark which columns were truly needed.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Is an employee ID PII?* Yes in practice, since it links to a person. 2) *What if a screenshot leaks?* Report it quickly to the owner or IT/security.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Thinking only name and phone count; emailing full sheets "for convenience".

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 Can you use your company's data, or a copy of it, for a personal portfolio project or to practise on while switching careers?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you respect confidentiality and know how to build a portfolio legitimately.
**Say this in the interview:** "No, not without written permission. Company data belongs to the company, so I practise on public or synthetic data and describe my work only in general terms."

**Explain simply:**
- Company data, even with names removed, is usually covered by your employment contract and confidentiality terms. Copying it home or into a personal account is typically a breach; check your contract and policy (from memory, unverified, confirm with HR or legal).
- Employee-level HR data is especially sensitive.
- Safe options: public datasets (government open data, Kaggle), synthetic data you generate (for example 1,000 fake employees with invented attrition patterns), or an HR-style dataset you build yourself.
- In an interview you can describe the problem, method and impact at a general level, with no names, no real figures that are confidential, and no screenshots of internal dashboards, unless approved.
- If you really want to show real work, ask your manager for written approval of an anonymised version.
**Practice step:** Generate a synthetic attendance dataset and build one dashboard on it.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if I remove names?* Still the company's data; ask first. 2) *Can I mention the project on my CV?* Yes, in general terms.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Emailing files to a personal account; posting screenshots online.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You realise you will miss a deadline you committed to. When and how do you tell the stakeholder?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Early, honest communication with a recovery plan.
**Say this in the interview:** "As soon as I know, not on the day. I give the reason, a new date and an option to meet part of the need sooner."

**First, clarify / contain:** I work out how late, why, and what I can still deliver. I do not wait hoping to catch up.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Realistic new date, with a buffer.
2. What is blocking me (data delay, scope growth, another request)?
3. Can a partial or draft version meet the need by the original date?
4. Does the delay affect anyone downstream?
5. Do I need help or my manager's input?
**If I find...:** Partial version possible: send it on time, labelled. Blocker is outside my control: name the owner and escalate. My own estimate error: say so plainly.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "The attrition report I promised for Wednesday will be ready Friday noon. The payroll extract arrived two days late. By Wednesday I can send the headline numbers, marked draft. Please tell me if Friday is too late for your meeting."
**Prevent it next time:** Pad estimates, check in at the halfway point, and surface risks at the first sign.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the stakeholder is upset?* Acknowledge, offer the options, stay factual. 2) *How early is early?* As soon as the risk is real, ideally with time to adjust.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Silence until the deadline; excuses without a new date.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 A colleague outside HR asks you informally, over chat, to send the employee-level salary sheet. What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you will not release sensitive data on an informal ask, but stay helpful.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not send it. Salary data is restricted, so I ask what they need it for and route the request through the approved owner."

**First, clarify / contain:** Pause: nothing is sent. I do not argue in chat; I say I need to check who can approve.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Who the colleague is and their role.
2. The purpose, and whether aggregated figures (average by grade) would do.
3. Who owns this data and approves access (HR head, compensation team).
4. Whether a formal access process exists.
5. Whether it is a possible social-engineering attempt (unusual urgency, new contact).
**If I find...:** Aggregate answers the need: send aggregates once approved. Legitimate need: the owner approves in writing, then I share the minimum, securely. Suspicious: report to my manager or security.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I cannot send employee-level salary data without approval from the compensation owner. Tell me what you are trying to decide; I can probably give you averages by grade. I will check and get back by tomorrow noon."
**Prevent it next time:** Documented data-access policy, and the habit of redirecting informal asks to a ticket.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the colleague is senior?* Same rule; seniority is not approval. 2) *What if they are annoyed?* Stay polite and offer the alternative.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Sending "just this once"; refusing with no alternative.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Three requests are in your queue, scored 1 to 10 on Impact, Confidence and Ease. Compute each ICE score (Impact x Confidence x Ease) and say which you would start with and what the score does not capture.
| Request | Impact | Confidence | Ease |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: monthly attrition dashboard refresh | 8 | 6 | 4 |
| B: fix duplicate employee IDs in the HRIS export | 5 | 9 | 8 |
| C: build a pay-band analysis for the CHRO | 9 | 3 | 5 |

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); ICE scoring appears in prioritisation guides (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you can apply a scoring method and also see its limits.
**Say this in the interview:** "B scores highest at 360, so I would start there, but I would check whether a deadline or the CHRO's seniority changes the order."

**What the data shows:** The cheap, reliable fix (B) scores highest; the high-impact but uncertain and harder request (C) scores lowest.
**Working:**
1. A: 8 x 6 x 4 = 192.
2. B: 5 x 9 x 8 = 360.
3. C: 9 x 3 x 5 = 135.
4. Ranking: B (360), A (192), C (135).
**Result:** Start with B. All scores are unitless points; out of a maximum of 1,000.
**Caveats / what else I would check:** The score ignores deadlines (a CHRO meeting could make C urgent), dependencies (the duplicate-ID fix may be needed before A and C are trustworthy, which strengthens B), reach (how many people benefit), strategic fit, and the fact that scores are subjective guesses. Low Confidence on C also suggests a quick scoping step could raise it. Multiplying also lets one low number sink an item.
**Next step / recommendation:** Do B first, schedule A, and do a 1-hour scoping call on C to raise confidence and clarify the date. Review scores with stakeholders for agreement.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why multiply, not add?* Multiplying punishes any single weak factor. 2) *When would you override the score?* A hard deadline, a compliance issue or a dependency.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating scores as facts; ignoring deadlines; not telling the requester whose item waits.

### Q10 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You discover an error in a report that went to the leadership team last week, and a decision may already have used it. What do you do, and in what order?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); wrong-data scenarios lead many question banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, speed, impact assessment and a calm correction process.
**Say this in the interview:** "I confirm the error, size its impact, tell my manager straight away, then send a clear correction to the people who used it. I would rather correct early than be found out."

**First, clarify / contain:** Re-run to confirm it is really an error and not a misreading. Stop further use: pause any scheduled sends of the same report. Do not quietly fix and re-send.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. What exactly is wrong (formula, filter, source, date range)?
2. How big is the error, and which numbers change?
3. Who received it and which decision used it?
4. Does the corrected figure change the conclusion?
5. Root cause, so I can say it will not repeat.
**If I find...:** Conclusion unchanged: send a short correction note. Conclusion changes: tell my manager first, then the decision owner personally, with the corrected figures. Other reports share the cause: check and fix them too.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I found an error in last week's attrition report: leavers from the notice-period group were counted twice. The corrected rate is X, not Y. This changes/does not change the recommendation. I am checking the other reports from the same file and will confirm by 5 pm."
**Prevent it next time:** Peer review for leadership reports, reconciliation checks against a known total, and version notes.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you wait for the root cause?* No, flag it now; root cause follows. 2) *What if it is embarrassing?* Still disclose; owning it builds trust.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Silent fix; blaming the data team; over-apologising with no corrected numbers.

### Q11 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 HR and Finance define "headcount" differently and both want their dashboard first. How do you prioritise and resolve the conflict?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you resolve the definition conflict before building, and not by picking a side.
**Say this in the interview:** "The real problem is two definitions, not two dashboards. I would document both, get them agreed or labelled, then build once from a shared source."

**First, clarify / contain:** I write down each definition side by side. For example, HR may count active employees including contractors and those on leave at month-end, Finance may count paid full-time equivalents (FTE). I do not build either dashboard yet.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Exact definition, source table and cut-off date for each side.
2. How much the two numbers differ and why (contractors, notice period, part-time, leave).
3. Which decisions each team makes with its number.
4. Deadlines for each dashboard.
5. Who can arbitrate (CHRO and CFO, or a data governance forum).
**If I find...:** Differences are explainable: build one dashboard showing both, named clearly ("HR headcount", "Finance FTE") with a reconciliation bridge. Teams want one number: escalate for a single agreed definition. Deadlines differ: the earlier hard date goes first.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "Your numbers differ by N because of contractors and part-timers. I propose one dashboard with both labelled, and a one-page definition note. I need a 30-minute meeting with both of you this week."
**Prevent it next time:** A shared metric dictionary with an owner for each definition.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who wins?* Neither; the definition owner agrees what is used where. 2) *Which dashboard first?* The one with the hard deadline, built on the agreed definition.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Building two conflicting dashboards; taking sides.

### Q12 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A stakeholder asks you to leave out one inconvenient region from a presentation because it "spoils the story". What do you do?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); data-integrity dilemmas appear on prep sites (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Integrity under pressure, with tact.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would not drop it silently. I would explain the risk of leaving it out, offer to show the region with context, and escalate if they still insist."

**First, clarify / contain:** I ask why they want it removed. Sometimes there is a legitimate reason (data is incomplete, region is out of scope). I do not change the deck until I understand.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Is the region's data wrong or incomplete?
2. Is it genuinely out of scope for this audience?
3. Would omission mislead the decision?
4. Can I explain the region (one-off event, small sample)?
5. Does the audience already know the region's numbers?
**If I find...:** Data is faulty: fix or footnote it. Out of scope: state the scope clearly on the slide. Omission would mislead: keep it, with context. Pressure continues: raise with my manager.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "If we leave out the East region, someone may ask and it may look like we hid it. I suggest we include it with a one-line reason (two plant closures this quarter) and a plan. Would that work?"
**Prevent it next time:** Agree scope and inclusion rules before building the story.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the stakeholder is senior?* Same principle; escalate respectfully. 2) *Is any exclusion fine?* Yes if scope is stated openly.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Quietly deleting; lecturing about ethics.

### Q13 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Halfway through a project, the stakeholder keeps adding new cuts and metrics. How do you handle this scope creep?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you control scope without damaging the relationship.
**Say this in the interview:** "I welcome the ideas, but I show their cost: each addition moves the date or pushes something out. Then we agree what goes into this version and what into the next."

**First, clarify / contain:** I restate the original goal and the agreed scope in writing. I do not just absorb the additions.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Does each addition serve the original decision?
2. Effort in hours and data availability for each.
3. Impact on the deadline.
4. Whether it can be a phase 2.
5. Whether the stakeholder is unsure of the real question.
**If I find...:** Small and relevant: add it, note the cost. Large: phase 2 or swap with something. Many unrelated asks: return to the question, "what decision will this help?"
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "These four new cuts add about two days. We can include them and move delivery to next Wednesday, or ship the agreed version Monday and add them as version 2. Which do you prefer?"
**Prevent it next time:** Written scope sign-off at the start, a change log, and an early prototype so ideas surface sooner.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you ever say no?* I say "not in this version". 2) *How do you spot creep early?* Compare requests to the original objective.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Silently absorbing, then missing the deadline; refusing everything.

### Q14 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You have 40 working hours this week, 12 of them already committed to recurring reports and data fixes. The open requests are listed below. Which combination uses the free hours exactly, and what do you say to the requester whose work is left out?
| Request | Estimated hours |
|---|---|
| Attrition deep-dive for a plant head | 14 |
| Hiring-funnel report for Talent Acquisition | 10 |
| Overtime audit for Payroll | 8 |
| Survey summary for the CHRO | 6 |

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Capacity arithmetic plus honest communication about what is dropped.
**Say this in the interview:** "I have 28 free hours. Attrition, overtime and survey add up to exactly 28, so the hiring funnel waits, and I would tell Talent Acquisition the new date straight away."

**What the data shows:** Requests total 38 hours against 28 free, so not all can fit.
**Working:**
1. Free hours: 40 - 12 = 28.
2. Total requested: 14 + 10 + 8 + 6 = 38, which is 10 over.
3. Checked every combination: only 14 + 8 + 6 = 28 matches exactly. Others: 14 + 10 = 24, 14 + 10 + 6 = 30 (over), 14 + 10 + 8 = 32 (over), 10 + 8 + 6 = 24 (4 spare).
**Result:** Do attrition (14), overtime audit (8) and survey (6) = 28 hours; leave the hiring funnel (10 hours).
**Caveats / what else I would check:** Estimates are guesses and need a buffer; an exact fit leaves no slack, so in practice I would keep a few hours spare and accept slippage. Deadlines and impact matter more than a perfect fit: if the hiring funnel has a hard date or a bigger impact, swap it for the attrition work. Pure hour-matching is a puzzle, not a real policy.
**Next step / recommendation:** Confirm priorities with my manager, then message Talent Acquisition.
**What I would tell the requester:** "I can start your hiring-funnel report next week, about 10 hours. This week's capacity went to work with earlier dates. If you need a quick headline by Friday, I can send one number, or we can ask my manager to reprioritise."
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the plan overruns?* I flag early and cut scope. 2) *Would you work extra hours?* Not as a default; I would raise it.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Forgetting the 12 committed hours; planning at 100% utilisation.

### Q15 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 The CEO asks for a headcount figure within the hour, but you have not finished validating the source file. What do you send, and how?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Speed with honesty: provisional, labelled, with a firm follow-up.
**Say this in the interview:** "I send my best current figure clearly labelled provisional, with the main caveat and a time for the confirmed number. I do not hold it back and I do not present it as final."

**First, clarify / contain:** Check which headcount definition they mean (all employees, permanent only, at month-end) and what it is for. A two-line reply, not a long report.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Quick reconcile: total against last month's number and payroll count.
2. Duplicates and obvious blanks (a distinct count of employee IDs).
3. Status fields: leavers and joiners in the period.
4. Which checks are still outstanding.
5. Reasonable range, e.g. the figure could move by about 1 to 2%.
**If I find...:** Figure is stable across checks: send with a small caveat. A big gap versus payroll: send the figure with a wider range and say it is unresolved. Cannot even give a range: say so honestly with a time.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "Provisional headcount as of today is 4,820 (permanent employees), pending validation of 40 records. I expect the final to be within about 1% and will confirm by 4 pm." (All figures here are illustrative.)
**Prevent it next time:** A validated headcount snapshot kept up to date daily, so quick answers are safe.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the final differs?* Send a correction promptly with the reason. 2) *Why not wait?* The CEO needs something now; the label manages risk.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Sending an unlabelled number; refusing to answer; over-precise figures.

### Q16 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A colleague wants to share a dashboard that shows salary and performance ratings with all people managers. What do you check before agreeing?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Access control thinking: who needs to see what, at what level of detail.
**Say this in the interview:** "I would check approval and need first. Managers should see only their own team, with the minimum detail, and I would involve the data owner before anything is shared."

**First, clarify / contain:** Pause the sharing. No access is granted until approval and purpose are clear.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. Who owns the data and has approved sharing (HR head, compensation)?
2. Purpose: what decision does each manager need salary and ratings for?
3. Row-level security: each manager sees only their direct reports.
4. Whether individual salary is needed or bands and aggregates would do.
5. Small groups: a team of two can identify individuals even in aggregates.
6. Whether download, export and screenshots are controlled, and whether access is logged.
7. Policy and legal basis (from memory, unverified, confirm with legal).
**If I find...:** No approval: stop and escalate. Need is real: share restricted views. Aggregates are enough: use bands. No row-level security: do not publish.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I suggest each manager sees only their team, with pay bands instead of exact salary, once the HR head approves. I can set this up by Thursday."
**Prevent it next time:** Role-based access documented for each dashboard, and periodic access review.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What is row-level security?* A rule so each viewer sees only the rows they are entitled to. 2) *Who approves?* The data owner.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Sharing to everyone for convenience; assuming internal means safe.

### Q17 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 A requester asks for a weekly report you believe nobody will act on. How do you handle it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you test value respectfully with evidence instead of refusing or building blindly.
**Say this in the interview:** "I ask what decision it supports, check whether anyone uses it, and propose a lighter or less frequent version if the value is low."

**First, clarify / contain:** Ask the requester: "what will you do differently if this number moves?" I do not decline outright.
**What I would check, in order:**
1. The decision or action the report feeds.
2. Who reads it (view or open counts, if available).
3. Effort per week (hours).
4. Whether a monthly summary or alert would suffice.
5. Whether a dashboard they can self-serve would be better.
**If I find...:** No decision tied to it: propose stopping or monthly. Decision exists: build it and agree a trigger threshold. Unclear: run it for four weeks and review usage.
**What I would tell the stakeholder:** "I will set this up. To make it useful, can we agree what level would trigger action? Let me also check readership after a month; if it is not used, we can drop it to monthly."
**Prevent it next time:** Review recurring reports quarterly and retire unused ones.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they insist?* Build the cheapest version, and revisit with data. 2) *How do you measure use?* Opens, replies, decisions referenced.

### Q20 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is data minimisation, and how does it change your response when someone asks for the full employee export "just in case"?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you apply a privacy principle in a real request.

**Explain simply:**

### Q21 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the difference between anonymised and pseudonymised data, and which one is a name-masked employee list with employee IDs kept?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); privacy-basics questions appear in prep-site ethics banks (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know masking a name is not the same as making data safe, and that you will not call it "anonymous" by mistake.
**Say this in the interview:** "A name-masked list with employee IDs kept is pseudonymised, not anonymised, because the ID can be linked back to a person. I would treat it as personal data."
**Explain simply:**
- **Anonymised:** no one can reasonably work out who a record belongs to, even by combining it with other data. The link back is gone for good. Example: a count of leavers by department with no individual rows.
- **Pseudonymised:** direct identifiers (name) are replaced by a code, but a key or lookup table (ID to person) still exists somewhere, or the record can be matched with other data. Example: the HRIS export with names blanked but employee ID, joining date and grade kept.
- The masked list is therefore **pseudonymised**. Anyone with access to the HRIS can look up the ID in seconds. Even without the ID, joining date plus grade plus location can single out one person in a small team.
- Why it matters: laws such as GDPR and India's DPDP Act generally still treat pseudonymised data as personal data, so the same care applies. From memory, unverified, confirm with legal.
**Practice step:** Take a 5-row sample and ask "could a colleague guess who this row is?" If yes, it is not anonymous.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How would you make it anonymous?* Drop the ID, group ages and dates into bands, report only aggregates, and hide small groups. 2) *Is pseudonymisation useless then?* No, it lowers risk and is good practice; it just does not remove the duty of care.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying "I removed the names so it is anonymous"; forgetting that rare combinations (one female in a senior Legal grade) identify people.

### Q22 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What is the RICE scoring method, and when would you use it instead of ICE for an analytics backlog?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); RICE and ICE are standard in product-management guides and vendor blogs (unverified).
**What the interviewer is checking:** That you know a simple, defensible scoring method and when the extra factor is worth it.
**Say this in the interview:** "RICE scores each request as Reach times Impact times Confidence, divided by Effort. I use it instead of ICE when the number of people affected differs a lot between requests."
**Explain simply:**
- **Reach:** how many people or decisions are touched in a period (employees, managers, orders).
- **Impact:** how much it moves things for each of them, on a small scale such as 1 to 3.
- **Confidence:** how sure you are of your Reach and Impact guesses (as a percentage). It stops a hopeful idea from beating a proven one.
- **Effort:** person-weeks to deliver. Bigger effort lowers the score.
- **ICE** is Impact, Confidence, Ease, with no Reach. It is quicker and fine for a small backlog where everything touches about the same audience.
- Use RICE when requests differ in audience: a payroll fix for 300 people versus a roster study for 1,200. Use ICE when you are short of time or have no reach data.
- Scores are a conversation starter, not a verdict (see Q29 for overrides).
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Who sets the numbers?* I propose them, then check Reach and Impact with the requester and Effort with whoever builds it. 2) *Is a high score always first?* No; legal deadlines and blockers can override.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating the score as exact; scoring Impact on different scales per request; ignoring Confidence.

### Q23 ⭐⭐ 🟢 A weekly report takes you 3 hours to prepare by hand, and automating it would take about 20 hours. After how many weeks does the automation pay back, and would you automate it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); gap-fill exercise (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Simple payback arithmetic plus judgement about whether the report will live long enough and be correct enough to automate.
**Say this in the interview:** "20 hours divided by 3 hours a week is 6.7 weeks, so it pays back in 7 weeks. If the report will run for months, I automate it, after checking it is still wanted."
**Explain simply:**
- Payback = build time / time saved per run = 20 / 3 = 6.67 weeks, so about **7 weeks** (21 hours saved by then, rounded up to a whole run).
- Over a year (52 weeks): 52 x 3 = 156 hours by hand, versus 20 to build; about 136 hours saved before maintenance.
- Real payback is a little longer because automated reports need upkeep (a changed column, a new filter) and a check that the numbers still match; I would budget a few hours a quarter and re-check.
- Before building, confirm: will the report run at least a few months, are the source and logic stable, and does anyone actually read it (see Q17)? If the report may be dropped in a month, do not automate.
- Decision: automate, because the payback is short, and tell the requester the manual hours return to the team for analysis.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the build is 80 hours?* 80 / 3 = 26.7 weeks (about 27); still worth it for a long-lived report, not for a short one. 2) *What is the risk?* Silent errors; add row-count and total checks.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Ignoring maintenance; automating a report nobody uses; not validating the first automated output against the manual one.

### Q28 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Three analytics projects are scored with RICE (Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort). Compute the scores, pick the first project, and say how you would break the tie between the other two.
| Project | Reach (employees) | Impact (1-3) | Confidence | Effort (person-weeks) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X: exit-interview theme summary | 500 | 2 | 80% | 4 |
| Y: shift-roster optimisation study | 1,200 | 1 | 50% | 3 |
| Z: payroll-error root-cause check | 300 | 3 | 100% | 2 |

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); RICE exercise is a gap-fill (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Correct arithmetic, a clear pick, and sensible tie-breaking beyond the score.
**Say this in the interview:** "Z scores 450, X and Y tie at 200. Z goes first; between X and Y I would look at confidence and risk, not flip a coin."
**What the data shows:** Z is small in reach but high impact, certain, and cheap, so it wins by a wide margin. X and Y land on the same score from very different shapes.
**Working:**
1. X: 500 x 2 x 0.80 / 4 = 800 / 4 = 200.
2. Y: 1,200 x 1 x 0.50 / 3 = 600 / 3 = 200.
3. Z: 300 x 3 x 1.00 / 2 = 900 / 2 = 450.
**Result:** Z = 450, X = 200, Y = 200 (units: reach-impact points per person-week). Pick Z first (about 2.25 times the next).

**Next step / recommendation:** Start Z now. For the X versus Y tie: X has the higher confidence (80% versus 50%), so the benefit is more likely real; Y has lower effort (3 versus 4 weeks) but its low confidence suggests a 1-week feasibility check first. I would take X next, and run the small Y check if capacity allows. Revisit the order after Z is done.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not Y, with the biggest reach?* Its impact per person is small and I am only half sure of it. 2) *Could you change the Impact scale?* Yes, but fix it for all requests before scoring.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Dividing by Effort in the wrong place; using confidence as 80 instead of 0.80 (check the units); treating a tie as meaningless.

### Q29 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Requests are scored on weights Impact 50%, Urgency 30% and Ease 20% (each scored 1 to 10). Compute the weighted scores and choose; then say when you would override the score.
| Request | Impact | Urgency | Ease |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 8 | 5 | 6 |
| B | 6 | 9 | 4 |
| C | 7 | 6 | 9 |

**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); weighted-score exercise is a gap-fill (not from a source).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Weighted-average arithmetic, and the judgement to treat the score as a guide.
**Say this in the interview:** "C scores 7.1, A 6.7, B 6.5, so C first by the model; but the scores are close, and I would override for a hard deadline or a risk to people."
**What the data shows:** The three are within 0.6 points of each other, so the ranking is fragile.
**Working:**
1. A: 0.5 x 8 + 0.3 x 5 + 0.2 x 6 = 4.0 + 1.5 + 1.2 = 6.7.
2. B: 0.5 x 6 + 0.3 x 9 + 0.2 x 4 = 3.0 + 2.7 + 0.8 = 6.5.
3. C: 0.5 x 7 + 0.3 x 6 + 0.2 x 9 = 3.5 + 1.8 + 1.8 = 7.1.
**Result:** C = 7.1, A = 6.7, B = 6.5 (out of 10). Choose C, then A, then B.
**Caveats / what else I would check:** A gap of 0.4 to 0.6 is within the noise of my own 1-to-10 guesses. Different weights could change the order: with Urgency at 50 percent and Impact at 30 percent, B would lead (6 x 0.3 + 9 x 0.5 + 4 x 0.2 = 7.1 versus C 6.9, A 6.1). So I would test sensitivity before presenting.
**Next step / recommendation:** Override the score when: there is a legal, audit or pay-date deadline (B's urgency may be a real hard stop); a request blocks several other people; the issue risks harm to employees or compliance; or the score rests on guesses I can cheaply improve. Always tell the stakeholders when I override, and why.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why weight Impact highest?* It reflects value; the weights should be agreed with leadership. 2) *Do the weights sum to 100%?* Yes: 50 + 30 + 20.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Treating 6.7 versus 6.5 as a real difference; changing weights after seeing the result to favour a pet request.

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 An attrition-risk model is 90% accurate on a company where 10% of employees leave. Show why that number can be misleading and how you would present it to HR.

**Say this in the interview:** "A model that predicts 'nobody leaves' is also 90% accurate and finds zero leavers. I would report how many real leavers it catches and how many of its flags are right, not accuracy alone."

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) Which metric would you use? Recall and precision, plus a lift view (how much better than random). 2) What is a false positive here? An employee flagged who would have stayed; treat flags as conversation starters, not verdicts.

### Q53 ⭐ 🟡 What does Goodhart's law mean for an analyst, and how would it apply to a call-centre team measured on average handle time?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you understand that targets distort behaviour and know how to protect against it.
**Say this in the interview:** "When a measure becomes a target, people optimise the number and it stops reflecting the goal. If agents are judged on short calls, they will rush or end calls, and customers will call back."

**Explain simply:**
- A metric is a proxy for something we care about (good service).
- Once pay or praise is tied to the proxy, behaviour shifts to the proxy.
**Worked mini-example (illustrative):** Average handle time falls from 8 to 6 minutes, a 25% drop. But if repeat calls on the same issue go up and satisfaction falls, service got worse while the target improved.
**What I would do:** Pair the measure with balancing metrics: first-contact resolution, repeat-call rate, customer satisfaction and quality audit scores. Review the metric set regularly and look for sudden unnatural jumps.
**Practice step:** For any KPI you own, list two ways someone could improve it without improving the real goal.
**Likely follow-ups:** 1) So should we drop handle time? No; keep it as a monitoring measure, not the only target. 2) Another example? Hiring speed target leading to weaker hires.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Quoting the law without an example; proposing to remove all targets.
