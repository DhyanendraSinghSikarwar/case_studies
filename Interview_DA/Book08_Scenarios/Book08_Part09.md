### Q1 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How did you learn to handle scenario-based questions such as data quality problems, vague stakeholder requests and metric drops, and how long did it take you?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep-site guides (unverified) say scenario questions appear at several interview stages.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you learned deliberately, can be honest about effort, and have a repeatable method.
**Say this in the interview:** "I learned it in stages over about ten weeks at roughly six to seven hours a week: first a fixed answer routine, then practice on data I already understood from HR work, then timed mocks aloud." (Template: replace every figure with your true one; say it only if true.)
**Explain simply:**
- Be truthful. The timeline below is an illustration, not a claim.
- A believable story has stages: (1) learn one routine (clarify, check, decide, communicate), (2) practise on the three common types: data quality, vague request, metric drop, (3) use real HR files (attendance, payroll, hiring funnel), (4) mock rounds with feedback.
- Mini-example: 10 weeks x 6.5 hours = 65 hours. Say what those hours contained, not just the total.
- Add one proof: "My first answers rambled for four minutes; now I give a headline in 20 seconds."
- Practice step: write your real timeline in four lines (what, how long, what changed) and say it in under a minute.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Are you still learning?* Yes, I review one weak answer a week. 2) *What would you change?* Start mocks earlier.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Claiming you were always good at it; inventing hours; listing courses without saying what you learned.

### Q2 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 What does a good answer to a "what would you do" scenario question look like, and what steps do you follow every time?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep guides (unverified) stress a repeatable framework for open-ended cases.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you answer in a calm, ordered way that a manager could trust on a real problem.
**Say this in the interview:** "I follow the same five steps: clarify and contain, check in order, decide by rules, tell the stakeholder plainly, then prevent a repeat. I give my first move in the first 20 seconds."
**Explain simply:**
- A good answer is short, ordered, and honest about what is unknown. It is not a perfect solution.
- Steps: (1) clarify the goal and the decision, (2) contain risk (do not send or publish yet), (3) list checks from most likely and cheapest first, (4) say what you do for each finding, (5) say what you tell the stakeholder, with a time, (6) prevention.
- Mini-example: "Headcount on the dashboard looks wrong." First: ask which report and date, pause sharing it. Then check filters, then duplicates, then the source file date.
- Say assumptions aloud, for example "I assume the HRIS export is the source of truth."
- Practice step: answer one scenario a day with a 2-minute timer using these steps.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the interviewer interrupts?* Pause, answer, return to the step. 2) *Is there one right answer?* No, they score the method.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Jumping to SQL or a fix before clarifying; a vague "I would investigate"; no stakeholder message; no time frame.

### Q3 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 You come from an HR or non-technical background. How did you build the on-the-job judgement that analyst scenario questions test, when you have not yet worked as a data analyst?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); typical of career-switcher screens.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can show transferable judgement with evidence, not just claim it.
**Say this in the interview:** "HR work already trained me in messy data, sensitive information and difficult stakeholders; I added analyst habits on top through projects and mocks." (Say only what is true for you.)
**Explain simply:**
- Map your HR experience to analyst situations: reconciling payroll and HRIS = data quality; a manager asking "why is attrition high?" = a vague request; confidentiality = ethics.
- Then add what you built deliberately: a portfolio project with dirty data, scenario drills, mock rounds.
- Mini-example: "In HR I found that two systems showed different joiner counts. I traced it to a cut-off date difference and documented it." Use only a real example, with no employee names or private details.
- Be clear about the gap: you have not been titled an analyst, and you are closing it with practice.
- Practice step: list five real HR situations and write the analyst skill each one shows.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What is still missing?* Large-scale SQL on production data; I practise on public datasets. 2) *Example of judgement?* One true story with a decision and result.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Pretending you worked as an analyst; apologising too much; sharing confidential employer data; vague claims without a story.

### Q4 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you practise explaining your analysis to a non-technical manager, and how do you know your explanation is getting better?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep guides (unverified) list stakeholder communication as a separate round at some product firms.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can turn numbers into a decision in plain words, and whether you improve with feedback.
**Say this in the interview:** "I practise a three-line summary: the answer, the reason with one number, and the action. I check progress by timing myself and asking a non-technical person to repeat it back."
**Explain simply:**
- Format: headline first, one supporting number, one recommended action, one caveat.
- Mini-example: "Attrition rose from 12% to 15% (3 percentage points), mostly in first-year staff in one site. I recommend reviewing onboarding there; I will confirm the exit reasons by Friday."
- Practice: explain a chart to a friend with no data background; record a voice note; delete jargon.
- Measures of improvement: length (target under 60 seconds), the friend can restate it, and fewer questions of "so what?".
- Practice step: do three explanations a week and keep the best and worst in a journal.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they push back?* Ask which number they doubt and show the source. 2) *What if the news is bad?* Lead with it plainly.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Starting with methods; using terms like "regression" without explanation; confusing percent with percentage points; no action.

### Q5 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you prepare real stories for behavioural scenario questions such as "tell me about a time the data was wrong" or "a stakeholder disagreed with your numbers" when you have limited work experience?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); prep guides (unverified) advise STAR stories (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and preparing "messiest dataset" and "persuading a stakeholder" stories.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you have genuine, specific, honest examples.
**Say this in the interview:** "I keep five short stories in STAR form, taken from my HR work, my portfolio project and my practice, and I label each as real work or project work."
**Explain simply:**
- Story sources: HR reporting errors, an audit or compliance report, a dashboard you built, a portfolio project, a volunteer or college project.
- Five themes: wrong data, disagreement, tight deadline, unclear request, a mistake you owned.
- Each story: situation (1 line), your task, your action (most of the time), result with a number if true, lesson.
- Mini-example: "Payroll count was 4 higher than HRIS; I traced it to leavers paid in the final cycle; I added a note to the monthly report." (Template; use only a true story.)
- Practice step: write each story in 6 lines and say it in 90 seconds.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Can I use a project?* Yes, say it was a project. 2) *What if you have no result?* State what you learned and changed.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Inventing stories; saying "we" for everything; no result; blaming others; sharing confidential details.

### Q6 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 Which project from your portfolio best shows scenario thinking, and how did you design it to include dirty data, a vague brief and a stakeholder question?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); sources (unverified) say candidates are asked to defend every project on the resume.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether your project has real judgement points, not just charts.
**Say this in the interview:** "My best one is a template like an attrition analysis: I deliberately started from an HRIS-style export with duplicates and blanks, a one-line brief from an imagined HR head, and a follow-up question that challenged my numbers." (Describe your own project; say it only if true.)
**Explain simply:**
- Design recipe: (1) pick a messy dataset, or inject realistic problems (duplicate IDs, mixed date formats, missing exit dates), (2) write the vague brief as the stakeholder would ("why are people leaving?"), (3) record your clarifying questions and assumptions, (4) write a one-page answer, (5) list a challenge question and your reply.
- Show a before and after: raw rows, cleaning log, final metric definition.
- Mini-example: the cleaning log says 3 duplicates removed from 1,000 rows (0.3%).
- Practice step: write one page titled "assumptions and what I would ask".

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What would you do differently?* One honest improvement. 2) *Was the stakeholder real?* Say plainly if simulated.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Claiming a real stakeholder for a self-made project; showing only the polished dashboard; no cleaning evidence.

### Q7 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 What is your weekly routine for practising scenario questions, and how many hours do you spend on each activity?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Discipline and a realistic, structured plan.
**Say this in the interview:** "About six and a half hours a week: three one-hour drills, a two-hour mock on Saturday, and an hour and a half of review on Sunday." (Illustrative; replace with your real routine.)
**Explain simply:**
- Mon, 1 hour: clarifying-question drill on two vague requests.
- Wed, 1 hour: metric-drop or data-quality scenario, written out.
- Fri, 1 hour: story practice and explaining a chart in plain words.
- Sat, 2 hours: timed mock with a friend, or a recorded solo mock.
- Sun, 1.5 hours: review, update the case journal, fix one weakness.
- Check: 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1.5 = 6.5 hours per week.
- Practice step: put these blocks in your calendar and track completion.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you miss a week?* Do the mock and journal only. 2) *How do you stay motivated?* Track one measurable gain.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Plans that need 20 hours and are dropped; only reading answers; no review step; hours with no output.

### Q8 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 You are asked a scenario question and you do not know the answer. What do you say and do in the next two minutes?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); guides (unverified) advise "I don't know, but here is how I would approach it".
**What the interviewer is checking:** Honesty, composure and the ability to reason without the answer.
**Say this in the interview:** "I have not seen this exact case, but here is how I would approach it." Then clarify the goal and walk through the checks.
**Explain simply:**
- Seconds 0 to 15: say honestly what you do not know; do not bluff.
- 15 to 60: ask one or two clarifying questions and state the goal in your own words.
- 60 to 120: lay out a plan: what data, what first check, what decision rule. Say which part you are unsure about.
- Mini-example: unfamiliar term "churn cohort": "I assume it means a group of customers who joined in the same month; correct me if not."
- Offer to verify: "I would confirm that with the data owner."
- Practice step: have a friend throw unfamiliar terms at you and practise the 2-minute recovery.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they offer a hint?* Use it and say so. 2) *Would you guess a number?* Only as a labelled assumption.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Silence; bluffing with jargon; saying "I give up"; answering a different question.

### Q9 ⭐⭐⭐ 🔴 How would you rate your scenario and problem-solving skills out of 10, and which one weakness are you working on?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Self-awareness and evidence, with a real improvement plan.
**Say this in the interview:** "About a 6 out of 10: strong on structure and clarifying questions, weaker on speed with messy numbers. I am fixing that with timed drills." (Illustrative; use your honest rating.)
**Explain simply:**
- Avoid 10 (not believable) and a very low score. A switcher with evidence can say 6 or 7.
- Three parts: the number, two strengths with proof, one weakness with a plan and a measure.
- Mini-example: weakness "stakeholder pushback"; plan "two mocks a week where the friend disagrees"; measure "I stay calm and answer in under 90 seconds".
- Pick a real weakness that is not fatal to the job; not "I work too hard".
- Practice step: score yourself 1 to 5 on clarify, check order, decision, communication, prevention; the lowest is your weakness.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not higher?* Limited live experience. 2) *What would make it 8?* More varied scenarios and real stakeholder exposure.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Saying 10; naming a fake weakness; no plan; no evidence.

### Q10 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Which free resources and datasets have you used to practise realistic analyst scenarios, and why did you choose them?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you practise on realistic material and can justify choices.
**Say this in the interview:** "I used public datasets with realistic mess, plus my own mock HR files, and question banks for the interview side. I picked them because they have missing values, mixed formats and real business questions." (List only what you really used.)
**Explain simply:**
- Types of resources (names from memory, unverified; check each is still free): public data portals; Kaggle-style open datasets (HR attrition, retail sales, e-commerce orders); government open-data sites; free SQL practice sites; free interview guides.
- Why: messy, business-shaped data beats clean textbook tables.
- Self-made data: generate a fake attendance or payroll file with deliberate errors; no real employee data.
- Mini-example: use a sales file with 5% blank regions and ask "what would I tell the sales head?".
- Practice step: choose two datasets and write three scenario questions for each.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Why not just use a course?* Courses teach tools; scenarios need messy data. 2) *Any paid tools?* Not required.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Naming resources you never opened; using real confidential company data; only clean datasets.

### Q11 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do you practise asking clarifying questions before analysing, and what are the first five questions you ask for any vague request?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); client-style role plays in analyst guides (unverified) test this.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you define the problem before touching data.
**Say this in the interview:** "I always ask what decision this supports, who it is for, which definition and period, what data exists, and by when."
**Explain simply:**
- The five questions: (1) What decision will this support? (2) Who is the audience? (3) What exactly do you mean by the metric (definition, period, population)? (4) What data do we have and who owns it? (5) What is the deadline and format?
- Mini-example: "Show me attrition." Ask: voluntary only? Trailing 12 months? Including contractors? For the board or for a site head?
- Practice: take 10 one-line requests and write the five questions for each in 3 minutes.
- Do not ask more than about five before offering a first plan.
- Practice step: keep a card of the five questions beside your desk.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if they say "just do it"?* State your assumptions and send them for a quick confirmation. 2) *Too many questions?* Prioritise those that change the answer.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Interrogating without proposing; starting analysis first; accepting the metric name as the definition.

### Q12 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you build a bank of your own scenario questions from a dataset you already know, such as an attendance, payroll or sales file?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Initiative and whether you can generate realistic problems.
**Say this in the interview:** "I take a file I know and write questions from each scenario type: data quality, vague request, metric change, prioritisation and ethics."
**Explain simply:**
- Recipe for an attendance file: (1) data quality: "absence rates show 140% for one team", (2) vague request: "managers want to see who is unreliable", (3) metric change: "absence jumped 2 points in March", (4) prioritisation: three requests, one analyst, (5) ethics: "a manager asks for one named employee's records".
- Write the question, the expected checks, the decision, and the stakeholder script.
- Mini-example: aim for 5 types x 4 questions = 20 questions per dataset.
- Use anonymised or synthetic data only.
- Practice step: write ten questions this weekend and answer two.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you know they are realistic?* Compare with a mentor's real examples. 2) *How often do you refresh?* Monthly.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Questions with obvious answers; using real personal data; never answering your own questions.

### Q13 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you run a mock scenario interview with a friend or mentor, and what feedback checklist do you use afterwards?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); guides (unverified) recommend mocks aloud and peer feedback.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you seek and use feedback.
**Say this in the interview:** "I give my partner five scenarios, answer aloud against a timer, and score a six-point checklist afterwards."
**Explain simply:**
- Setup: 45 minutes, 3 scenarios, 8 minutes each, 5 minutes for feedback and a record of it.
- Ask the partner to interrupt and push back at least once.
- Checklist: (1) Did I clarify first? (2) Was the answer ordered? (3) Did I state assumptions? (4) Was there a decision rule? (5) Was the stakeholder message plain? (6) Did I finish on time and give a headline?
- Mini-example: score each item 0 or 1; a total of 4 of 6 means a pass at this stage.
- Practice step: schedule one mock a week and save the score.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *No partner available?* Record yourself and review. 2) *How do you act on feedback?* Pick one fix per week.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Friendly mocks with no pushback; no scoring; ignoring repeated feedback.

### Q14 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you track progress in scenario practice, and what self-check test tells you that you are ready for interviews?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you measure improvement rather than just count hours.
**Say this in the interview:** "I track mock scores, answer time and a miss log, and I treat myself as ready when I consistently pass a fresh, unseen scenario within the time limit."
**Explain simply:**
- Log: date, scenario type, checklist score out of 6, time taken, one fix.
- Readiness test (illustrative thresholds, set your own): on five unseen scenarios, score at least 5 of 6 on four of them, each under 6 minutes, with a headline in the first 30 seconds.
- Mini-example: scores 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6 over six weeks show a trend; hours alone do not.
- Watch for plateaus: one weak type (for example ethics) means more drills there.
- Practice step: start the log today and review it every Sunday.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if scores stall?* Change the method, not just the volume. 2) *Is a score enough?* Also ask for a mentor's view.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Counting hours only; repeating the same easy scenarios; no unseen test.

### Q15 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you learn from a scenario answer that went badly in a real interview or mock round?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Resilience and a learning loop.
**Say this in the interview:** "I write down what happened within an hour, find the root cause, rebuild a better answer, and retest it with a new scenario within a week." (Use a true example only.)
**Explain simply:**
- Step 1: note the question, my answer, and where it went wrong while memory is fresh.
- Step 2: find the cause: missed clarifying questions, wrong check order, nerves, or a knowledge gap.
- Step 3: write the better answer and say it aloud twice.
- Step 4: practise a similar new question to test the fix.
- Mini-example: "I jumped to SQL; the fix is to open with 'what decision is this for?'."
- Practice step: keep a "bad answers" section in your journal and review it monthly.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you email the interviewer?* Not needed; focus on the next round. 2) *How do you handle nerves?* Practise the opening line.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Blaming the interviewer; only feeling bad; fixing nothing; never retesting.

### Q16 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you practise making a recommendation under time pressure when the data is incomplete or messy?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Whether you can decide with imperfect data and state the risk.
**Say this in the interview:** "I give a recommendation with a confidence level, the main caveat, and what extra data would change my mind."
**Explain simply:**
- Drill: give yourself 10 minutes, a messy table, and one question; write a three-line recommendation.
- Format: recommendation, evidence in one number, risk, next check.
- Mini-example: "With 8% of exit reasons blank, I would say the main reason is pay, with moderate confidence; I will complete the blank cases by Friday."
- Separate what is known, assumed and unknown.
- Practice step: do one timed drill a week with a stricter clock each time (10, 8, 6 minutes).

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *When do you refuse to recommend?* If the data could mislead a high-stakes decision, say what is needed first. 2) *How do you show uncertainty?* A range or confidence word.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Waiting for perfect data; hiding the caveats; false precision.

### Q17 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do you stay current with the business context (retail, e-commerce, HR, finance) needed to answer scenarios well, without reading everything?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); guides (unverified) say business context matters in case rounds.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Curiosity that is focused rather than scattered.
**Say this in the interview:** "I keep it small: ten minutes a day on one industry, and I write the key metrics and drivers of each on one page."
**Explain simply:**
- One-page sheet per industry: how it makes money, the five main metrics, the common problems.
- Examples: HR (attrition, time to hire, offer-acceptance rate), retail (conversion, average order value, stock-outs), finance (default rate, collections), operations (turnaround time).
- Sources: company annual reports, business news, the interviewer's company website (check current info yourself).
- Before each interview spend 30 minutes on that company's business model.
- Practice step: build one one-pager per week for four weeks.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Which industry first?* The ones you are applying to. 2) *How do you avoid overload?* A fixed time limit.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Reading widely with no notes; memorising facts without metrics; ignoring the target company.

### Q18 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Design a 30-day plan to get ready for scenario rounds if you can give one hour on weekdays and three hours on weekends. What do you do each week?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the plan is a judgement-based template.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Realistic planning, prioritisation and a way to measure results.
**Say this in the interview:** "That is 11 hours a week, 44 hours over four weeks: week 1 method, week 2 core scenario types, week 3 timed mocks, week 4 polish and weak spots."
**Explain simply:**
- Hours: 5 weekdays x 1 hour + 2 weekend days x 3 hours = 11 hours a week; 11 x 4 = 44 hours.
- Week 1 (method): learn the five-step routine; weekdays clarifying questions and checks, weekends two full answers written out.
- Week 2 (types): data quality, vague request, metric drop, prioritisation, ethics; one each, with stakeholder scripts.
- Week 3 (speed): timed drills and two mocks aloud; start the case journal.
- Week 4 (polish): three stories, a project walkthrough under 5 minutes, one final mock, fix your weakest type.
- Exit check: a fresh scenario answered in under 6 minutes with a headline.
- Practice step: schedule all 4 weeks in your calendar today.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the interview is in 10 days?* Compress to the routine, three stories and two mocks. 2) *What do you drop first?* Industry reading.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Spending all four weeks on theory; no mocks until the end; no review.

### Q19 ⭐⭐ 🔴 Design a 90-day plan that takes you from zero scenario experience to being comfortable with data quality, stakeholder, dashboard, metric-change and project walkthrough questions.
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); the plan is a judgement-based template.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Structure, sequencing and realism over a longer horizon.
**Say this in the interview:** "Three phases of about 30 days: foundations, then each scenario type with real data, then interview simulation." (Illustrative: about 8 hours a week, 13 weeks, roughly 104 hours; adjust to your time.)
**Explain simply:**
- Days 1 to 30, foundations: answer routine, clarifying questions, basic SQL and Excel checks; one portfolio dataset cleaned with a log.
- Days 31 to 60, scenario types: weeks 5 and 6 data quality and stakeholders; week 7 dashboards (what to show, and why a number looks off); week 8 metric changes (decomposition by segment); one scenario written per type per week.
- Days 61 to 90, simulation: week 9 and 10 project walkthrough under 5 minutes plus deep follow-ups; weeks 11 to 13 weekly mocks, stories, and a final readiness test on unseen questions.
- Milestone each month: Day 30 a routine, Day 60 a portfolio project, Day 90 passing mocks.
- Check: 13 weeks x 8 hours = 104 hours.
- Practice step: write the three milestones and the date of each.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *How do you adjust?* Use the progress log monthly. 2) *Do you apply during the plan?* Start applying around day 60.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Learning tools endlessly; no portfolio output; no mocks; no milestones.

### Q20 ⭐ 🟢 How do you use a written decision log or case journal to improve your scenario answers over time?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a differentiator question (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Reflective practice and documentation habits.
**Say this in the interview:** "After each scenario I write the question, my answer, what I would change, and the rule I learned; I review it weekly."
**Explain simply:**
- Fields: date, scenario type, assumptions, decision, outcome or score, lesson.
- Over time you see patterns, for example "I forget to ask the deadline".
- Turn lessons into a short personal checklist of ten rules.
- The same habit applies on the job: a decision log helps later audits and handovers.
- Mini-example: entry "Metric drop: I checked the data pipeline first; next time check the definition change before anything else."
- Practice step: start a simple spreadsheet or notes file with those six columns.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *Do you share it?* Not with confidential information. 2) *How long does it take?* Five minutes per scenario.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Writing long entries you never reread; no lesson column; recording confidential data.

### Q21 ⭐ 🟡 How do you practise scenario thinking for ethics and prioritisation questions, where there is no single correct answer?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); a differentiator question (judgement).
**What the interviewer is checking:** Judgement, values and a clear reasoning method.
**Say this in the interview:** "For ethics I name the risk, protect the people and the data, escalate properly, and explain; for prioritisation I rank by impact, urgency and effort and state the trade-off."
**Explain simply:**
- Ethics steps: what is the harm, who is affected, what policy applies, who must approve, what safe alternative can I offer (for example aggregated data instead of a named list). Confirm the actual rules (for example data-protection laws) with the legal or compliance team; I do not state legal specifics from memory.
- Prioritisation: list requests, score each on impact and urgency, note the effort, agree the order with the requester's manager.
- Mini-example: three requests, one analyst: the payroll error affecting payment this week comes first; the monthly dashboard second; the nice-to-have analysis last, with dates communicated.
- Practice: write one dilemma a week and argue both sides, then choose.
- Practice step: answer aloud and say the trade-off in one sentence.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if the boss insists?* Escalate calmly to the data owner or compliance. 2) *Do you ever say no?* Yes, with an alternative.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Giving a quick yes; a preachy refusal; no alternative; deciding alone on sensitive data.

### Q22 ⭐ 🟡 How do you practise a project walkthrough so that it stays clear, honest and under five minutes, and how do you prepare for deep follow-up questions on it?
**Asked at:** commonly asked (no company-specific report found); sources (unverified) say candidates must defend every project on their resume.
**What the interviewer is checking:** Clarity, ownership and honesty about your role.
**Say this in the interview:** "I use a five-part structure: problem, data, method, result, and what I would improve, timed to four minutes, with a list of likely follow-ups prepared." (Use your own project.)
**Explain simply:**
- Structure with time: problem and user 45 sec, data and cleaning 60 sec, method and why 60 sec, result and action 60 sec, improvement 15 sec; total 4 minutes.
- Say exactly what you did yourself and what was provided.
- Prepare follow-ups: why this method, what were the data issues, what would you change, how did you check results, what was the effect.
- Know every number on your slides and where it came from.
- Practice step: record yourself twice, then trim to the time limit.

**Likely follow-ups:** 1) *What if you cannot recall a detail?* Say so and explain how you would check. 2) *Was it real?* State plainly if it was a practice project.
**Common mistakes / traps:** Tool-by-tool narration; claiming team results as your own; running over time; unknown numbers.

### Q23 ⭐ 🟡 How do you use AI tools to practise scenario questions without letting them write your answers for you?
