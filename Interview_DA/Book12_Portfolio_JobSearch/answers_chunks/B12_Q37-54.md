### Q37 ⭐⭐ 🟡 What is a guesstimate deck project and how do I build one? (Guesstimate technique: Book 7.)
**Short answer:** A guesstimate deck is a short slide deck (5 to 8 slides) that estimates one business number, such as "how many cups of chai are sold in Hyderabad daily", using clearly stated assumptions. It shows structured thinking, not exact answers.

**Why / explain simply:**
- A guesstimate is a reasoned estimate built from assumptions you can defend, because no exact data exists.
- Two methods: top-down (start with a big population and narrow it) and bottom-up (start from one unit, such as one shop, and scale up). Using both and comparing them is the sanity check.
- The deck is a portfolio piece because interviews for analyst roles sometimes include case or guesstimate questions; the technique itself is taught in Book 7 Part 1.
- Every base number in the deck is an assumption, and must be labelled as one.

**What the evidence says:** Guesstimates, cases and statistics appear in about 6 to 8 of roughly 20 interview profiles (IP Section 4, candidate-reported, anonymous). Whether a guesstimate deck helps a recruiter decide: No reliable data found. The outline treats it as a differentiator, not a must-have (A12a sources, our judgement).

**What to do:**
1. Pick one question (for example, "How many people ride the Hyderabad metro on a weekday?").
2. Slide 1: question and scope. Slide 2: assumptions table (number, source or reason, confidence).
3. Slides 3 and 4: top-down and bottom-up calculations. Slide 5: compare them and explain the gap.
4. Slide 6: sanity check and what data would improve it. Optional last slide: limitations.
5. Put the deck as a PDF in your GitHub repository or LinkedIn Featured section.

**Example:** (illustrative) An HR person estimates "new joiners a mid-size company in Bengaluru must onboard per year" using headcount, attrition rate and growth, all flagged as assumed round numbers, then checks it against a bottom-up count of teams.

**Common mistakes:** Presenting assumed numbers as facts, quoting statistics from memory without a source, making 20 slides, hiding the arithmetic, and skipping the sanity check.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.3 (project 5); Book 7 Parts 1 and 8; Q44 for time boxing.

### Q38 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Interview question: "Did you follow a tutorial for this? What did you do yourself?" How do I answer honestly?
**Short answer:** Yes, say so plainly. Name which parts followed a tutorial and which parts you changed, chose or decided yourself. Honest and specific beats polished and vague.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Interviewers ask this because many beginner portfolios copy a tutorial exactly. They want to know whether you can work alone.
- Following a tutorial to learn a tool is normal. The problem is only claiming it as original work.
- "Yourself" can be small but real: a different dataset, a new question, your own KPI definitions, extra checks, a written recommendation.
- Be ready to re-create the key number by hand, because follow-up questions test exactly that.

**What the evidence says:** A project or resume walk-through appears in about 14 of 20 interview profiles (IP Section 4, candidate-reported, anonymous), so expect this question. How interviewers judge the answer: No reliable data found. Blog guidance only says to be clear on "I" versus "we" (A12a sources, unverified).

**What to do:**
1. Before the interview, write two lists: "followed a guide" and "my own decisions".
2. If everything followed a tutorial, rebuild it on a different dataset and change the question. Then you can answer honestly with a real difference.
3. Practise a 30-second answer aloud.

**Example:** (illustrative) You learned the dashboard layout from a video, but you chose the attrition definition, built the tenure bands and wrote the findings yourself.

**Say this in the interview:** "I followed a [tutorial/course] to learn [tool], then rebuilt it on [dataset] with my own question. The [KPI definitions / tenure bands / findings] are mine, and here is where I would still be unsure."

**Common mistakes:** Claiming everything as original, getting defensive, saying "we" for solo work, or being unable to explain a chart you built.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.5; Book 0 Q28; Book 8 Part 8; Q47 and Q39.

### Q39 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Interview question: "What are the limitations of your project and what would you do differently?"
**Short answer:** Name two or three real limits (data, method, scope), say how they affect the conclusion, and give one concrete next step for each. Saying "none" sounds untrained.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Every beginner project has limits: small or synthetic data, no cause proven, no live users.
- Naming limits shows judgement. Hiding them is the real red flag.
- "What would you do differently" can be technical (clean the data earlier, test the formula) or about the question (interview HR to ask what decision this supports).
- Correlation is not causation: a pattern in the data does not prove what caused it (Book 5 Part 7).

**What the evidence says:** Limitations and "what would you change" are listed among the follow-ups to prepare (A12a outline, Section 2.5, our checklist from blog guidance, unverified). Interviewer scoring: No reliable data found.

**What to do:**
1. In every README, add a "Limitations" section. Reuse it as your answer.
2. For each limit write: what it is, why it matters, what you would do next.
3. Pick one improvement you could genuinely do in a week.

**Example:** (illustrative) "The data is synthetic, so the overtime pattern may be designed, not real. With real data I would first check how leavers were recorded."

**Say this in the interview:** "The main limit is [data size/source]. So I would not claim [cause]. Next I would [test or collect X]."

**Common mistakes:** Claiming no limitations, blaming the tutorial, listing ten trivial limits, or promising advanced fixes (machine learning) you cannot explain.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.4 (README checklist) and 2.5; Q47, Q48.

### Q40 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Is a Kaggle dataset acceptable, and how do I cite the source and licence?
**Short answer:** Usually yes, if the licence allows reuse and you cite it. Read the licence on that dataset's own page, then record source, download date and licence in your README.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Kaggle is a site that hosts public datasets. Each dataset has its own licence, which says what you may do with it.
- A licence is the owner's permission rules. Some allow any use with credit; some limit commercial use or sharing.
- Citing means naming the dataset, author, link, date you downloaded it and the licence.
- Do not re-upload data to GitHub if the licence forbids it; link to it instead.

**What the evidence says:** The Kaggle licence field for the IBM HR attrition dataset was not retrieved; its licence terms are described as loosely specified in search snippets (A12a sources, snippet only, unverified). The Indian government portal licence (GODL, notified 2017 per secondary snippets) was not read directly. Check the live page for each dataset.

**What to do:**
1. Open the dataset page and find the licence field.
2. If it says unspecified or unclear, link to it instead of copying files.
3. Add to the README: name, author, URL, date downloaded, licence, and any changes you made.
4. Never mix in employer data.

**Example:** (illustrative) README line: "Data: [dataset name] by [author], Kaggle, downloaded [date], licence: [as shown on the page]. Not redistributed here."

**Common mistakes:** Copying the file into GitHub without checking, forgetting the date, citing only "Kaggle", and treating designed (synthetic) patterns as real-world causes.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.2; Q41; Book 0 Q100.

### Q41 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Can I generate my own synthetic data, and how do I disclose it?
**Short answer:** Yes, you can, and for an HR project it is often the safest choice. Say clearly in the README that the data is synthetic (made up) and show the rules you used to generate it.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Synthetic data is invented by you with code or Excel formulas, so no real person's data is involved. This avoids confidentiality problems.
- Disclosure means writing, at the top of the README, that the data is fake and how it was made.
- Because you wrote the rules, patterns in it prove nothing about the real world. Do not claim real causes.
- Never copy and lightly edit employer records and call it synthetic; that is still employer data.

**What the evidence says:** The public IBM HR attrition dataset is described in search snippets as fictional, with about 1,470 rows and 35 columns (A12a sources, snippet only; verify by loading the file). No reliable data found on how recruiters view synthetic versus real data. Decision rule: disclose, always.

**What to do:**
1. List 8 to 10 columns (employee ID, department, join date, exit date or blank, overtime, and so on).
2. Write generation rules (for example: random departments, a tenure range, leaving more likely in year one). Keep rules in a text file.
3. Generate 500 to 1,000 rows in Excel or Python.
4. Put "Synthetic data, generated by me, rules in rules.md" in the README.

**Example:** (illustrative) "Rule: 30% of leavers in the first 12 months, assumed for demonstration only."

**Common mistakes:** Not disclosing, presenting designed patterns as findings, using real names or phone numbers, and making data too clean.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.2; Q40, Q48.

### Q42 ⭐⭐ 🟡 Where should the portfolio live: GitHub profile, personal website, PDF or LinkedIn Featured section?
**Short answer:** Use GitHub as the main home, link it from your resume and LinkedIn Featured section, and keep a PDF or screenshots as a backup. A personal website is optional.

**Why / explain simply:**
- GitHub is a site for storing project files. Each project gets a repository (a project folder with a README, the page explaining it).
- LinkedIn Featured is a profile section where you pin links, so a recruiter can click straight from your profile.
- A PDF case summary works when someone will not open links.
- A website adds effort; only do it after the projects are done.

**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on how much recruiters in India value GitHub, websites or public dashboards (A12a sources). Fueler-type blogs suggest free public links (vendor/blog claim, unverified). Decision rule: fewest clicks to a working example.

**What to do:**
1. Create one GitHub repo per project with a clear README.
2. Pin 3 to 5 repos on your GitHub profile.
3. Add your GitHub link to the resume header and the best 2 projects to LinkedIn Featured.
4. Keep one PDF of your best project for email.

**Example:** (illustrative) A recruiter reads your resume, clicks one link, and sees a README with a screenshot and 3 findings in under a minute.

**Common mistakes:** Linking an empty profile, spreading projects across five places, publishing confidential data, and broken links.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.4; Q43; Book 0 Q100 and Q101.

### Q43 ⭐⭐ 🟡 I cannot use Power BI's publish-to-web option. How do I still show my Power BI project?
**Short answer:** Show it through a PDF export, screenshots, a short screen recording and the .pbix file on GitHub (if size and data licence allow). Never publish HR or confidential data to the web to make it viewable.

**Why / explain simply:**
- "Publish to web" makes a Power BI report viewable by anyone on the internet with no sign-in. It needs a licence and a tenant setting enabled by an admin (Microsoft Learn, snippet only; check the live page).
- A .pbix file is the Power BI project file. Others can open it only in Power BI Desktop.
- A PDF or screenshot lets anyone see the result without any account.
- A recording lets you demonstrate interaction, such as slicers and filters.

**What the evidence says:** What a free personal account can do: No reliable data found (A12a sources). The Microsoft warning against publishing confidential data came through snippets and community posts, wording revised July 2025 per those posts (unverified, check the live page).

**What to do:**
1. Use public or synthetic data only.
2. Export the report to PDF and take screenshots of each page.
3. Record a 1 to 2 minute screen video if you can (see Q45).
4. Upload the .pbix to GitHub only if the data licence allows; otherwise link the data.
5. Mention tool limits honestly in the README.

**Example:** (illustrative) README shows 4 screenshots, a PDF link and a note: "Interactive file available on request."

**Common mistakes:** Using work data in the file, assuming publish-to-web is private, and uploading huge files.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.4; Book 4 Part 7 (publishing); Q45.

### Q44 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How long should each project take, and how do I know when to stop polishing?
**Short answer:** Finish a first version, publish it, then improve. Stop when your README is complete and you can answer three follow-up questions about it.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Polishing has no end, but job applications have a deadline in your life.
- A published, honest project beats a perfect unpublished one.
- A simple stopping test: README done, key number reproducible by hand, three likely follow-ups answered.

**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on ideal project duration. The time boxes here are our suggestion, not sourced (A12a outline, Section 2.3). Blogs favour 3 good projects over 10 (vendor/blog claim, unverified).

**What to do:**
1. Set a budget per project, for example 1 to 2 weeks part-time (our suggestion; adjust to your pace).
2. Week one: build and publish. Week two: README, limitations, one improvement.
3. Use the stop checklist: problem stated, data cited, 3 findings, limitations, screenshots.
4. Then move on; revisit only after interview feedback.

**Example:** (illustrative) You spend three days perfecting colours on a dashboard but have no README. Stop, write the README, and apply.

**Common mistakes:** Starting five projects and finishing none, endless chart tweaking, and delaying applications until the portfolio feels perfect.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.3; Book 0 Q14; Q37.

### Q45 ⭐ 🟢 Should I record a 2-minute video walkthrough of each project?
**Short answer:** It is optional but useful, especially for Power BI projects you cannot publish. Keep it short, show real screens and speak plainly.

**Why / explain simply:**
- A recruiter may not open files, but may watch a short clip.
- A recording proves you built it, because you navigate it live.
- It helps when publishing is blocked (see Q43).
- Keep nothing confidential on screen, including browser tabs and file names.

**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on whether recruiters watch portfolio videos or whether they raise callbacks (A12a sources). The 2-minute length is a common suggestion, not a measured figure.

**What to do:**
1. Write a 5-line script: question, data, 2 or 3 clicks showing the main finding, limitation.
2. Record your screen with a free tool (any is fine), with your voice.
3. Upload it as unlisted or share a file link, and add it to the README.
4. Re-record if you stumble badly, but do not seek perfection.

**Example:** (illustrative) "This dashboard answers which tenure group leaves most. Here is the filter... The data is synthetic, so treat patterns as a demo."

**Common mistakes:** Videos over 5 minutes, reading the screen aloud word by word, showing private data, and skipping the limitation.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.4 and 2.5; Q43; Q42.

### Q46 ⭐ 🟡 Should I add an attrition-prediction (machine learning) model to the HR project? (ML detail: Book 9.)
**Short answer:** Not at first. Finish the descriptive dashboard and EDA, and add a simple model only if you can explain it plainly and it is honest about limits.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Descriptive analysis shows what happened (who left, when). A prediction model guesses who might leave. Machine learning (ML) means a program learns patterns from data.
- Interviews probe whatever you list. A model you cannot explain hurts you.
- Attrition prediction raises ethical points: predicting individuals can be unfair or misused. Treat it carefully.
- Entry-level JDs rarely name ML.

**What the evidence says:** ML appears in about 10% of JDs (MS Addendum, n=186 non-random sample, about +/-7 points). Excel appears in 60% and SQL in 54%. In interviews, SQL appears in nearly all of about 20 company profiles (IP Section 4, candidate-reported, anonymous); no source shows ML as a common entry-level test.

**What to do:**
1. Complete projects 1 to 3 first.
2. If you add a model, keep it simple (logistic regression), say it is a learning exercise and report limits.
3. Do not claim it would work in a real company.
4. Learn the detail in Book 9 Part 8.

**Example:** (illustrative) Adding a short "Extension" section to the README after the dashboard, clearly marked optional.

**Common mistakes:** Adding a model you cannot explain, quoting accuracy without context, ignoring imbalance and fairness, and replacing the dashboard with the model.

**Where this is covered:** Book 9 Part 8; Guide Section 2.3; Q47.

### Q47 ⭐ 🔴 Interview question: "How exactly did you calculate the attrition rate in your dashboard?" How do I defend the numbers?
**Short answer:** Define it in one sentence, show the formula and the data it used, and re-create it by hand from a few rows. Defending a number means being able to rebuild it.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Attrition rate is usually leavers in a period divided by average headcount in that period. Averages vary by company, so state your formula.
- Be clear about the period (monthly or annual) and who counts as a leaver (voluntary only or all exits).
- If your dataset has a yes/no leaver column, the share of "Yes" is a snapshot, not an annual rate.
- Interviewers use this question to test whether you understand your own dashboard.

**What the evidence says:** Project walk-throughs appear in about 14 of 20 interview profiles (IP Section 4, candidate-reported). The IBM sample is reported as about 237 "Yes" of 1,470 rows (search snippet, unverified; verify by loading the file). HR metric definitions: Book 10 Part 1.

**What to do:**
1. Write the formula in the README.
2. Take 10 rows and calculate by hand once.
3. Know the filters and slicers affecting the number.
4. Prepare for "what if data is wrong": check duplicates, blanks and dates.

**Example:** (illustrative) 12 leavers in a year, average headcount 120, so 12 / 120 = 10%. Round numbers for demonstration only.

**Say this in the interview:** "I defined attrition as [leavers] divided by [average headcount] for [period]. The number is [x] with [filter]. Let me show how I checked it."

**Common mistakes:** Dividing by end headcount without saying so, mixing voluntary and involuntary exits, and being unable to explain a slicer.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.5; Book 10 Part 1; Q38; Q39.

### Q48 ⭐ 🔴 What do I say if my project finds no clear pattern, or the dataset is synthetic and its drivers look unrealistic?
**Short answer:** Say so honestly. "No clear pattern" is a valid finding, and a designed (synthetic) dataset means patterns should not be claimed as real-world causes.

**Why / explain simply:**
- Real data often shows weak patterns. Reporting that is good analysis.
- Synthetic data patterns are built in by whoever made it, so they may look unrealistic.
- The skill being tested is judgement: what the data can and cannot support.
- Never invent a story to look impressive.

**What the evidence says:** The IBM HR dataset is described as fictional and its patterns as designed (A12a sources, snippet only, unverified). No reliable data found on how interviewers react to null results. Correlation versus causation: Book 5 Part 7.

**What to do:**
1. In the README, write the finding as "limited evidence for X".
2. Add what you checked: groups, time, missing data.
3. Suggest what extra data would help.
4. Frame the project as method practice.

**Example:** (illustrative) Overtime and attrition move together in the file, but the data is synthetic, so you present it as a demonstration of method.

**Say this in the interview:** "In this [synthetic/small] dataset I found [no clear pattern / this pattern]. I would not claim a cause. To test it I would need [data]."

**Common mistakes:** Forcing a story, hiding the synthetic origin, claiming causes, and claiming business impact that never happened.

**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 2.2 and 2.5; Q41; Q39.
