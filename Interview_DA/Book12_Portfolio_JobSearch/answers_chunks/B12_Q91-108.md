### Q91 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How can I tell from job descriptions whether a certificate is worth paying for in my target roles?
**Short answer:** Read 10 job descriptions (JDs) for the roles you actually want and count how many name a certificate, and whether as "required" or only as "preferred". If 0 to 2 name one, your money and hours are better spent on a project.
**Why / explain simply:**
- A JD is the employer's own wish list, so it is the nearest thing to evidence of what they screen for.
- "Required" is a filter. "Preferred", "good to have" or "advantage" is a tie-breaker at best.
- Words like "certifications" with no name tell you nothing specific; do not buy a certificate on that basis.
**What the evidence says:** No analytics certification was named in any of the 40 in-scope JDs of the first sample (MS Section 5). In the pooled sample of 186 JDs (non-random, about +/-7 points), no certification was required; Tableau or Power BI certificates, Google Data Analytics, the Microsoft Data Analyst Associate and CBAP appeared only as advantages (MS Addendum). Employer weight on certificates in India: No reliable data found.
**What to do:**
1. Open a sheet with columns: company, role, certificate named, required or preferred.
2. Fill it for 10 JDs from Naukri, LinkedIn or career pages for your target role.
3. Decision rule: 0 to 2 mention one, skip for now and build a project. 3 or more list it as preferred and you already have a finished project, a certificate is a reasonable add. Required in 3 or more, treat it as a filter for that type of employer.
4. Repeat after a month, because your target roles may change.
**Example:** (illustrative) Of 10 MIS and reporting JDs, 1 says "Power BI certification is an advantage" and 9 name only Excel, SQL and dashboards. You decide: project first, certificate maybe later.
**Common mistakes:** Counting "good to have" as required; reading only 2 JDs; paying first and checking JDs afterwards; copying a certificate list from a course ad.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 5; Q85, Q86 and Q88; Book 0 Q55.

### Q92 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What happens when PL-300 expires, and how do I renew it?

Rules change: check the live page.

**Example:** (illustrative) Your expiry date is 15 March. You set a reminder for 15 September, take the 45-minute assessment in the second week of the window, and update the date on your resume.

### Q94 ⭐ 🔴 Is it safe to use exam dumps, paid proxy test-takers or shortcut courses to get a certificate quickly?
**Short answer:** No. Dumps and proxy test-takers are cheating, risk the credential and your reputation, and leave you unable to do the work in a practical interview round. Do not use them.
**Why / explain simply:**
- An exam dump is a leaked set of real exam questions. A proxy test-taker is someone else sitting the exam in your name.
- Exam providers have candidate agreements that forbid this (from memory, unverified; check the provider's exam policy page). Penalties such as a cancelled result or a ban are possible.
- Interviewers for analyst roles ask you to explain a dashboard or write a query live. A certificate without the skill is exposed in minutes.
- "Guaranteed certificate" or "pass or refund" bundles are marketing; treat them as a warning sign.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on how often these practices are caught or penalised in India. No analytics certification was required in any sampled JD (MS Addendum), so the risk is also not worth the gain.
**What to do:**
1. Prepare the honest way: official free learning material and practice assessments from the provider (check the live page for what exists).
2. Build one real project while you study, so you can answer "how did you use this?".
3. If a course promises a certificate or job with no effort, apply the checks in Book 0 Q56 and Q57.
4. If you already bought something like this, stop and do not sit an exam with it.
**Example:** (illustrative) An advertisement offers "PL-300 passed in 3 days, pay after result". You say no, because passing for you by someone else is not your skill and may cost you the credential.
**Common mistakes:** Believing "everyone does it"; thinking it is a victimless shortcut; spending on shortcuts instead of one more project.

### Q97 ⭐⭐⭐ 🟡 How many applications should I send each week, and should I tailor each one or apply in bulk?

2. Match test: apply only if you can honestly cover most must-have tools in the JD.
3. For top-fit roles, adjust 3 resume lines to the JD (Q6, Q15; Book 0 Q83 for translating HR work).
4. Review the tracker after 4 weeks; if replies are low, change the resume before raising the volume.

**Short answer:** Silence after 10 applications is a signal to change the resume, not the target role. Check match, resume, profile and source, change one thing, and wait two weeks.

**What the evidence says:** The "10 applications, then change the resume" rule is Book 0's own rule of thumb, not sourced data. No reliable data found on typical reply rates for HR-to-analytics switchers.

1. Match: do your roles ask 0 to 2 years and tools you have? (Q108)

3. Profiles: do Naukri and LinkedIn say the same as the resume? (Q53, Q62)
4. Source: are all 10 from one board? Try referrals (Q67).
5. Make one change, apply to 10 more, compare reply rates (Q100).

### Q100 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do I calculate my response rate by source and resume version from my tracker? (example: 12 applications, 3 replies from Naukri, 1 from a referral)
**Short answer:** Response rate is replies divided by applications for each source or resume version. Add a Replied column (Yes or No) and use a COUNTIFS formula.
**Why / explain simply:**
- A reply means any human response: a call, an email or a rejection. Silence is No.
- COUNTIFS counts rows matching two or more conditions.
- With small numbers, one reply changes the percentage a lot, so treat results as hints.
**What the evidence says:** All numbers here are illustrative placeholders. No reliable data found on normal response rates, so there is no benchmark to compare against; compare only your own sources with each other.
**What to do:** Add a Replied column, then use these formulas (column letters are an example; Source in D, Resume in F, Replied in K):

```
=COUNTIFS(D:D,"Naukri")
=COUNTIFS(D:D,"Naukri",K:K,"Yes")
=COUNTIFS(D:D,"Naukri",K:K,"Yes")/COUNTIFS(D:D,"Naukri")
=COUNTIFS(F:F,"A",K:K,"Yes")/COUNTIFS(F:F,"A")
```

Illustrative summary of 12 applications:

| Source | Applications | Replies | Response rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naukri | 8 | 3 | 37.5% |
| LinkedIn | 2 | 0 | 0% |
| Referral | 1 | 1 | 100% |
| Career page | 1 | 0 | 0% |
| Total | 12 | 4 | 33% |

By resume (illustrative): version A 6 applications, 1 reply (17%); version B 6 applications, 3 replies (50%).
**Example:** (illustrative) The referral row is 1 of 1, which is too small to prove referrals always work. You send 5 more through referrals before you decide.
**Common mistakes:** Judging on 1 or 2 rows; not logging resume version; counting only calls and ignoring rejections.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 6; Q95 and Q99; Book 1 (Excel functions).

### Q101 ⭐⭐ 🟢 When and how should I follow up on an application, and how many times is too many?
**Short answer:** Follow up about 1 to 2 weeks after applying, politely and briefly, and stop after 2 to 3 attempts. Then record it as Ghosted and move on.
**Why / explain simply:**
- A follow-up is a short message that reminds the recruiter of your application and asks for the status.
- The 1 to 2 week gap and the 2 to 3 limit are a convention in career blogs, not data.
- Silence is often process delay, not rejection (Book 0 Q72).
**What the evidence says:** Follow-up norms of about 1 to 2 weeks and a maximum of 2 to 3 follow-ups come from vendor and career blogs (convention, unverified, A12c sources). No reliable data found on whether follow-ups raise response rates in India.
**What to do:**
1. In your tracker set Next action date to 7 to 10 days after applying.
2. Send the first follow-up to the named contact, or to the recruiter on LinkedIn or by email if you have one.
3. Wait another week for the second. A third, only if there is new information to share, such as a finished project.
4. After that, mark Ghosted (Q105).
**Example:** (illustrative message):

"Hello [Name], I applied for the [role] position on [date] and I am still very interested. My experience in [your true skill] fits [one JD point]. Could you tell me where the application stands? Thank you, [your name]."

**Common mistakes:** Daily messages; copy-pasting the same text to ten people at a company; angry tone; following up on a rejected application.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 6; Q69 and Q105; Book 0 Q72.

### Q103 ⭐⭐ 🔴 I am serving a notice period of 60 days and have received an offer: how do I handle the overlap, buyout and start date?
**Short answer:** Read your own contract first, tell the new employer your honest notice terms, and ask for a start date after your notice ends or an agreed early release. This is general information, not legal advice.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Notice period is the time you work after resigning. Its length is set by your contract.
- A buyout means paying the employer to leave early. Whether it is allowed is the current employer's decision.
- Some new employers reimburse a buyout; that is a negotiation, not a right.
- As an HR person you know your company's policy better than most; use that knowledge.
**What the evidence says:** Contract governs notice; buyout is at the employer's discretion; some employers reimburse buyouts; ask for a later start date (vendor/blog claims, unverified; A12c sources). One blog says Labour Codes leave notice to contract (unverified). Legal points are from memory, unverified: confirm with your HR or a lawyer. No reliable data found on buyout norms by company.
**What to do:**
1. Read: notice days, buyout clause, recovery clauses, relieving letter conditions.
2. Illustrative arithmetic for a buyout: days remaining x daily pay (monthly pay / 30), plus any dues; replace with your own figures.
3. Ask the new employer for a start date after day 60; ask early about buyout reimbursement before you resign.
4. Ask your manager about early release only when the offer is written.
5. Hand over properly and collect the relieving letter.
**Example:** (illustrative) Notice has 40 days left, monthly pay Rs 50,000: 40 x (50,000 / 30) is about Rs 66,667 before taxes and dues. You compare this with what the new employer might reimburse.
**Say this in the interview:** "My notice period is [60 days] per my contract. I would like to serve it with a proper handover; I will ask my employer about early release once I have a written offer."

### Q105 ⭐⭐ 🟢 What do I do when recruiters or companies never reply (ghosting)?

### Q106 ⭐ 🔴 How do I recognise and avoid fake job offers or recruiters who ask for money?

**What to do:**
1. Find the same role on the company's official careers page by typing the address yourself.
2. Check the recruiter's email domain matches the company; ask for a video call.
3. Never pay, share bank OTPs, or install remote-access apps.
4. Report and block, and tell colleagues who are searching.
5. See Q75 for consultancy fees.
**Example:** (illustrative) You get a WhatsApp offer for a "data analyst" role with no interview and a "refundable Rs 5,000 registration fee". You stop and report it.
**Common mistakes:** Trusting a company logo; paying "small" fees; sending ID documents early; feeling embarrassed and staying silent.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Sections 4 and 6; Q75 and Q84.

### Q107 ⭐ 🟡 How do I avoid burnout during a long job search while keeping a full-time job?

**Why / explain simply:**
- Burnout is exhaustion that makes you stop. A search can last months and the replies are slow.
- Working full time plus studying leaves little spare energy, so a smaller steady plan lasts longer.
- Weekly actions you control (applications sent, messages, practice) are better to track than replies you do not control.
**What the evidence says:** The advice is general career-centre guidance, not India data (A12c sources). No reliable data found on search duration or burnout for switchers.
**What to do:**
1. Keep one full rest day with no job search (Q96).
2. Set a daily cap, for example no job search after 9 pm (illustrative).
3. Track actions per week, and celebrate completing them.
4. Review each month: continue, change the plan, or pause for a week.
5. Tell one trusted person your plan; talk to a doctor or counsellor if stress affects sleep or health.
**Example:** (illustrative) Your plan was 3 hours every evening. After two weeks you drop to 1 hour on four evenings and a weekend block, and you keep it going for 8 weeks.
**Common mistakes:** All-or-nothing plans; checking email every hour; comparing yourself with posts on social media; no rest day.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 6; Q96; Book 0 Q52 and Q67.

### Q108 ⭐ 🟢 Should I apply to jobs that ask for 3 or more years of experience when I have none in analytics?

**Why / explain simply:**
- Experience in a JD is often a screening filter, and some filters are rigid.
- But labels are sometimes wide, for example "0 to 4 years", and some roles are open to strong candidates.
- Roles asking 3+ years tend to want more tools such as Python, cloud and ML (impression, not counted).
**What the evidence says:** In our JD research, roles needing 3+ years were left out of the in-scope tally (about 25 read); they mentioned Python, Power BI or Tableau, cloud data platforms and GenAI/ML more often than the 0 to 2 year set (impression only, not counted; MS observations under the JD tally). Experience labels in Hyderabad and Bengaluru sometimes ran wide (MS Addendum). Probability of an interview from a mismatch: No reliable data found.
**What to do:**
1. Rule: apply if the role asks 2 to 3 years or a range that starts at 0 or 1, and you match most must-have tools.
2. Skip roles that need 3 or more years plus tools you cannot show.
3. Use a project as proof, and keep the resume honest.
4. Mark these applications in your tracker and compare their reply rate (Q100).
**Example:** (illustrative) A posting says "2 to 4 years" and asks Excel, SQL and Power BI. You have projects in all three, so you apply and say so. Another says "5+ years, Python, Snowflake"; you skip it.
**Say this in the interview:** "I do not have [3 years] of analytics employment. I have [X years] of [HR reporting work] and projects in [tools], and I can show them."
**Common mistakes:** Inflating years; applying to every senior role; not reading the must-have list.
