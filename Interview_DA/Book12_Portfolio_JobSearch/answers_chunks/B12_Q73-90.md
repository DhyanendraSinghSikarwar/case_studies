### Q73 ⭐⭐ 🟢 How do I ask for a 15-minute advice chat without sounding like I only want a job?
**Short answer:** Ask two specific questions about their work, offer a 15-minute call at their convenience, and do not mention a job or a referral in the first message. Ask for a referral later, only if the chat goes well and a real opening exists.
**Why / explain simply:**
- People say yes to small, specific, easy requests. "Can you help me get a job?" is large and vague; "How does your team use Power BI for HR reports?" is small.
- An advice chat is a conversation to learn how the work really looks. A referral (an employee submitting your profile for a named opening) is a separate, later ask.
- You are not hiding anything. You are simply asking one thing at a time.
- Your HR background is an advantage here: you already know how to talk to people and keep conversations professional.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on reply rates for advice requests, or on whether advice chats lead to referrals for switchers (A12b sources). The template shape (name the reason, one specific ask, keep it short) comes from vendor and blog advice, unverified.
**What to do:**
1. Pick someone you have a real link with: ex-colleague, alumnus, vendor, recruiter you worked with.
2. Write 4-5 lines: who you are, why you chose them, two specific questions, the 15-minute offer.
3. Let them pick the time and the channel (call, chat, coffee).
4. Prepare the two questions plus a 30-second summary of your switch.
5. Send a thank-you the same day. See Q80.
**Example:** (illustrative message, replace names and facts with your own true ones)
"Hi Meera, I worked in HR at [company] and I am moving into reporting and analytics. I saw your team builds workforce dashboards. Two questions: what do you use daily, Excel, SQL or Power BI, and what would you test in a junior candidate? Would you have 15 minutes sometime in the next two weeks? Happy to work around your schedule."
**Common mistakes:** Attaching a resume in the first message. Asking "do you have any openings?" in the same note. Writing a long life story. Not saying why you chose that person.

### Q74 ⭐⭐ 🟡 How do consultancies and staffing firms work, and should I register with them?
**Short answer:** A consultancy (or staffing firm) is an agency that finds candidates for client companies. Registering with a genuine one that charges you nothing is fine as one channel among several, but do not rely on it alone.
**Why / explain simply:**
- In the usual model the hiring company pays the agency, not you (vendor-stated, unverified).
- Two common set-ups: you join the client's payroll (permanent), or you join the agency's own payroll and work at the client on a contract. They differ a lot in notice, benefits and security. See Q84.
- The agency's recruiter is paid to fill roles, so they may push you towards roles that are easy to fill rather than your best fit.
- A genuine agency never needs money from you. See Q75.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on which consultancies in Hyderabad or Bengaluru place career switchers into analyst roles, or on what share of analyst hires come through consultancies (A12b sources). The employer-pays description is from a vendor with a commercial interest: unverified.
**What to do:**
1. Register only with firms you can verify: working website, office address, company registration details, and the client roles visible on the hiring company's own careers page.
2. Ask in the first call: who is my employer, what is the contract length, what is the notice period, is there conversion to permanent, is background verification required.
3. Track every consultancy submission in your tracker (Section 6) so two agencies do not submit you to the same company.
4. Ask the agency to tell you the company name before they send your resume anywhere.
**Example:** (illustrative) A recruiter from an unknown firm says "we have a reporting role at a bank." You ask for the company name, the pay band, and whether the offer is on the firm's payroll or the bank's. If they will not say, you pause.
**Common mistakes:** Paying a "registration" fee. Letting two agencies submit the same resume to one company. Accepting a role without learning who the employer is.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 4 (consultancies); Q75, Q84.

### Q82 ⭐ 🟡 How many outreach messages a week is reasonable, and when does outreach become spam?
**Short answer:** Three personalised messages a week is a sustainable starting point. It becomes spam when messages are copy-pasted, repeated, or sent to people who have not replied.
**Why / explain simply:**
- The figure of 3 a week is a suggestion from Book 0 Q74, not research.
- Quality beats volume: each message should name the person, the role and one reason you fit.
- Spam is judged by the receiver: same text to many people, repeated follow-ups, or messages that ignore a previous "no".
- You are employed, so time is limited; consistency matters more than bursts.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on reply rates or the best volume of outreach. Follow-up advice of one nudge after about 5-7 days, then stop, is from vendor and blog sources (A12b sources), unverified. LinkedIn invitation-note limits and monthly quotas conflict between sources (200 or 300 characters; check the counter in the app).
**What to do:**
1. Start with 3 messages a week for 6 weeks; log them in your tracker.
2. Send one follow-up after about a week, then stop.
3. If you get no replies after about 10 messages, change the message or the target group, not the volume.
4. Never send the same message to many people in one company.
5. Respect silence and "no".
**Example:** (illustrative) Week 1: 3 messages to ex-colleagues. Week 2: 2 follow-ups plus 3 new messages to alumni. Tracker shows 2 replies out of 6, so you keep the format.
**Common mistakes:** Sending 30 messages in an evening. Using automation tools (terms-of-service risk, unverified). Chasing weekly. Changing nothing after weeks of silence.

For Tableau Desktop Specialist, the official page shows a US dollar fee and no expiry; it matters only if your JDs name Tableau (about 33% in the pooled sample, n=186, non-random, about +/-7 points) (MS Addendum). I quote no prices.
**What to do:**
1. Open the official Microsoft exam page and select India for the price. Open the Coursera page and check the price in rupees.
2. Screenshot the price with the date.
3. Fill a worksheet: Total cost = exam or subscription fee + (study hours x what an hour of your time is worth) + retake fee x chance you retake.
4. Compare with the same hours spent on a project.
5. Look for financial aid on Coursera if cost matters.

### Q90 ⭐⭐ 🟢 Where do I list certificates on my resume and LinkedIn, and how do I describe one that is still in progress?
**Short answer:** Put certificates in a short section below your projects and skills, with the full name, issuer and date earned. List one in progress only if you are truly doing it, and say so plainly.
**Why / explain simply:**
- Projects and skills carry more weight for a first analyst role, so certificates sit lower.
- Plain wording stops confusion: "Microsoft Certified: Power BI Data Analyst Associate (PL-300), [month year]".
- In progress means you are enrolled and studying. If you stopped, remove it.
- On LinkedIn there is a Licenses and certifications area. Exact field names may differ, so check the live page.
**What the evidence says:** No reliable data found on how employers treat in-progress certificates. No certification was named in any of the 40 in-scope JDs (MS Section 5). This guidance is our own, plus honesty rules.
**What to do:**
1. Resume: one line per certificate under "Certifications", after projects and skills.
2. In progress: "Google Data Analytics Professional Certificate (in progress, expected [month year])". Use an expected date only if you can truly meet it.
3. LinkedIn: add earned ones to Licenses and certifications; mention one in progress in your About or a post, not as an earned credential.
4. Never list a certificate you have not earned or one you only started to read about.
5. Keep dates and names identical on resume, LinkedIn and Naukri.
**Example:** (illustrative)
Certifications
- Microsoft Certified: Power BI Data Analyst Associate (PL-300), [month year]
- Google Data Analytics Professional Certificate (in progress, 5 of 9 courses completed, expected [month year])
**Common mistakes:** Putting certificates at the top above projects. Listing an unearned certificate. Writing "in progress" for months with no progress. Mismatched dates across profiles.
**Where this is covered:** Guide Section 5 and Section 1 (resume); Guide Section 3 (profiles); Q85, Q87.
